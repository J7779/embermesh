# African buffalo (Cape buffalo)

_Encyclopedia article (Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown and excerpted: references, images, infoboxes, navigation boxes and non-practical sections were removed._

The **African buffalo** (***Syncerus caffer***) is a large bovine native to sub-Saharan Africa. First described in 1779 by Anders Sparrman, it is the only extant species of the genus *Syncerus*. Four subspecies are currently recognized: the **Cape buffalo**, **West African savanna buffalo**, **Central African savanna buffalo**, and African forest buffalo. It is a heavily built animal with a broad head and torso, a strong neck, large horns, and a bony armor or "boss" across the forehead of males. The fur ranges in color from rusty to black, and the tail ends with a dark, tuft of hair.

The African buffalo occurs in a variety of habitats, such as woodlands, savannas, forests, grasslands, shrublands, and wetlands. It is a ruminant and a grazer, primarily feeding on grass and dietary fiber. A social animal, the African buffalo forms herds ranging in size from a few individuals to over a thousand members. Herds typically consist of adult females and their young, and a number of adult males that temporarily join herds for mating opportunities. Males have a pecking order where dominant individuals hold the best access to mates. After a gestation of more than 10 months, females give birth to a single calf. Weaning occurs between 10 and 18 months, and the lifespan is about 12 years in the wild. Adult African buffaloes have few natural predators because of their size and aggression, though calves are much more vulnerable.

Since the 19th century, the African buffalo's range has greatly reduced due to human factors such as poaching, climate change, agriculture, and disease outbreaks. Despite this trend, conservation efforts have managed to increase populations in some parts of its range. As of 2019, it is listed as Near Threatened on the IUCN Red List, with an estimated population of 398,000 to 401,000 mature individuals. A Big Five game animal, the African buffalo is legally hunted as a trophy in 16 out of 37 range countries. It has co-existed alongside humans for thousands of years and has in many ways contributed to human culture.

## Distribution and habitat

Historically, the African buffalo ranged throughout most of sub-Saharan Africa. Since the 19th century, the species lost a major chunk of its range due to poaching, climate change, agriculture, and disease outbreaks. Protected and managed hunting areas host the majority of savanna buffalo populations. The Cape buffalo occurs across East Africa in isolated pockets from southern Ethiopia and southern Somalia, to as far south as South Africa and as far west as Angola. Both the West and Central African buffaloes are found within the Sudano-Sahelian zone, with the former ranging from Senegal in the west to Cameroon in the east, whereas the latter is distributed north to Chad, south to the Democratic Republic of Congo, and east to Ethiopia. In West and Central Africa, there are two separate populations of forest buffalo.

The African buffalo can be found in a variety of habitats, such as woodlands, savannas, forests, grasslands, shrublands, and wetlands. A requirement the species has for survival in partially dry areas is that a permanent water source should not be beyond 20–40 km. Normally, it prefers habitats that receive over 250 mm of rain each year and that are home to herbaceous plants. In East and Southern Africa, buffalo populations prefer woodlands, while those in West and Central Africa are found in various mixed woodland-grassland habitats. The forest buffalo lives in forest-savanna mosaics and rainforests.

## Description

The African buffalo is among the most polymorphic of African mammals, as populations vary considerably in size, color, and horn shape. The largest subspecies, the Cape buffalo, has a head-to-body length of 210 to 300 cm and a shoulder height of 140 to 160 cm. Males weigh 650 to 900 kg, whereas females reach a maximum of 500 kg. At 100 to 130 cm tall at the shoulder, 170 to 220 cm in body length and 265 to 320 kg in weight, the forest buffalo is the smallest subspecies.

A large bovine, the African buffalo is characterized by its heavyset body with a large head and torso, thick, muscular neck, stubby legs, and curved hooves that grow larger in the forelegs than the hindlegs. The sagged, often marred ears are outlined with long hairs, and a wet nose lies on a broad snout with big eyes. The African buffalo is rusty to black in color and has a dark, tuft of hair at the end of a 55 to 120 cm long tail. Before obtaining the adult coat, young Cape buffaloes change in color from tawny to dark brown, and gradually reduce their coat density. Adult forest buffaloes typically have a rusty to black coat pattern, whereas the denser coat of young individuals is more reddish, sometimes with black markings.

Both sexes have large horns, which in males form a boss (a continuous bony armor) when bone fuses between the base of the horns. The boss is most pronounced in male Cape buffaloes. Females in savanna populations have smaller horns than those of males and instead of a boss, females have hairy skin between their horns. Compared to the Cape buffalo, West and Central African buffaloes possess similar laterally curved horns, but without the downward arch. Conversely, the horns of forest buffaloes point horizontally backwards, lack a boss and are smaller than those of savanna buffaloes.

## Behavior and ecology

### Social behavior

Adult females form the core of African buffalo herds. The number of individuals in herds is variable and differs across regions; for West and Central African buffaloes, herds of females and their young have an average of 45 and a maximum of 150 individuals, whereas in Southern and East African floodplains, Cape buffaloes are known to form large herds of up to 2,000 members. About 3–25 animals make up a forest buffalo herd.

Female savanna buffaloes remain with the herd for life and may continue bonding with their mother even after the age of weaning. On the other hand, young males grow increasingly detached from their mothers and separate from the herd to join bachelor groups at age four to five for a limited period. They later join a herd to compete for mating opportunities. As males lose their strength, they again leave for bachelor groups so as to recover by excessively grazing in areas inaccessible to herds. The permanent departure of males aged ten or older from mixed herds appears to happen randomly. In the forest buffalo, males make up the minority of the stable herd and travel with the group throughout the year.

Savanna buffaloes form durable groups that have been known to last over 50 years. These groups generally show a strong preference for their home ranges. In mixed herds, fission-fusion behaviors exist, where members briefly separate and form subgroups, then unify once more. Separation can occur during both the wet and dry seasons. Sometimes, separation may be due to large herd size. A few bulls and some females lead the herd when foraging. Decisions on where to eat or drink are not made by these individuals but rather through a process called 'voting.' After a lengthy resting session, right before dusk, adult females will stand up, raise their heads, and point in a particular direction, then again rest. If multiple voting females point in the same direction, consensus is strong. However, in cases of weak consensus, the herd may separate and rejoin days later.

Most forest buffalo sightings are of groups, though solitary individuals of both sexes have been observed. Solitary male sightings are more frequent than sightings of solitary females. In Dzanga-Ndoki National Park between 2007 and 2008, short-lived fission-fusion dynamics and a correlation between an increase in herd size and fission-fusion dynamics were recorded in a specific herd. Another finding was that during the wet season members of the herd traveled farther away from each other in smaller groups.
