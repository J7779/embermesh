# Cookbook: Lentil

_Wikibooks textbook pages (Wikibooks, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown: navigation templates, images, references and link lists were removed._

__notoc__

The **lentil** is a variety of pulse. In some cultures, the term "lentil" may refer to a broader class of pulses—when in doubt, double-check what ingredient is correct.

## Characteristics

The defining feature of the lentil is its small, round, flattened shape (like the lens, which is named after it). The color depends on the variety, ranging in color from brown to black, green, yellow, and red. Size and texture also vary across the different types, but they are all quite small. They may be sold whole or split. Generally, lentils are relatively high in protein and fiber.

### Varieties

A number of lentil varieties can be found. Generally, they can be divided up into larger, flatter, mushier varieties versus smaller, firmer, rounder varieties. The table below describes some common varieties.

| Variety | Image | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Red; (Masoor) | | Skin often brown with inside orange to red; often sold split as dal; quick-cooking and soft |
| Brown | | Greenish-brown in color; large and flat; softer texture once cooked |
| Green (Laird) | | Very similar to brown lentils; mild flavor; soften and break down with cooking |
| French green (Puy) | | Dark brownish-green color and sometimes faintly speckled; small and more rounded; keep their shape and bite well |
| Beluga | | Small, shiny, and black; firm and hold their shape; creamy texture and solid flavor |

## Selection and storage

Dried lentils are the most commonly available form. Store these at room temperature away from light, heat, and moisture. Cooked lentils will keep on the order of a week in the fridge and months in the freezer.

## Preparation

Before using lentils, you'll want to spread them out on a flat surface and pick out any stones or other pieces of debris. Unlike other, larger pulses, lentils do not require soaking. However, if you're using whole lentils and want to reduce the risk of them bursting, it can be beneficial to brine them. To do so, make a brine of 1 tsp salt per quart of water, and soak the lentils for 1–24 hours before draining.

## Use

The absolute simplest way to cook lentils is simply by simmering in water or another flavorful liquid, often with aromatics added. The amount of liquid needed varies, but three volumes of liquid per volume of lentils is a good place to start. Their small size means they cook fairly rapidly—start checking them at the 20-minute mark. Firmer lentils do well in salads, while softer lentils are great for dal and soups, where the starch thickens the liquid. Lentils can also be fried for a crunchy texture or ground into flour and turned into breads or other preparations.

## Substitution

Brown lentils and green Laird lentils can be substituted for each other and may even be confused with each other.
