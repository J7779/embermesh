# Kvass

_Encyclopedia article (Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown and excerpted: references, images, infoboxes, navigation boxes and non-practical sections were removed._

**Kvass** is a fermented, cereal-based, low-alcohol beverage of cloudy appearance and a sweet and sour taste.

Kvass originates from northeastern Europe, where grain production was considered insufficient for beer to become a daily drink. The first written mention of kvass is found in *Primary Chronicle*, describing the celebration of Vladimir the Great's baptism in 988.

Traditionally, kvass is made by soaking a mash of rye bread or rye flour and malt in hot water, then fermenting it for about 12 hours at room temperature with sugar and bread yeast or baker's yeast. In industrial methods, producers make kvass from wort concentrate combined with various grain mixtures. It is a drink known in Belarus, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Moldova, Armenia, Georgia, Poland, Russia, and Ukraine. Kvass (or similar beverages) are also popular in parts of China, Finland, Kazakhstan, and Uzbekistan.

## Nutritional composition

Naturally fermented kvass contains 5.9%±0.02 carbohydrates, of which 5.7%±0.02 are sugars (mostly fructose, glucose, and maltose), as well as 0.71±0.09, 1.28±0.12, and 18.14±0.48 mg/100 g of thiamine, riboflavin, and niacin respectively. In addition to that, 19 different aroma volatile compounds have also been identified in naturally fermented kvass, most notably 4-penten-2-ol (10.05×10⁷ PAU), which has a fruity odour; carvone (2.28×10⁷ PAU) originating from caraway fruits used as an ingredient in rye bread; and ethyl octanoate (1.03×10⁷ PAU), which has an odour of fruit and fat.

Traditional kvass made from rye wholemeal bread has been found to have, on average, twice the dietary fiber content, 60% more antioxidant activity (due to the addition of caramel and citric acid to the bread), and three times less reducing sugar content than industrially produced kvass.

Historically, alcohol by volume (ABV) in kvass varied with ingredients, microbial flora, temperature, and fermentation time, but today it is usually no higher than 1.5%. The wide availability and consumption of kvass, including by children of all ages, together with the lacking indication of ABV for kvass on the labels and in advertisements, has been named a possible contributor to chronic alcoholism in the former Soviet Union.

## Use

Beyond drinking, families also use kvass as the base for many dishes. Traditional cold summertime soups of Russian cuisine, such as okroshka, botvinya, and tyurya, are based on kvass.

## Non-bread varieties

A traditional Ukrainian drink was pear and apple kvas, made by soaking dried fruit in water for two months. This drink was popular at banquets and also kept people from drinking low-quality water. Another popular traditional specialty of Ukraine is beetroot kvas, which could be consumed as a drink or used as an ingredient of other dishes, most famously borshch. Some types of non-bread kvass have been produced to be sold in the United States including beet and ginger and sauerkraut varieties.
