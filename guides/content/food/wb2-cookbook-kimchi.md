# Cookbook: Kimchi

_Wikibooks textbook pages (Wikibooks, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown: navigation templates, images, references and link lists were removed._

**Kimchi** is a class of Korean fermented or pickled vegetables.

## Characteristics

Because kimchi is such a large group containing various types, with some sources stating the existence of hundreds of varieties, there is no single canonical way to describe it. Typically, kimchi consists of salted fruits and vegetables, often but not always seasoned with gochugaru (for spice) and fermented seafood products like salted shrimp or fish sauce (for umami flavor). Kimchi is also sometimes fermented to allow the mixture to pickle and develop additional flavour, though some fresh, unfermented kimchis do exist. Some varieties of kimchi are considered seasonal due to tradition and the availability of ingredients; summer kimchis are typically fresher and lighter than cold-weather kimchi, which often ferments for a while and must last through multiple months.

## Production

Kimchi production can be done on an industrial, community, or individual scale. Depending on the kimchi variety, some or all of the following steps take place.

The vegetables may first be pre-salted or pre-brined to change their texture and make them more receptive to seasoning. The seasoning mixture and/or other ingredients are then applied before packing into containers for fermentation. Additional brine/pickling liquid may be added at this point. Fermentation then proceeds according to the type of kimchi.

The fermentation that takes place is the same as that for other salted pickled vegetables (i.e. lactic acid bacterial). By putting the produce in a salty, air-free environment (the pickling liquid), the growth of some "bad" microbes is inhibited and the correct environment for "good" lactic acid bacteria is created. These bacteria consume carbohydrates and, crucially, produce acid that pickles the vegetables and further inhibits the growth of many spoilage microbes. Usually, the fermentation is done below 68°F (20°C), which can be achieved at cool room temperature, in the fridge, or in a container buried outside in cold weather. The higher the temperature, the faster the fermentation. The precise fermentation duration ranges from hours to over a year, and it depends on various factors, such as the type of kimchi, specific ingredients, salt level, carbohydrate level, temperature, and more.

## Selection and storage

When purchasing kimchi from commercial producers, the quality ranges from brand to brand. General recommendations support kimchi from Korean grocery stores.

The fermented varieties of kimchi generally keep for a long time when kept cool (i.e. in the refrigerator or buried in a cold climate). In fact, some argue that these will keep "indefinitely". While there is some degree of truth to this, note that the kimchi will continue to ferment over time, so it is not fully stable. This continued fermentation typically means that the kimchi will become more sour and stronger in flavor over time. Notably, kimchi can still develop mold, especially when portions are exposed to the air; for this reason, it is recommended that the kimchi be kept fully submerged in its juices and that the container be covered well.

Specialty kimchi refrigerators exist, and these have cooled walls for more consistent temperature and fermentation.

## Use

Kimchi features very prominently in the cuisine of Korea, being served as a banchan or as an ingredient in other dishes. Examples of the latter include kimchi bokkeum bap (fried rice), kimchi jjigae (stew), kimchi jeon (pancake), noodles. The sourness of fermented kimchis pairs well with fatty and savory ingredients like heavy meats. Fermented kimchi that is too strong to eat on its own can still be used as a component ingredient in dishes that will even out the flavor, like stews and fried starchy dishes.

## Techniques

### Baechu kimchi

First, the cabbage must be quartered and salted/brined for several hours, which makes the leaves more pliable. This must be done until the majority of the leaves bend near the root without snapping, but over-brining will eliminate the often-desirable residual crunch of the kimchi. The duration varies according to season, salt level, and cabbage type, and a range of 4–10 hours is typical. After this point, the cabbage should be rinsed and drained well to remove excess salt and liquid—after rinsing, the cabbage should still be flavored with salt throughout the leaves.

Once the cabbage is prepared, the seasoning mixture is made with rice flour paste and ground chiles, plus additional ingredients like sugar, fish sauce, salted shrimp, kelp broth, ginger, garlic, onion, Korean radish, scallions, apple, and/or Asian pear as called for by the recipe. This mixture is then applied between every layer of leaves on the cabbages. Some individuals use gloves for this process to protect the hands from the red stain.

Once seasoned, the cabbage is packed into containers for fermenting. Weighing down the cabbage helps squeeze out liquid and ensures everything is fully submerged. Airtight containers are a good idea to keep the odor inside and keep other microbes out. Ferment according to your recipe.
