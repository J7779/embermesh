# Confit

_Encyclopedia article (Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown and excerpted: references, images, infoboxes, navigation boxes and non-practical sections were removed._

**Confit** (confisat) (from French 'to preserve') is a food that is cooked in fat as a method of preservation.

A confit is cooked in fat at a low temperature, typically 200 °F or less.

For meat, this method requires the meat to be salted. After salting and cooking in fat, confit can last for several months or years when sealed and stored in a cool, dark place. Confit is a specialty of southwestern France.

## Fruit confit

Fruit confit is candied fruit (whole fruit, or pieces thereof) preserved in sugar. The fruit must be fully infused with sugar to its core; larger fruit takes considerably longer than smaller ones to candy. Thus, while small fruit such as cherries are*confits*whole, it is quite rare to see whole large fruit, such as melon*confits*since the time and energy involved in producing large*fruit confits*makes them quite expensive.

## Meat confit

Confit of goose (*confit d'oie*) or duck (*confit de canard*) is usually prepared from the legs of the bird. The meat is salted, seasoned with herbs, and slowly cooked submerged in its own rendered fat at 85 °C or less. The meat is put in containers with its fat. Turkey and pork may be treated in the same manner. Meat confit is a specialty of the southwest of France (Toulouse, Dordogne, and other areas) and is used in dishes such as cassoulet. Confit preparations originated as a means of preserving meat before refrigeration and canning.

In restaurants, confit is usually served after further preparation. Whole confit leg is baked to crisp the skin or added to a casserole-type dish. Confit duck leg is used to make rillettes.

## Condiment confit

Italian cooking uses several "condiment confits", such as onion confit, chili confit, and garlic confit.
