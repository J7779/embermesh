# Tortilla

_Encyclopedia article (Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown and excerpted: references, images, infoboxes, navigation boxes and non-practical sections were removed._

A **tortilla** is a thin, circular unleavened flatbread, originally made from corn (maize) but now also made of wheat flour. The corn tortilla is a staple food in most of Mexico and Central America, while the flour tortilla is typical of Northern Mexican and Mexican-American cuisine. The tortilla has become popular across the world.

Corn tortillas are made by treating corn with limewater, a process known as nixtamalization, forming a dough known as masa, which is baked on a griddle. Flour tortillas are made of refined wheat flour, water, and shortening, and are generally larger than corn tortillas. Both types are commonly industrially produced, with corn tortillas usually using a dehydrated form of masa called masa harina. Tortillas are soft but quickly become stale. They are high in calories but lack essential nutrients.

Fresh tortillas can be used to carry various ingredients, forming dishes such as the taco, burrito, enchilada, and quesadilla. They may instead be used as a spoon to pick up accompanying food. Other uses of tortillas include dishes such as tortilla soup and fried products such as tostadas and tortilla chips.

Tortillas were first made by ancient peoples of Mesoamerica, where corn was an important crop. Corn tortillas became a cornerstone of Mesoamerican cuisine as complex societies formed, including the Oaxaca Valley and the Aztec civilization. Tortillas also spread to the Maya civilization. After the 16th-century Spanish conquest of Mexico, corn tortillas remained popular among indigenous communities. Flour tortillas were invented after the Spanish introduced wheat to Mexico, becoming common among settlers of Northern Mexico. The flour tortilla remained popular in parts of Mexico that became part of the United States, including Texas and California. Mass-produced tortillas became common in the 1950s, using tortilla machines and masa harina. Tortillas became common globally in the early 21st century.

The tortilla is the most consumed food in Mexican and Central American cuisine, as well as a national symbol of Mexico. Rural indigenous communities of Mexico frequently prepare corn tortillas at home, while the urban population purchases tortillas from bakeries known as tortillerias or from supermarkets. Tortillas are also common in the United States due to Hispanic influence on American cuisine. Mexican food establishments and manufacturers such as Gruma have made tortillas a global industry.

## Serving

Tortillas may be eaten for any meal. Corn and flour tortillas have similar uses. They are accompanied by foods such as meat, beans, vegetables, or eggs. A tortilla can function as a serving plate or as a utensil, tearing off small pieces and wrapping them around accompanying foods to eat by hand.

Many dishes use tortillas to carry other foods. Common fillings in tortilla-based dishes include refried beans, pulled meat, chorizo, scrambled eggs, or fish. Additionally, they may have salsa (a spicy sauce) and ingredients such as onion slices, lettuce, or guacamole, as well as herbs such as cilantro.

The most common use of fresh tortillas is the taco, which consists of a tortilla folded around a filling. The filling may simply be meat or vegetables, while a common type in Mexico combines meat, beans, and sauce. A burrito consists of a large flour tortilla rolled around a filling. An enchilada is also rolled, but the tortilla is covered in tomato sauce and fried; this often uses a corn tortilla that is dyed red. A quesadilla is a snack in which a tortilla is folded in half over a filling of cheese and other ingredients, then fried. A flauta is prepared like a taco, rolled, and then fried. For a panucho, the fillings are inserted inside the tortilla. Tortillas are also used for a category of sandwiches in American cuisine known as wraps.

Tortillas may instead be made into fried products, with the addition of salt or additional seasonings. This is a way to utilize tortillas that are partly stale. Fried corn tortillas are known as tostadas; these are served with toppings. Taco shells, in American cuisine, are tostadas that are bent rather than flat. These are used for the American style of taco, typically with ground beef, cheese, and lettuce. Tortilla chips are corn tortillas baked as triangles, circles, or strips, which are then fried and salted. Tortilla chips may be topped with cheese and jalapeños, forming nachos, or they may be coated in flavorings, to be sold as packaged snack products. Totopos are another fried corn tortilla product. Flour tortillas are fried into foods including taco bowls. A salbute is a fried tortilla made of both masa and wheat flour.

Stale tortillas may also be sliced and mixed into various dishes. For tortilla soup, they are sliced and fried to be used as a crouton. For other dishes, they are cut into strips like noodles. For example, chilaquiles are strips of tortilla covered in tomato sauce.

## Nutrition and properties

Both corn and flour tortillas are starchy, making them an excellent source of calories. Flour tortillas are higher in calories as they contain more fat. Both types are sources of fiber, with content between 5.4% and 16.45% in corn tortillas, and about 1.8% in flour tortillas. Their water content ranges is about 42–46%. Both types contain minor nutrients such as folic acid, policosanols, and tocopherols, as well as phytochemicals such as ferulic acid. In corn tortillas, the use of limewater significantly increases the content of calcium and magnesium and the digestibility of niacin, while removing toxins such as aflatoxins. They are the largest source of calories, protein, and calcium in Mexico and Central America.

Tortillas are not complete sources of protein as they lack two essential amino acids, lysine and tryptophan. They also lack vitamins A, B₁₂, D, and E as well as minerals including iron and zinc. Thus, they are often fortified with protein-rich ingredients such as soybeans or with vitamins and minerals. Corn tortillas are harder to modify as added ingredients strongly influence masa. Whole-grain versions of both types are produced to meet the standards of nutrition programs.

Pliability is an important quality of tortillas as they must be folded for dishes such as tacos. Other qualities desired by consumers include softness, structural integrity, a uniform shape, and a toasted appearance. Corn tortillas are soft while fresh but rapidly lose this texture. Flour tortillas are also soft and pliable and have a longer shelf life. Tortillas become hard and brittle once they are stale, though these may still be eaten, sometimes intentionally. Mold growth, rather than staling, is the main factor that decreases the shelf life of tortillas. Both types may grow mold after two days of storage. Other factors that decrease shelf life include basic pH, high storage temperature, and poor packaging. They may use additives such as preservatives such as propionates or sorbates to avoid mold growth, while additives such as proteins, hydrocolloids, emulsifiers, and enzymes can limit staling. In a freezer, tortillas can last 6–12 months. For tortillas to be sold with long shelf lives, they are commonly preserved using vacuum packing, sometimes in combination with modified atmosphere packaging.

## Consumption

Tortillas are popular as they use commonly available ingredients. About 1.4 billion tortillas are eaten every day. The market size for tortillas, as of 2012, is about $12 billion, with another $10 billion for related products including tacos and tortilla chips. The world's production of tortillas is 42% in Mexico, 36% in the United States, and 9% in Central America, as of 2004. The average annual consumption of tortillas, as of 2001, is 85 kg in Mexico—or as high as 120 kg in some regions of the country—as well as 6 kg in the United States and 0.3 kg in Europe. The largest tortilla company is Gruma, with global sales of $4.2 billion in 2011 (equivalent to $ billion in ).

Many people eat tortillas for every meal. Some prepare them fresh at home, which requires hours of labor every day. Others purchase tortillas for everyday consumption, reserving homemade tortillas for special occasions. Automated machines are used by tortillerias, small businesses dedicated to tortilla-making, as well as supermarkets, which sell tortillas at lower prices. Tortilla mislabeling occurs when tortilla products are incorrectly labeled in their ingredients or dietary claims. Some small-scale tortillerias sell handmade, but these are much more expensive than those using machines. Many chefs create unique variations of tortillas; for example, the Mexico City restaurant Pujol serves a corn tortilla filled with eggs and beans, Los Angeles-based chef John Rivera Sedlar presses flower petals into corn tortillas, and Tucson-based chef Janos Wilder mixes local native ingredients into flour tortillas. Tortilla chips and tostadas are sold as snack foods or used in fast food dishes.

Economically, tortillas are an inferior good as they are more common among consumers with lower incomes. They can be a commodity, as they provide interchangeable culinary and nutritional value. Because they are a staple food, demand does not decrease during crises. Some households rely on tortillas even when a crisis increases the price; thus, they can be considered a Giffen good, for which demand increases as price increases. Price elasticity of tortillas in Mexico is very high, as exemplified by the Tortilla Crisis of 2006–2007. The price of tortillas increases faster than the price of corn. The production of masa harina is concentrated by a few firms, forming an oligopoly that influences the price of tortillas. The Mexican public discusses the price of tortillas as an indicator of the state of the economy.

### Mexico and Central America

In Mexican and Central American cuisine, 98% of tortillas are made of corn. Tortillas are a staple food in most of this region, with corn tortillas being the region's most consumed type of food.

Tortillas are considered the national bread of Mexico. Tortillas are more common among Mexico's rural population, with the average daily consumption per person being 56.7 kg in urban areas and 79.5 kg in rural areas. Tortilla production is Mexico's fourth-largest industry, comprising 0.9% of its GDP and 40% of its corn use, as of 2004. Annually, about 9 million tons of soft corn tortillas are produced in the country, of which 41% use traditional masa, 37% use fresh masa, and 23% use masa harina, as of 2016.

As of 2022, 80% of the country's tortillas are from tortillerias, 10% are from supermarkets, and 10% are homemade. Making tortillas from scratch is typical for the rural indigenous population, although this is declining in the modern day. In Maya villages, tortillas are typically made by women, while men partake in the harvesting of maize on milpa farming. Though this has decreased in the modern day, some communities consider knowledge of tortilla making an essential skill for a woman to be ready for married life. The urban population, including indigenous people, purchases machine-made tortillas. Tortillerias are typical of urban areas across the country. They face competition from white bread.
