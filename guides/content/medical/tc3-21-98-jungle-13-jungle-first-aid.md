# Jungle First Aid, Hygiene and Tropical Health

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 13. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

The ability to provide effective first aid is critical for preserving life and minimizing suffering in both combat and non-combat situations, particularly when immediate medical attention is unavailable. This chapter details the essential principles and practices of tactical first aid, with a specific focus on the unique health challenges presented by tropical environments. This chapter explores maintaining health and hygiene in challenging climates, techniques for casualty recovery and evacuation, and the core first aid procedures necessary to address a wide range of injuries and illnesses. Proficiency in these skills is paramount for self-aid, buddy-aid, and ultimately, mission success.

## Section I – Health and Hygiene in Tropics

13-1. Health and hygiene in a tropical environment are important and failure to place importance on this can result in mission failure.

## Personal Protective Measures

13-2. Personal hygiene includes:

- Recognize that small infections quickly worsen in the jungle. Treat injuries as soon as possible given the tactical situation.
- Frequently wash hands.
- Do not rub eyes or the inside of the nose with untreated fingers or gloves.
- Bathe whenever possible; use field expedient showers.
- Do not shave, as cuts from shaving can become easily infected.
- Change socks and/or boots every 8 hours or whenever wet (use wet kit/dry kit procedures).
- Lightly apply foot powder when changing socks or boots.

13-3. Protective measures:

- Wearing long sleeves, insect repellent, and mosquito nets.
- Avoiding stagnant water and contaminated food.
- DOD insect repellant system:
  - Permethrin treated uniform.
  - Permethrin treated bed net.
  - DEET applied to skin.
  - Properly worn uniform.
  - Anti-malarial medicine.
- Consideration for selected uniform:
  - Gloves should only be worn when necessary for protection. Gloves retain heat and moisture, increasing one’s hands susceptibility to infection if not periodically aired out.
  - Headgear is worn to protect from sunlight. It also traps heat and moisture; take off headgear when appropriate to mitigate heat stress.
  - Pants should be always tucked into boots to protect against insects and harmful vegetation. Pants may be unbloused for heat mitigation when appropriate.
  - Bed nets treated with permethrin can ward off insects during sleep.

13-4. Hydration and nutrition:

- Purifying water using tablets, filters, or boiling.
- Maintaining electrolyte balance with oral rehydration salts.

13-5. Always ensure that oneself and every member of the team bring a first aid kit when operating in a jungle environment. These kits will help treat wounds and prevent infections. Even small injuries in the jungle can lead to serious problems, which can severely limit survival capabilities.

- Trauma and medical kits consist of a complete, military-issued Individual Field Aid Kit or Jungle Field Aid Kit.
- Personal aid kits. This kit should be individualized to one’s needs:
  - Plasters/bandages.
  - Butterfly closure strips.
  - Hydrocortisone.
  - Anti-fungal cream.
  - Antiseptic spray/wipes.
  - Analgesic heat rub.
  - Petroleum jelly.
  - Iodine.

## Hot Weather Injuries

13-6. In jungle environments, heat and hot weather injuries are a constant concern. Heat-related injuries are cumulative; use the following acronym (HEAT) when considering the effects of heat on the unit.

- H–Heat category for the past 3 days.
- E–Exertion level in the past 3 days.
- A–Acclimation/Individual risk factors.
- T**–**Temperature/Overnight rest.

13-7. Everyone will have different risk factors. The following factors may exacerbate heat stress:

- Poor fitness.
- Large body mass.
- High levels of motivation.
- Minor illnesses (for example, allergies or the common cold inhibiting the ability to sweat).
- Medications (for example, antidepressants inhibiting the ability to sweat and increasing sensitivity to sunlight).
- Diuretics (for example, caffeine) in the bloodstream.
- Skin conditions (for example, rashes, sunburns, reactions to poisonous flora).
- Workout supplements (for example, pre-workout and/or thermogenic compounds).
- Prior hot/cold weather injuries.
- Age (over 40).

13-8. Humidity accelerates the effects that heat exerts on the body. Humidity can cause more futile sweat, depleting the body of vital electrolytes and fluids. It also prevents sweat from evaporating (as the air is already saturated with water) and reduces effectiveness of cooling measures. Heat-related deaths have occurred when temperatures were less than 75°F but relative humidity was greater than 95 percent.

## Cold Weather Injuries

13-9. It is still possible to suffer cold weather injuries in jungle environments. Intense changes in temperature may occur due to humidity and high elevation, increasing the bodies’ susceptibility to cold weather injuries; the ambient temperature does not need to be below freezing to cause such injuries. The most common cold injuries are hypothermia and chilblains.

## Injuries and Diseases

13-10. Leaders within the formation must understand the risks of specific injuries and diseases to ensure the proper health on the formation.

### Trench Foot

13-11. Causes: Prolonged exposure of the limb or body part to damp, unsanitary, or cold conditions.

13-12. Signs and symptoms include:

- Sensations of pins and needles, where the tingling leads from numbness to pain.
- Skin discoloration (red to blue to black, where black indicates necrosis).
- Cold, swollen, and waxy appearance of the limb or body part.
- Development of blisters, open sores, or bleeding.

**Note.** A particular concern with this symptom is the susceptibility of the injury to fungal infections.

13-13. When untreated, the limb or body part develops gangrene and will require amputation.

13-14. Treatment:

- Prevention is the best treatment.
- Dry oneself as often as possible, keeping the feet dry and changing socks whenever possible.
- Use the dry kit/wet kit technique between rest cycles to air out the body.
- Wear lightweight shoes during rest cycles to help dry feet and wet boots.
- Use foot powder and other preventative measures as appropriate.

### Fauna-Related Injuries

13-15. Insects (and other arthropods) present a major concern while operating in jungle environments. A Soldier is fighting more than just the enemy; a good rule of thumb is to assume that 50 percent of everything in the jungle is trying to kill and eat oneself. To ward off insects, it is best to use preventative measures such as proper field sanitation procedures and the DOD-approved insect repellant systems.

13-16. Common stinging or biting arthropods in Pacific AO—

- Spiders.
- Mosquitoes.
- Scorpions.
- Bees.
- Wasps.
- Ants.

13-17. Avoidance measures include the following:

- Avoid sleeping directly on the ground.
- Frequently shake out uniform, boots, and bedding.
- Avoid walking barefoot.
- Avoid putting fruity or sweet scents on oneself or equipment.
- Brush off arthropods found on the body or equipment. Some insects emit a toxic substance that may transfer onto the skin if crushed.

13-18. Symptoms of insect-related injuries (such as anaphylaxis) that may require emergency medical attention may include the following:

- Chest pains.
- Nausea.
- Severe sweating.
- Loss of breath.
- Serious swelling.
- Slurred speech.

13-19. Snakes are also a common threat in jungle environments. Although there are no snakes in Hawaii, they are common in almost every other jungle environment. Use the following considerations if faced with an injury from a snake bite:

- Lay down the casualty.
- Reassure and calm the casualty.
- Remove constricting items from the casualty, including jewelry, watches, rings, and shoes.
- Apply a pressure dressing to the afflicted area, wrapping from the heart downwards.

> **WARNING**
>
> Do not use a tourniquet while treating a snake bite. Using a tourniquet while treating a snake bite may lead to necrosis, venom bolus, and embolism.

- Immobilize the injured part of the casualty’s body.
- Immediately transport the casualty to a medical facility.

13-20. Bats are another common jungle animal and a vector for rabies, a deadly disease—

- Signs and symptoms of rabies include agitation, confusion, hallucinations, headaches, hypersalivation, hydrophobia, and paralysis (in rare cases).
- Any mammal can contract and carry rabies.
- The rabies virus enters another host through the saliva of the infected animal. It then travels from the spinal cord of the host into the peripheral nerves, eventually making its way into the brain stem. It then spreads throughout the entire nervous system. Rabies completes the viral cycle by infecting the salivary glands of the host animal.
- Once the rabies virus travels to the brain, it is almost always fatal.
- Rabies may be prevented through prophylactic vaccination.
- Post-exposure, the Rabies Post-exposure Prophylaxis treatment (a four-dose series of vaccines) by medical providers may prevent fatal progression of the virus.

13-21. Leeches are common in the jungle, particularly in and around waterways—

- Leeches excrete saliva that will dilate blood vessels, thin blood, and deaden the pain of their bite.
- As always, prevention is better than the cure; use DEET when appropriate.
- Pouring salt or burning off attached leeches are not recommended removal methods. If leeches have latched onto the body, seek treatment from trained medical personnel.
- Always wear boots that are serviceable and fit well; keep pants tucked into boots; and regularly check teammates and oneself for leeches.
- Recommended leech removal procedures: Find the head (generally near the thinner end), slide a flat object under the head to break suction, repeat the second step for its posterior end.

### Flora-Related Injuries

13-22. Injury mechanisms include the following:

- Thorns may puncture skin.
- Leaves, stems, and sap may cause rashes on contact.
- Consuming unidentified or toxic plants may cause illnesses.

13-23. Indicators of poisonous flora. These are general indicators that will help one avoid poisonous plants. Not all plants that display these indicators are poisonous. It is best to rely on trustworthy local experts to identify edible and toxic flora in the AO—

- Spiny fruit.
- Milky sap.
- Hairy leaves.
- Atypical fruit shapes.
- Oily parts.
- Shiny leaves.
- Avoided (for example, unconsumed) by local animals.

### Tropical Infectious Diseases

13-24. Malaria is a disease caused by a parasite that infects a certain type of mosquito—

- Symptoms: fever and flu-like illness; chills; headaches, muscle aches, and tiredness; possible nausea, vomiting, and/or diarrhea.
- Severe symptoms: kidney failure, seizures, mental confusion, and coma.
- Prevention: prior to traveling to an area with high malaria risk, speak with a healthcare provider to obtain preventative medication; use DOD-approved insect repellant systems; avoid areas with high mosquito activity; and when resting, use mosquito nets.
- Treatment: use of prescription drugs.

13-25. Dengue fever is a viral disease caused by any of the four related dengue viruses—

- Symptoms: aches and pains (eye pain typically behind the eyes and muscle, joint, or bone pain); nausea and/or vomiting; rashes.
- Prevention: any method to avoid mosquito bites as previously discussed.
- Treatment: there is no specific medicine that treats dengue fever. The afflicted person should rest as much as possible, take acetaminophen if appropriate to control fever and relieve pain, stay hydrated, and seek further attention from medical personnel.

13-26. Leptospirosis is a disease caused by the bacterium Leptospira present in contaminated water or soil. It is common in Hawaii. This disease typically spreads through the urine of infected animals—

- Symptoms: fever, headache, chills, bone or muscle aches, vomiting or nausea, jaundice (yellow skin or eyes), red eyes, stomach pain, diarrhea, and rash.

**Note.** People suffering from leptospirosis may display any or none of these symptoms. Symptoms generally manifest between 2 to 30 days after coming in contact with Leptospira bacterium and may last up to several weeks or even months without treatment. Some people experience two phases of the illness, where the second phase is more severe (causing possible organ failure) and occurs after appearing to recover from the relatively mild first phase.

- Prevention: practice proper field sanitation and hygiene techniques; avoid swimming in water that may be contaminated with animal urine; cover exposed cuts, scratches, or wounds with waterproof bandages.
- Treatment: Leptospirosis is treated with antibiotics; trained medical personnel should administer antibiotics as soon as possible if a casualty is suspected of suffering from leptospirosis.

13-27. Field expedient differential diagnosis between types of illnesses—

- Assume any fever is malaria until proven otherwise, especially if it is cyclical (peaking every 8, 12, 24, 48, 72 hours).
- Bloodshot eyes with fever are indicative of hemorrhagic fevers (Ebola, Crimean-Congo fever, four types of dengue).

> **WARNING**
>
> If a hemorrhagic fever is suspected, immediately don personal protective equipment and quarantine the casualty.

- Suspect dengue fever if the casualty presents with retro-orbital (behind the bloodshot eyes) pain.
- Suspect chikungunya virus if the casualty presents with joint pain and fever without bloodshot eyes.

## Section II – Casualty Recovery

13-28. There are many challenges that are involved in a jungle CASEVAC—

- Dense vegetation: thick undergrowth and towering trees make it difficult to move casualties and create clear paths for evacuation.
- Limited visibility: the jungle canopy often blocks sunlight, reducing visibility and complicating navigation.
- Terrain: steep slopes, muddy ground, and water obstacles (for example, rivers, swamps) hinder movement.
- Weather: frequent rain and high humidity can degrade equipment and increase the risk of injuries or infections.
- Hostile environment: the presence of enemy forces, dangerous wildlife, and poisonous plants adds to the complexity of evacuation.
- Communication: Radio signals may be obstructed by the dense canopy, making coordination with extraction teams more difficult.

13-29. When a casualty occurs in a jungle environment, the first step is to assess the situation by determining the nature and severity of the injury while identifying any immediate threats, such as enemy presence, wildlife, or environmental hazards. Once the situation is understood, the priority is to stabilize the casualty by performing life-saving measures, including controlling bleeding, managing the airway, and treating for shock. It is equally important to protect the casualty from further harm by moving them away from danger and shielding them from environmental factors like rain. Communication is critical during this phase; use radios, signal mirrors, or flares to contact the command post or extraction team. When communicating, provide a clear and concise report using the MIST format: describe the Mechanism of injury, the Injuries sustained, the Signs and symptoms observed, and the Treatment provided. This structured approach ensures that the casualty receives timely and effective care while enabling the extraction team to prepare appropriately.

## Requesting Medical Evacuation

13-30. Considerations for requesting medical evacuation include the following:

- The decision to request a medical evacuation and the level of evacuation precedence will be made by the senior medical personnel on the scene, or if medical personnel are unavailable, the senior ranking military officer based on the patient’s condition and the tactical situation.
- The same 9-line format is used for both air and ground medical evacuation requests. (Utilize GTA 08-01-004.)
- The request should be transmitted using secure communications for operational security. Use brevity codes whenever possible.

13-31. Difficulties include the following:

- The range of FM radio communications in the jungle may be significantly reduced due to the dense undergrowth, heavy rains, and hilly terrain. The radio transmission range can be extended by using additional radio relays and field expedient antennas.
- There may be few suitable LZs. Many LZs will only be large enough to support one or two helicopters at a time.
- LZs may have to be created by clearing brush, which takes time and manpower.
- Hoist operations may be required more frequently in the thick jungle vegetation where LZs are not available.

13-32. Jungle LZ requirements include the following:

- Minimum size of 30-meter x 30 meter (100 feet x 100 feet).
- Clear canopy and surface.
- LZ marking.
- Clear visibility.
- LZ brief.
- Marking techniques.

## Jungle Penetrator

13-33. A jungle penetrator is a specialized hoist device used for vertical extraction in areas with no LZs. The steps for use include the following:

- Clear a small area of vegetation above the casualty.
- Signal the helicopter using VS-17 panels, smoke, or radio.
- Guide the penetrator to the casualty.
- To deploy, disconnect a strap, wrap it around the body under the armpits, and reconnect the strap to the device. An alternative is to place the right arm through the loop followed by the left arm.
- Fold one or two seats down (two are much more comfortable).
- Mount the seat and tighten the strap.
- Signal when ready (radio, shake the cable vigorously, or give a thumbs up).
- Fold arms around the device, around the chest.
- Drag feet as the hoist lifts one off the ground to prevent excessive spin (see figure 13-1).
