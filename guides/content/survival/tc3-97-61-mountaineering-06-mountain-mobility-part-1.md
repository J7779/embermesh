# Mountain Walking, Scrambling, Rock Climbing and Movement on Snow and Ice (Part 1 of 2)

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 6. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

A unit’s ability to maneuver through difficult and hazardous terrain provides it with access to the most tactically advantageous areas in the mountains. Units with Soldiers trained to a high level of mobility can move further, faster, and more safely through all levels of terrain. Mobility skill is the primary tool to manage risk when there is a fall hazard. Rope systems and belays can be used to mitigate fall risk during movement but require more time to complete, more technical training to accomplish, and can result in increased exposure to enemy attack. By far, the greatest combat multiplier in the mountains is high mobility skill. Units anticipating combat operations should focus on building individual movement skills over nearly all other special technical or tactical skills.

## Section I – Individual Movement Techniques

6-1. The speed at which the unit can move, the complexity of terrain they can negotiate unroped, and the violence of action they can muster as a team on the objective are all determined by the mobility of the unit’s weakest members. Properly equipped units with mobility skills are more likely to outmaneuver the enemy in physically demanding and hazardous terrain. Soldiers who have been trained in mobility techniques can take advantage of terrain above operational level 2. This provides commanders with access to the highest and most difficult to reach points of domination in their areas of operation. For those untrained in mountaineering, such terrain can prove too hazardous to move in. However, units well-trained to maneuver here can take advantage of reduced combat risks during all types of combat operations by owning ground that most enemy forces cannot access, granting them overwhelming tactical advantage. As a result, individual mobility skills training should always be a high priority for units anticipating these operations.

## Mobility

6-2. Individual mobility comprises many factors, the most important of which are fitness and movement techniques. Soldiers can increase their mobility by practicing the movement techniques specific to negotiating rough terrain. Soldiers build proficiency by approaching movement from a skill-building perspective, whether the movement is walking, scrambling, or climbing, and regardless of whether the surface is dirt, rock, snow, or ice. The hallmarks of mobility are balance, surefootedness, and net endurance. The results are reduced likelihood of slipping or falling, reduced energy expended, increased speed, and the ability to focus on other critical tasks. A reasonable goal for Soldiers is the ability to move confidently through 3rd-class terrain unroped, even when exposed to fall hazards that would result in significant injury or death.

### Balance

6-3. The most important aspect of mobility is balance. Soldiers who learn to move in such a way that their body is rarely out of balance will display a higher level of surefootedness and endurance than ones who have not mastered this skill.

6-4. Moving in balance requires a basic understanding of how the human body moves, how gravity and other forces act upon it, and how to counteract them. In the beginning of skills development, Soldiers can build knowledge by paying attention to when they feel out of balance, such as when they are stepping around, over, or under small obstacles in their path. Carrying a pack or heavy tactical equipment high on the body will increase the effort required to stay in balance.

6-5. As Soldiers begin to identify when movements put them out of balance, they should begin to experiment with adjusting body position, or foot placement, to maintain the feeling of being in balance. Early in training, achieving a less out-of-balance feeling may be the best a Soldier can achieve. Through practice, this will improve.

6-6. The complexity or severity of the terrain will determine the difficulty of the movement techniques and body positions required. Techniques for 5th-class rock climbing are much less intuitive than those necessary for hiking, but the principles for building skill are the same for both, and across the spectrum of difficulty.

6-7. With enough focus and practice, Soldiers will develop enough skill that moving becomes natural and requires little effort. At that point, they can focus on other mission critical tasks, such as navigation, security, communicating, firing weapon systems, moving casualties or any other complex tasks without the distraction of trying not to fall.

### Fitness

6-8. Soldiers develop and improve their mountain fitness through strengthening muscles that move and stabilize the body. Although strength in some large muscle groups is useful to move the body uphill against the pull of gravity, muscles used in maintaining balance are far more important. Recovering from a slip or stumble requires far more energy than just maintaining balance and footing in the first place. Well-developed supporting and stabilizing muscles prevent these slips and stumbles and reduce the burden on large muscle groups. In this way, mountain Soldiers are better at controlling corrective movements and conserving energy, which reduces fatigue and results in greater endurance over the course of a movement. (See FM 7-22 for more information on training specific muscle groups.)

6-9. Flexibility is another important aspect of fitness. Soldiers who are flexible have a greater range of movement. Soldiers with low flexibility may not be able to perform movements necessary to negotiate some obstacles. Soldiers should develop flexibility along with strength when training for mobility in the mountains.

6-10. General combat fitness does not translate to mountain fitness. Ruck marching, especially over relatively level ground, does not develop the balance and flexibility necessary for movement over rough, broken, and steep terrain. Upper body mass developed through heavy body building regimens does nothing for dismounted mobility and little for scrambling and climbing. A holistic approach to fitness is necessary. Physical training plans must balance combat fitness and mountain fitness requirements to best prepare Soldiers for combat action.

6-11. A Soldier’s role within the unit should be considered when training for the physical requirements of the mountains. An advanced military mountaineer who will be responsible for regular movement in 4th- and 5th-class terrain requires a higher level of mobility than a machine gunner does.

## Mountain Walking

6-12. Most terrain negotiated during combat operations in mountainous terrain requires only well-developed walking skills. However, this terrain is typically undeveloped, broken, steep and covered by loose dirt, rock, or snow. Surfaces are often slick. Heavy loads, nonuniform stride, and unpredictable footing challenge Soldiers moving in any of these conditions. Half of the battle for mountaineers is knowing where to step under these variable conditions to gain maximum traction, and the other half is knowing how to position their body to ensure their footing holds.

### Types of Terrain

6-13. Typically, mountain-walking terrain is considered class 1 and 2 terrains, but these skills are equally important in terrain that is more difficult. Mountain-walking techniques provide the foundation for higher-level skills, such as climbing. They require the same level of focus and practice to master.

### Mobility Equipment

6-14. Quality footwear, such as mountaineering boots, reduces many risks associated with moving in mountainous terrain. Quality boots increase both mobility and security of footing and can reduce orthopedic injuries. Boots designed for mountaineering range in weight, breathability, flexibility, and in how well they interface with other equipment. The rand helps protect the toe area from sharp rock and standing water. They have a high enough upper boot to protect the wearer’s ankles from sharp rock and provide joint support against rolling. They have recessed soles made of softer rubber, a protective band around the forefoot, at least a partial shank, and are constructed with abrasion and water-resistant materials. Mountain boot soles are designed for traction on dirt and slick rock alike. Their stiffer, stickier rubber soles provide a significant advantage when climbing.

6-15. Quality socks are more durable, help reduce blisters, better manage moisture, and dry faster than other materials.

6-16. Good quality insoles are critical to reducing minor foot injuries, such as blisters, during movement in rough, rocky terrain that can interfere with mission completion if they progress untreated.

6-17. Most Soldiers do not require special packs in walking terrain if they assemble their packs neatly and do not overload them.

6-18. The rucksack is the issued, modular lightweight load-carrying equipment (MOLLE) pack. The carry harness should be adjusted to the body of the Soldier carrying it. Soldiers should place heavier items close to the frame. Heavy items attached to the outside of, or higher on the pack, increase strain on the Soldier and raise the Soldier’s center of gravity, increasing the effort required to stay in balance. Soldiers should remove large pouches that they usually attach to the outside of MOLLE packs to streamline the pack and reduce its likelihood of being caught on obstacles. Soldiers should use compression straps to prevent loads from shifting and throwing them off balance. They should use waist belts to support the weight on their hips and lower their center of gravity.

6-19. Mountaineering packs are designed to be slimmer, taller, and lighter than Army packs. Typically, mountaineering packs are not designed with organization and accessibility in mind but rather to consolidate loads close to the body and to make movement and balance easier. They are generally less durable than Army packs; however, crossover packs, designed for remote mountain hunting, provide an excellent middle ground. The drawback of crossover packs is the high expense.

6-20. Using assault packs when walking terrain can reduce the negative impact on Soldier mobility if loads are not too heavy. Their lack of a rigid frame means that they provide less support. If lashing items to the outside of the assault pack becomes necessary to carry all required items, then carrying a properly loaded MOLLE pack is probably less detrimental to the Soldier’s mobility.

### Grassy Slopes

6-21. Grassy slopes are usually composed of small tussocks of growth rather than one continuous field. When ascending, step on the upper side of each hummock or tussock, where the ground is more level. When descending a grassy slope, climbers should use the traverse technique because of the uneven nature of the ground. A climber can easily build up too much speed and fall if a direct descent is attempted. When traversing, the climber’s uphill foot points in the direction of travel. The downhill foot points about 45° off the direction of travel (downhill). This maintains maximum sole contact and prevents possible downhill ankle rollout.

### Mountain Walking

6-22. Two critical skills in mountain walking are selecting foot placements and moving the body in a way that maintains balance with each step. (See figure 6-1, page 86.) These skills increase the security of foot placements and decrease slips. Surface characteristics vary greatly in mountains, and Soldiers should experiment with various features and surfaces in their operational environment. To obtain maximum traction on a given feature, Soldiers must position their bodies so that their weight pushes perpendicularly against the feature. Soldiers must reposition their body with each step to do this and should try to gain as much sole contact as possible to achieve maximum friction. As Soldiers become familiar with surface characteristics in their area of operation, they learn just how much friction is necessary for the footing to hold on various features such as the following:

- Common microfeatures that can provide greater footings are flat spots, small divots, or depressions, the uphill side of stable rocks and tussocks, rough rock features, dry spots, rough or heavily featured rock, and stable compact dirt.
- Common microfeatures that make traction more difficult to achieve and that should be avoided if possible are smooth features, loose dirt or small rocks, wet spots, and any surface covered with loose debris, such as leaves or small sticks.

*[Figure 6-1. Foot placement — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-23. Selecting reliable foot placements is even more difficult while descending. This is primarily because they must be made dynamically (while stepping down) and often out of their ideal body position. This prevents the Soldier from testing the placement before committing weight. If a slip occurs, momentum increases the likelihood of a fall or tumble.

6-24. General techniques for ascending:

- As terrain becomes steeper, routes should adopt a traversing or switchback pattern. This involves moving diagonally up and across a slope, then turning back and doing the same in the opposite direction as necessary. This switchback pattern reduces the intensity and demand on large muscle groups, slows the onset of fatigue, and allows Soldiers to maintain situational awareness. Charging in a straight line up a steep hill is more demanding, results in more extreme fatigue and longer recovery time, and increases the likelihood of slips, falls, and injury. This technique should be avoided unless speed is critical, and all members of the group are highly fit and experienced moving this way.
- The rest step technique involves locking out the rear leg briefly with each step. This gives muscles a micro rest with each step and results in greater endurance over long movements. (See figure 6-2, page 88.)
- Pressure breathing—this technique involves breathing all the way out on the exhale to empty the lungs of air. This does two positive things. First, it ensures all carbon dioxide is pushed out of the lungs, reducing the likelihood of cramping, and making more space for oxygenated air. Second, the negative pressure created in the lungs makes it easier to pull lower pressure air into them when moving at higher altitudes.

*[Figure 6-2. The rest step — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-25. General techniques for descending:

- Whenever Soldiers must use a questionable foot placement, they should identify a quick plan for how they will react prior to stepping down.
- Soldiers should ensure they bend their knees when stepping down to reduce impact on their joints. Prolonged descents often prove painful, and they could sustain long-term damage if they do not take measures to protect their joints under heavy loads.
- Poles are helpful when descending. They make it easier to stay in balance and allow the arms to absorb or reduce much of the impact on lower extremity joints. Even one pole can be very helpful.
- When Soldiers are new to walking in the mountains, it takes more focus to do so effectively. They move slower, but they benefit by moving at a rate that allows them to remain in balance without slipping and stumbling. Additionally, they improve mountain walking skills when allowed to move a little slower during training, becoming faster and more efficient in the end. Leaders should look at every dismounted movement in training as an opportunity to improve.

6-26. When a moderate pace is set, the need for rest halts decreases and the chance of personnel overheating lessens. Typically, shorter breaks taken more often are better than long breaks, especially in colder weather. However, leaders should consider fitness level, difficulty of the terrain, length of movement, speed, load, and level of fatigue when determining rest durations.

6-27. During the first half-hour of movement, an adjustment halt should be taken. Soldiers will loosen or tighten bootlaces as needed, adjust packs, and add or remove layers of clothing.

### Brush

6-28. Brush is both a help and a hindrance. Brush-filled gullies can provide routes and rally points concealed from observation; on the other hand, steep, brushy terrain is hazardous to negotiate. Cliffs and steep ravines are hidden traps, and blow downs and thickets can obstruct travel as much as manmade obstacles. When brush must be negotiated, take the most direct route across the obstacle; look for downed timber to use as raised paths through the obstacle; or create a tunnel through the obstacle by prying the brush apart, standing on lower branches, and using upper limbs for support.

### Dirt and Rocky Terrain

6-29. Surfaces comprised of mixed dirt are very common in mountainous terrain. As the terrain gets steeper and rockier, it typically becomes rougher and looser. This may require slowing the rate of movement to one that is maintainable by the less fit and skilled or overburdened members of the group. If this is not done, they are at risk of falling out or injury while struggling to catch up.

6-30. Hard ground is firmly compacted, rocky soil that does not give way under the weight of a Soldier’s step. It is usually found under mature forest canopy, in low brush or heather, and areas where animals have beaten out multiple trails. When ascending, employ the rest step to rest the leg muscles.

6-31. Scree fields are comprised of loose rock approximately fist-sized or smaller, that are common in the mountains and are a challenge to ascend. The experience is like climbing sand, except that the loose rock can be rough on ankles and lower legs. Climbers should avoid ascending scree fields, if possible, as they demand the expenditure of much more energy than other surfaces. Soldiers should kick in with the toe of the upper foot so that a step is formed in the loose scree. After determining that the step is stable, the Soldier transfers weight to the upper leg, then steps up, and repeats the process with the lower foot. Traversing scree is very difficult, as the loose stones slide out from under the foot downslope with each step. On the other hand, they are often a good option for descent routes. Greater focus on maintaining balance is required since foot placements are invariably unstable. The best method for descending scree slopes is to come straight down the slope using a short shuffling step with the knees bent, back straight, feet pointed downhill, and heels dug in. When several climbers descend a scree slope together, they should be as close together as possible (one behind the other at single-arm interval) to prevent injury from dislodged rocks. Avoid running down scree as this can cause a loss of control.

6-32. Talus fields are comprised of larger rocks. Often these rocks are loose and present a hazard to Soldiers moving through them. Soldiers must choose their foot placements carefully, often testing rocks to ensure they are stable. Typically, talus is easier to ascend than scree or loose dirt, but it is still demanding physically. Often it requires occasionally using hands for balance on steeper slopes. If a talus field is particularly unstable, then the switchback travel technique may not be appropriate for larger groups to use, as the risk of Soldiers higher on a slope knocking off a rock that hits Soldiers lower on the slope is potentially high.

### Scrambling

6-33. Scrambling is the act of moving over difficult or broken terrain, characterized by using mostly walking techniques but requires some use of the hands for balance or other specific climbing techniques. It is perhaps the most dangerous means of mobility in which snow and ice are not present. At the lowest level, scrambling involves simply placing a hand or hands on features periodically to help maintain balance and improve efficiency during movement. At the higher level, scrambling involves Soldiers making multiple easy rock-climbing moves, followed by several steps walking, and repeating that sequence throughout an ascent.

6-34. Typically, 3rd-class terrain is where Soldiers begin scrambling, but just exactly what terrain and terrain features require is determined largely by the mobility skill of the individual Soldier. Soldiers with low-mobility skill will need to use hands more often, especially if a fall risk is present, which will slow their movement. Soldiers with higher mobility skills need to use their hands less often for balance enabling them to move faster even when a fall risk is present.

6-35. The higher the rock-climbing skill, the more quickly, efficiently, and safely Soldiers can scramble. The best way to train Soldiers to be better at scrambling is to provide rock-climbing training. Rock climbing is easier to protect with ropes and forces Soldiers to use better movement techniques even though rock-climbing training involves steeper and more difficult terrain.

### Scrambling During Military Operations

6-36. Scrambling terrain can be some of the most dangerous terrain through which Soldiers can move. Often it is the low ground between higher terrain features or cliffs, subjecting it to falling object hazards and significant tactical disadvantage. Additionally, it can be exposed to fall hazards below.

6-37. Good reconnaissance and route planning are critical for movements through scrambling terrain. Whenever possible, small teams of advanced military mountaineers should be sent to find the best route ahead of the main body.

6-38. Maneuvering is incredibly difficult in terrain that requires a lot of scrambling. If enemy contact is a possibility, bounding overwatch must be used to ensure security during movement. Small teams may need to be sent forward to clear points of domination over the route. Sniper or machine gun overwatch positions may need to be established to cover movement through sections of terrain that is particularly exposed to enemy attack. All these security measures increase movement times significantly. (See ATP 3-21.50 for VDA-crossing TTP.)

6-39. Two-point slings should be used to secure rifles close to the body. Heavier weapon systems, such as sniper rifles and machine guns, may need to be secured to rucksacks close to the body during sustained scrambling to allow Soldiers to use their hands freely for assistance.

### Scrambling Considerations

6-40. There are no scrambling specific movement techniques, but greater care in selecting hand and footholds is often necessary given the nature of the terrain. Possessing rock-climbing skills (discussed later in the chapter) increases the efficiency with which Soldiers can scramble. Vegetation may appear to provide good handholds but may easily pull free. Large boulders or masses of loose rock may be knocked free without warning. Groups should move in such a way that Soldiers further back in the formation are not subject to rockfall from those above. Some scrambling terrain may require simple fixed lines to aid movement. This is even more likely during descending. These sites must be identified, planned for, and established prior to the main body arriving for efficiency. (See chapter 11 for more techniques for moving on ropes.)

## Climbing Rock

6-41. Climbing rock, typically 4th- and 5th-class terrain, requires a very high level of skill, especially when done for sustained periods. The probability and consequence of falls are high, even under the best conditions. Rope systems are usually necessary to protect climbers from injury or death, slowing movement and reducing tactical maneuverability.

6-42. Building rock-climbing skill increases the confidence and speed at which Soldiers can move unroped in easier terrain, even when subjected to higher fall consequence. Soldiers who possess moderate rock-climbing skills can maneuver more safely and efficiently unroped in easy terrain. This should be the goal for level 1 mountaineers. Soldiers with high rock-climbing skills can do the same in moderate terrain. This should be the goal for level 2 mountaineers.

6-43. The basic principles of rock climbing are the same as walking in rough terrain; they are just more difficult to achieve, and the required movements less intuitive. Selecting hand and foot placements can be much more difficult, and the movements and body positions required for them to hold can be challenging to perform. Experience built through climbing terrain more difficult than would need to be accessed in military operations is the path to building rock-climbing skill.

### Climbing Equipment

6-44. Climbing shoes are small, lightweight, close-fitting shoes with a smooth, soft, rubber sole used by recreational climbers, which increase the precision in which weight can be applied to small rock features. They contain hook-and-loop fasteners or laces to secure to the foot, and they are designed for various levels of climbing difficulty. The tighter, stiffer, and more downcurved the toe is, the more difficult the terrain (YDS 5.9 and higher) for which they are designed. Typically, climbing shoes are not appropriate for military applications because they are much more uncomfortable to wear for long periods and unsuitable for walking even short distances. However, these are an excellent choice for advanced military mountaineers looking to improve their skills using established routes off military installations, where climbing with muddy boots may be frowned upon. A moderate climbing shoe that is comfortable for the Soldier to wear for long periods is sufficient in training and (rarely) operationally. Operationally, climbing shoes are only appropriate to small, assault climber teams that must guide or establish routes for others in the most difficult operational terrain. (See figure 6-3.)

*[Figure 6-3. Rock-climbing specific footwear — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-45. The issued advanced combat ballistic helmet provides sufficient protection from head impacts during a fall and from small falling objects. However, it is not designed to absorb large impact from above, and its weight introduces substantial additional fatigue during sustained climbing. Rock-climbing helmets are very lightweight and are designed to protect the wearer against impact from falling objects but do not provide a means of mounting night vision devices. Plastic, tactical bump helmets offer a reasonable compromise for military rock climbers as they provide the best combination of top- and side-impact protection, the ability to mount night vision devices, and weight savings. Commanders must consider risks when determining headgear requirements for Soldiers during rock climbing. It is recommended that lead climbers use, at a minimum, a lighter-weight helmet. (See figure 6-4, page 92.)

*[Figure 6-4. Climbing helmets — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-46. The issued MOLLE pack is not suited for rock climbing. Its size, empty weight, and width interfere with movements necessary for even moderate climbing. When loaded, climbing may not even be possible. Soldiers climbing sustained rock routes may require a smaller lightweight pack to carry required items (for example, water, sensitive items, communications equipment) during the climb. Whatever packs are worn should be snugged to the body, and waist straps as well as chest straps worn to reduce the load from shifting during climbing and throwing the climber out of balance.

### Rock Climbing During Military Operations

6-47. Sustained climbing in 5th-class terrain during conventional military operations is impractical. However, routes through easier terrain are often blocked by shorter sections of 4th or easier 5th-class terrain. These routes may be the only or most tactically sound approaches to points of domination for properly trained units. Small teams of advanced military mountaineers can be used to establish fixed-rope routes for lesser-trained Soldiers to use for protection during movement. Accessing these points may be required to reach enemy defensive positions, establish observation posts, or position small teams conducting reconnaissance or forward observation of fires.

6-48. Soldiers climbing 4th- and 5th-class terrain are typically unable to provide themselves sufficient local security during movement. Bounding overwatch in vertical terrain by small-specialized teams or VDA-crossing procedures must be performed by a larger unit to provide adequate security for climbing Soldiers. (See ATP 3-21.50 for more on VDA.) Small reconnaissance and surveillance teams may have to rely on careful technical route selection that provides concealment throughout the approach and the climb. Fortunately, 4th-class and easier 5th-class terrain often provide such opportunities for teams with the technical and mobility skills necessary to negotiate them.

### Climbing Route Selection

6-49. Before climbing, Soldiers should scan the rock for features that indicate that climbing the route is possible. With experience, Soldiers can determine how difficult a section of rock will be for them to complete. Climbers should begin to form a plan for climbing the route and a plan to protect themselves with a rope system (when necessary) before leaving the ground. (See chapter 10 on how to protect climbers from fall hazards.)

6-50. During military operations, the least difficult routes possible should be selected for travel. (See chapter 7 for more information on planning technical movement routes.)

### Rock-Climbing Techniques

6-51. The basic principles for rock climbing are the same as those for walking in rough terrain. They are finding and selecting suitable holds and positioning the body to achieve sufficient friction for continued movement. Due to the vertical nature of the terrain, hands must be used to aid Soldiers in maintaining balance and preventing them from tipping backward off the rock.

6-52. Suspect rock-climbing holds should be tested to ensure they are secure by tapping with a hand or foot. Holds that move or shift should be avoided. This is common in undeveloped 4th- and 5th-class terrain.

### Maintaining Balance

6-53. Balance is the most important aspect of rock climbing and footwork is the foundation of balance. Good use of the feet saves energy and makes staying in balance easier. A relaxed, comfortable stance is essential. The body should be centered over the feet as much as possible. On lower angle sections of climbing, it should be positioned away from the rock face. Alternatively, it should be positioned closer to the rock face on steeper sections. This puts downward pressure on footholds, increasing their security. Smaller lower body movements are often less strenuous than larger ones, which take more effort to stay in balance.

6-54. Rock climbing usually requires the use of handholds to maintain balance. Handholds can be large jugs that allow climbers to hold nearly their whole weight or tiny crimpers that only hold a fraction of their weight. Specific handhold techniques are discussed throughout this section. New climbers commonly overuse their arms to pull themselves up the rock and burn out quickly. Tired or pumped forearms are a sign of bad footwork.

6-55. Whenever possible, three points of contact should be maintained while moving the fourth to the next hold. This can reduce the likelihood of a fall should one point of contact fail.

### Types of Contact

6-56. Though counterintuitive, often pushing rather than pulling is required during climbing. Using downward pressure involves pushing down on a feature with the hands to allow unweighting of the other hand or one foot for movement to the next hold. Using counterpressure involves pushing away from features to allow a hand or foot to be unweighted for movement. (See figure 6-5, page 94.)

*[Figure 6-5. Downward or counterpressure — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-57. Occasionally, the holds available put the climber out of balance, but repositioning the body (usually by extending a leg to the left or right) improves the climber’s balance. This technique is called counterbalancing and often allows the climber greater reach. (See figure 6-6.) Often the climber can find a microfeature to touch or apply slight pressure to with the foot improving the security of the move.

*[Figure 6-6. Counterbalance — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-58. Occasionally, the climber must switch hands or feet on a hold to make the next move. Sometimes the hold can be shared temporarily by two hands or feet, even if they are unsecure, while the lead hand or lead foot moves. Other times, the second hand or foot may need to use a temporary, perhaps less desirable hold, while the lead one moves.

6-59. Dynamic moves are sudden, explosive moves where climbers throw themselves out of balance, potentially even losing contact with the rock, to reach another hold. These moves must be calculated carefully because failing to execute them perfectly usually results in a fall.

### Slab and Face Climbing

6-60. Slabs are typically smoother, lower angle rock features. They can be large or small. Comparatively, they provide the climber with few hands and footholds and require good footwork and balancing to climb. The primary technique used to climb them is smearing with the feet. This involves placing the sole of the boot or shoe on the rock with toe facing uphill to obtain maximum contact and friction. The body is then positioned away from the rock so that the foot does not slip. The closer climbers can get to pushing perpendicular to the rock, the more friction they will create. (See figure 6-7, page 96.) Hands are usually used to help balance by pushing against the rock. Any microfeature that can be found will improve the security of climbing slabby terrain. Climbing slabby rock helps build confidence in footwork and good balance skills.

6-61. Face climbing is what is commonly associated with rock climbing. Face climbs are typically suitably featured rock faces (cliffs) where climbers employ a wide variety of basic moves to ascending. Climbers spend most of their time facing the rock, like climbing a ladder. Many footholds are small or sloped and require edging or smearing to be effective; others are larger. (See figure 6-8.) Many techniques for using handholds exist. (See figure 6-8.) Using knees should be avoided because they are more likely to slip than good footwear.

6-62. Most slab or face climbing terrain appropriate to military operations can be climbed in combat boots, mountaineering boots, or approach shoes. Moderate to difficult climbs require climbing shoes. (See figure 6-9.)

**Resting During Climbing**

6-63. Resting is critical during sustained rock climbing. Any opportunity to stand up and let go or lightly hold on with the hands should be taken. Sometimes the body or a body part can be wedged into a large crack, allowing the climber to relax. Often, the only rests available are between moves while looking for the next hold. When not actively moving, climbers should keep their weight on their skeletal system as much as possible. This means locking out legs or arms and providing muscles with a brief rest. Doing this is particularly important to preserve arm strength. Often one arm and the climbers’ feet can support their weight while they shake out the other arm and hand. (See figure 6-10, page 98.)

**Specific Moves**

6-64. Mantling involves pulling down with both arms on the edge of a ledge type feature until the chest is ledge height, then transitioning to pushing down on the ledge while walking the feet up the face below. This move is common to climbing out of a swimming pool. (See figure 6-11.)

6-65. Stemming involves placing the feet on features to the left and right of the body so that force is applied outward as well as down. Stemming is typically very stable and requires a relatively low amount of energy to maintain. (See figure 6-12, page 100.)

6-66. Underclinging involves gripping an overhanging rock feature with the hands, palm up, and pushing against a lower rock face with the feet. Underclings can be very stable but can be quite strenuous and tiring. Occasionally, underlings are the only means of getting through a section of climbing. (See figure 6-13, page 101.)

6-67. Liebacking involves using counterforce by pulling hard on a vertical or diagonal feature (such as a large flake) while pushing hard against the face of the rock. These moves are very strenuous because little or none of the climbers’ weights is supported by their feet. In fact, leg muscles are being used to push away from the rock, so they are very taxing to the upper body. The climber’s hands are then walked up the feature while the feet are walked up the face. Footholds on the face, however small, can be used to make liebacking easier and more secure. (See figure 6-14, page 102.)

### Crack Climbing

6-68. Crack climbing requires a specific set of nonintuitive techniques that take quite a bit of practice to build proficiency in. While some climbs involving cracks can be climbed with weak or no crack technique, more difficult crack climbs require climbing shoes and good technique to be climbable. Mixed crack climbing involves using features on the rock face for footholds while using a prominent crack for handholds. (See figure 6-15.) Classic crack climbing involves moving up a prominent crack by alternately jamming hands or fingers and feet or toes and walking them up the crack. Often, one foot is placed out of the crack to the side for balance, even on a featureless face. Off-width crack climbing is the term for climbing cracks that are larger than fist and foot sized but not large enough to fit the entire body. These climbs are difficult.

*[Figure 6-15. Mixed face and crack climbing — illustration not reproduced.]*

**Jamming**

6-69. A critical technique in climbing cracks involves placing a hand or foot into a crack and turning, twisting, or otherwise manipulating them so that they become temporarily jammed in the crack. A good jam can be achieved in a variety of ways depending on crack size, whether it has constrictions in it or not, and its position in relation to other holds on the climb. Jams can provide solid hands or footholds but are often uncomfortable or even painful. Crack size determines which jam technique to use for a given hold. Constrictions in the crack make jam holds more secure.

**Types of Hand Jams**

6-70. Hand-sized jams are the easiest to perform. The hand is inserted open into the crack and then the fingers bent exert pressure against the walls of the crack. Often turning one hand thumb down is useful. Typically, leaning to the same side adds security to a thumb up hand jam. (See figure 6-16, page 104 for variations of hand jamming.)

6-71. Fist jams are techniques necessary for cracks that are larger than those that work for hand jams. The same jamming principles apply, but climbers just insert their entire fist into the crack. (See figure 6-17 for variations of fist jamming.)

6-72. Finger jams are techniques necessary for cracks that are smaller than those that will accept a hand. The same jamming principles apply, but the climber only inserts their fingers, or parts of their fingers into the crack. Finger jamming is more difficult and can be painful for the joints. It is important to strengthen finger muscles prior to attempting to support substantial weight with finger jams to prevent joint strain. (See figure 6-18.)

**Types of Foot Jams**

6-73. Foot jams involve turning the foot so that it fits into the crack and then twisting it so that the width of the foot wedges into the crack. This can be uncomfortable but can provide very secure footholds. (See figure 6-19.)

6-74. Toe jams are used in the same manner as foot jams, but in smaller cracks. (See figure 6-20, page 106.) **Types of Arm Jams**

6-75. Arm bars is a technique used for off-width cracks and involves inserting an entire arm into the crack and bending at the elbow to create a jam. (See figure 6-21, page 106.)

6-76. Chicken wing is a technique also used for off-width cracks and involves inserting an entire arm into the crack bent at the elbow and pushing against one wall of the crack with the hand to create a jam. (See figure 6-21, page 106.)

### Dihedral and Chimney Climbing

6-77. Climbing dihedrals and chimneys involves positioning the body inside a feature and using counterpressure or stemming to make upward movement. These features can often make for less strenuous climbing due to the counterpressure positions allowing for more rests. A dihedral is two rock faces that meet to form a corner, like an open book. Often there is a crack where the two faces meet. Chimneys are roughly parallel-sided features. They could be like a vertical shaft or crack large enough to accept a whole body.

6-78. Dihedrals can usually be climbed with a combination of stemming and either crack or face climbing. Dihedrals are easier and less strenuous to climb when the two faces have multiple and large features.

6-79. Chimneys are often unpleasant features to climb but provide a lot of security during movement. The basic technique for climbers is to position their body in the chimney and use counterpressure by pushing with arms, legs, and back against opposing sides. (See figures 6-22 through 6-24, pages 107 through 109, for variations in chimney climbing techniques.) Often during chimney climbing, it is necessary to attach climbing packs to the harness hanging below the climber.

### Traversing

6-80. Traversing is moving laterally on the rock. This is often necessary because the features that make up a rock route are not always continuous or travel straight up a face. Traversing often involves a series of placement exchanges. Most traversing is done facing the rock.

6-81. Hand traversing requires a ledge, flake, other horizontal feature, or a series of horizontally oriented large handholds. Climbers’ hands walk their way across the features while their feet walk their way across a face below. (See figure 6-25, page 110.)

6-82. Steep traverse is a technique often used when handholds are not as good, but decent footholds are present. Climbers lean heavily in the direction they want to move and place hands on available holds, and then walk their feet in the same direction using available footholds. Sometimes a crossover step is helpful. This technique typically requires solid side pulling, counterpressure, and counterforce skills. (See figure 6-26.)

*[Figure 6-25. Hand traverse — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Topping Out or Gaining Ledges

6-83. Topping out or gaining a ledge can be one of the more challenging parts of climbing. Often low holds located near where climbers want to put their feet need to be used to get over the edge. Often, a mantling technique is effective. Most importantly, climbers should avoid laying on their belly on the edge when moving because it may be difficult to get their feel back on the rock, and they may slide off on sloped ledges. A crossover step is helpful. This technique typically requires solid side pulling, counterpressure, and counterforce skills. (See figure 6-26 for the proper technique.)

### Down Climbing

6-84. Down climbing is far more difficult than climbing up and takes much practice to gain proficiency. This is largely because rock features are often difficult to identify from above. They are easily obscured by other features above them or even the climber’s body parts. However, down climbing is an important skill to practice, as it is often necessary to do in 3rd- and 4th-class terrain.

6-85. Facing out is a technique used on low angle rock. Climbers face out and use their hands for stability behind and below their body while moving their feet down to the next hold. (See figure 6-27, page 112.)

6-86. Walking down a slab is a technique that involves simply walking down slabby rock while maintaining maximum friction and balance. (See figure 6-27, page 112.)

6-87. Facing sideways involves climbers facing to the side and side stepping down to lower footholds while controlling and balancing their movement using handholds above. Climbers use this technique on steeper rock. (See figure 6-27, page 112.)

6-88. Facing the rock involves climbing down in the same position as the climber would use to move up the rock. Climbers lower themselves down with their arms, keeping their body away from the rock so they can see footholds below, and then transition their weight onto the next foothold. (See figure 6-27, page 112.)

### Training for Rock Climbing

6-89. Beginner rock-climbing training is typically conducted at established climbing areas using top rope belay techniques (see chapter 10). When supervised by qualified mountaineers, this kind of rock climbing is relatively low risk and has a high payoff for developing mobility skills. One restriction is that sites do not often allow large groups to be actively engaged. Thus, climbing training should be planned along with concurrent training for smaller groups to rotate through. Route difficulty should be challenging to climbers but easy enough that they can be successful. This motivates climbers to build skills. A motivational rather than critical atmosphere is most effective when building climbing skills.

6-90. Climbing training for more advanced military mountaineers can be conducted the same way, just on more challenging routes, but should also be conducted in a multipitch setting that more accurately reproduces conditions of climbing during military operations. This setting requires the development of lead climbing and route-finding skills, which are critical to the advanced military mountaineer.

6-91. Leaders should try to learn the etiquette associated with climbing before using civilian climbing areas.

## Section II – Movement over Snow and Ice

6-92. Deep snow, steep powdery slopes, glare ice, and frozen waterfalls all present significant challenges to movement. The basic individual movement principles and skill development necessary for movement under other surface conditions also apply to movement over snow and ice. However, additional skills are necessary for efficiency and safety. Specialized equipment is required in even the easiest snow or ice-covered terrain.

## Mobility over Snow and Ice

6-93. Fitness and movement techniques are the foundation of moving through snow or up steep ice. However, identifying and selecting secure foot placements can be difficult because traction is drastically reduced. Movement without specialized equipment, such as crampons or skis, is difficult under almost all conditions above 1st-class terrain. Even when it is possible, deep snow requires a substantial amount of energy to move through unaided. Anything deeper than calf-deep snow will impede movement in even easy terrain, and a few inches of snow or a glaze of ice will make steeper terrain treacherous. Soldiers must develop the same level of skill using snow and ice equipment, which requires proficiency in an additional set of skills. Like moving over broken rocky terrain, these skills can only be developed through experience and repetition. Units that train their Soldiers to proficiency in these conditions can move faster, safer, and access terrain that others cannot.

### Equipment for Snow and Ice

6-94. Skis provide substantial floatation in deep snow and require the least amount of energy for movement. This is especially true for downhill movement where much energy can be saved over walking. There are varieties of ski systems available for movement over snow, but those most suited to military mountaineering are called alpine touring skis. The ski itself is like a downhill ski, but the binding allows the heel to release for movement over flat or uphill terrain. It can also be locked down for greater control when skiing downhill. Climbing skins are used in conjunction with skis to climb uphill. Skins are designed to slide in only one direction, providing traction in deep snow and on steep slopes. At a minimum, a stiff mountaineering boot is necessary for use with skis. In the steepest terrain, a dedicated rigid plastic ski boot may be required. Compatibility between boots and bindings must be considered. (See figure 6-28, page 114.)

*[Figure 6-28. Alpine touring skis — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-95. Snowshoes are another tool available to Soldiers for moving over deep snow. Modern snowshoes provide sufficient floatation and traction for moving through snow on fairly steep (class 2) slopes and should be small and light enough to pack, if necessary. Snowshoes work with any boot. (See figure 6-29.)

*[Figure 6-29. Snowshoes — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-96. Ski poles are typically used with snowshoes and skis to provide more lateral stability and balance. Ski poles can be used to provide excellent support for weapons systems during firing from the prone, kneeling, and standing positions.

6-97. Sleds are useful for carrying large amounts of equipment, such as bivouac equipment, heavy weapons, food and water, or other supplies. They range in size, shape, and weight, all of which should be considered before selection for military operations. Four- and ten-Soldier Ahkio-style sleds are available through the military supply system. These are designed to carry all group sustainment equipment (for example, tents, stoves, fuel) for a four- or ten-Soldier group. These come with a cover and rigid poles, convenient for dragging up and downhill. (See figure 6-30, page 116.)

*[Figure 6-30. Ahkio sleds — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-98. Crampons attach to the Soldier’s boot to provide traction on ice and snow. There are two basic types: general mountaineering crampons and ice climbing crampons. Mountaineering crampons have less aggressive points. They are designed for use in steep snow and on low-angle ice and can be used for ice climbing but are not ideal. Ice climbing crampons have points that are more aggressive and are typically more rigid. They are designed for steep ice climbing but can be used in snow and low angle ice as well. Crampons work best when paired with a stiff leather or rigid plastic mountaineering boot. They do not work well with issued cold-weather combat boots. (See figure 6-31.)

*[Figure 6-31. Crampons — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-99. Mountain axes are important tools for movement over steep snow and occasional low-angle ice. The two types of axes usually used in alpine terrain are general mountain axes and hybrid mountain axes. General axes are designed for steep snow and hybrid axes are designed for steep snow and low-angle ice climbing. Ice axes have an adze to the rear of the pick for chopping snow and ice. (See figure 6-32.)

6-100. Ice tools are modified ice axes designed for steep ice climbing. They are much more aggressive and curved to help obtain solid sticks in hard vertical ice and over bulges. They are less effective in snow than general or hybrid axes because of their short length. Ice tools can include an adze or a hammerhead. Hammers are used for placing pitons during mixed climbing. ### Military Operations in Snow- and Ice-Covered Terrain

6-101. Snow and ice present many additional challenges for units that need to move in mountainous terrain. Specialized equipment, training, and experience are necessary to move in it safely and effectively. Whether the challenges are deep snow, slick slopes, or steep sections of vertical ice, they will prove substantial obstacles for unprepared units. Units anticipating operating in such terrain and conditions should train in them prior to engaging in combat operations.

6-102. Even with proper equipment and training, it is unlikely that larger units can move in terrain steeper than 2nd-class terrain without ropes due to the high risk of long sliding falls. Small teams of advanced military mountaineers should be trained to move over ice and snow and tasked with finding routes and establishing rope systems for larger units. (See chapter 11 for establishing fixed-rope systems for troop movement in steep terrain and ATP 3-21.50 for crossing VDA TTP.)
