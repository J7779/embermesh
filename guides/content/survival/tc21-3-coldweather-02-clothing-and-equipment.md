# Cold-Weather Clothing, Sleeping Systems and Load Carrying

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 2. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Wear and Care of Individual Clothing

2-1. The Army’s cold-weather clothing system is designed for protection in various weather situations. The principal design features of this system are insulate, layer, and ventilate. Insulation material reduces the amount of body heat loss. Regulating the amount of insulation can affect the amount of heat that is lost or retained. (See figure 2-1.) Ventilation helps maintain a comfortable body temperature by allowing excess heat and moisture to escape.

*[Figure 2-1. Insulation — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-2. Several layers of clothing provide more insulation and movement than one heavy garment, even if the heavy garment is as thick as the combined layers. Air pockets trapped between the layers provide insulation, warmth, and ventilation.

2-3. There are four ways to make these principles work: keep it clean, avoid overheating, wear it loose, and keep it dry. Remembering this mnemonic as C-O-L-D helps when dressing for the weather. (See figure 2-2.)

*[Figure 2-2. C-O-L-D — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-4. Dirty clothes are cold clothes. Dirt and grease clog the air spaces in clothing. reducing insulation and ventilation. Select comfortable clothing but do not overdress. In cold weather, it is better to be a little cool rather than risk overheating.

2-5. All items of the cold-weather uniform are large enough to allow wearing the appropriate number of needed layers. If the uniform items do not fit loosely, the insulation that generates warmth is greatly reduced.

2-6. It is very important to keep clothing dry. Wet clothing conducts heat away from the body faster than dry clothing. Moisture soaks into clothing from the inside and the outside. From the inside, guard against sweating and from the outside, guard against melting snow. Always brush snow and frost from clothing before entering any heated shelter or vehicle.

### Dressing for Wet-Cold or Dry-Cold Weather

2-7. As mentioned in chapter one, temperatures that are above 19°F (–7°C) are known as wet-cold conditions; temperatures consistently below 19°F (–7°C) cause dry-cold conditions. Wet-cold conditions usually involve a freezing and thawing cycle, often accompanied by rain or wet snow. This type of weather is hard to dress for and Soldiers battle ever-changing elements to keep clothing dry.

2-8. During dry-cold conditions, the ground is usually frozen and snow is dry. Although temperatures during these conditions may be lower than wet-cold conditions, it is easier to stay warm. The temperature zones below dry cold (intense, extreme, and hazardous) are also dry in nature, and Soldiers should dress for the dry-cold environment.

2-9. A major protection in reducing the effects of heat loss is caring for the head and feet. When in the shelter, be sure not to hang clothing on drying lines too close to the tent heater or stove fire. Too much heat damages fabric or dries leather, destroying the insulation value.

### Balaclava

2-10. Wear the balaclava (hat) under the helmet or under the hood of the shell layer. To do so, there are three configurations. Fold the balaclava—

- Up, into a top-of-the-head hat.
- Down, with the face exposed.
- Down, with the face covered.

2-11. Up, into a top-of-the-head hat: fold the bottom of the balaclava to the inside to form a hat. Wear the hat with the face opening to the rear to prevent breath condensation forming on the face-forward portion. If there is a need to change this configuration later, a wet hat is not placed directly on the face.

2-12. Down, with the face exposed: pull the balaclava over the head with the lower portion of the face opening under the chin. Note that doing this repeatedly stretches the fabric and reduces its ability to stay over the nose when needed.

2-13. Down, with the face covered: pull the lower portion of the face opening over the mouth and nose. Use goggles to cover the eyes and any remaining exposed skin, if required.

### Boots

2-14. Intermediate cold-wet boots (ICWB) with removable tan liners can be ordered using the national stock number (NSN) 8430-01-527-8274. They are rated from 68°F to 14°F (20°C to –10°C). Two pairs of liners are issued with this boot. When donning the boot with liners, put the liners on first and secure them with hook and loop tape. Then, insert the foot into the boot, ensuring there is no bunching as this causes blisters. When fitting this boot, make sure to wear the sock type used in the field and that the boot fits as comfortably as street shoes.

2-15. Extreme cold-weather boots (NSN 8430-00-655-5535) are also called white vapor barrier (VB) boots, or bunny boots. They are rated from 19°F to –60°F (–7°C to –51C) and are used in cold-dry environments. Some VB boots have a pressure relief valve. The valve is used to equalize air pressure. When rapidly changing altitude, briefly open the valve to equalize pressure and then close it. (See figure 2-3.)

*[Figure 2-3. Pressure equalization valve — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-16. There is a fine line when Soldiers should switch from the ICWB to the VB boot. Temperature zone II dry cold (19ºF to –4ºF) is the recommended range to switch to VB boots. However, this depends on the workload. Leaders should ensure that both types of boots are available in the event of a temperature swing or change of mission. This gives maximum flexibility.

2-17. When active, feet sweat. Due to the waterproof rubber lining, moisture remains on socks or in the bottom of the boots. The feet, even if damp, stay warm because the heat given off by the body remains inside the boots. If the boots are worn for long periods of time, the feet become white and wrinkled when the footwear is removed. This is not cause for alarm. Drying, massaging with foot powder, warming, and putting on dry socks corrects this.

2-18. If there are long periods of inactivity, feet may become cold. Exercises such as knee bends, stomping, running in place, or wiggling toes help make them warm. Staying active and caring for these boots is very important. They should be washed inside and out with soapy water at least once a month, or as required by the unit standard operating procedure (SOP). Do not wear VB boots in a sleeping bag, as the feet need to dry.

2-19. Inspect these boots often and repair any holes or punctures immediately. Temporary repairs can be made by patching the hole with any kind of tape. Even chewing gum can be used. Seal the hole quickly to prevent the insulation from becoming wet. If one of the boots feels heavier than the other, the insulation is probably wet, and should be turned in for new ones. Insulated boots are tested annually to ensure they are still waterproof and have less than 0.01 percent moisture in the insulation. The repair kit (NSN 8465-00-753-6335) is similar to a bicycle inner tube repair kit.

**Note**. This is a tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTP): use the upper leg portion of the sock to dry the toe box of the VB. Store a pair of socks in a zippered plastic bag in a cargo pocket for unexpected trips into the overflow or after extra-tough movement. A fresh pair of socks can significantly elevate mood. Always change socks when getting into a sleeping bag.

## Extended Cold-Weather Clothing System, Gen III

2-20. The extended cold-weather clothing system (ECWCS) is issued as a kit, allowing flexibility through all temperature zones. System components use the most recent materials and technology. The result is a reduction in weight and bulk from the GEN II system without loss of protection from the cold. The GEN III ECWCS uses the layering approach to draw moisture away from the body and should be worn as discussed below.

2-21. The first layer of clothing is the base layer. It draws moisture away from the body, allowing it to evaporate. The next or middle layer is the insulation layer. It provides trapped air spaces that create warmth. The last layer is the shell layer. It protects all the other layers, blocking wind and precipitation.

### Seven Levels of Dressing

2-22. Level I: lightweight cold-weather undershirt and drawers. This is a base layer consisting of a long sleeve top and full-length bottom garments constructed with silk-weight moisture-wicking polyester. The material aids in moving moisture from the skin to the outer layers of the garment. The top garment has holes in the sleeves for the thumbs. Place thumbs through the holes to keep the garment down around the wrist. Wear it next to skin or with a mid-weight cold-weather shirt and drawers for added insulation. This aids the transfer of moisture. (See figure 2-4.)

*[Figure 2-4. Lightweight underclothes — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-23. Level II: middleweight cold-weather shirt and drawers. This layer is also a base layer. However, it can also act as an insulation layer when used in conjunction with the Level I layer. It consists of a long sleeve top and full-length bottom garments constructed of polyester grid fleece. These garments provide light insulation for use in mild climates, and act as a base layer for colder climates. There is an increased surface area to help wick moisture away from the wearer during movement.

2-24. The top has a zipper that is used to form a mock turtleneck. When zipped down, it allows better ventilation as the workload increases. The top has holes in the sleeves for the thumbs. Place thumbs through the holes to keep the garment down around the wrist and wear this layer over the lightweight cold-weather undershirt and drawers or next to the skin, depending on weather conditions. (See figure 2-5.)

**Note**. This is a TTP: sew a 6-inch square of 100-gram (3.5 ounce) fleece as a pocket onto the Layer 2 shirt to store small batteries, energy bars, and other items that stay warm and can be ready to use. The Layer 2 neck zipper can be held closed with the addition of hook and loop tape across the top of the zipper.

*[Figure 2-5. Middleweight underclothes — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-25. Level III: fleece jacket. The jacket acts as the primary insulation layer for use in moderate to cold climates. Imitation fur mimicking insulation provides an increased warmth-to-weight ratio and reduces volume when packed. There are two inner mesh pockets to store things that need to be kept warm, such as batteries, rations, canteens, and other items. The zipper forms a mock turtleneck or can be used to ventilate the garment, as needed. Wear underneath the shell layers. This layer provides little protection from the wind or precipitation and should not be used as an outer garment. Always wear it in conjunction with a shell. (See figure 2-6.)

*[Figure 2-6. Cold-weather fleece jacket — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-26. Level IV: wind cold-weather jacket. Made of a lightweight, windproof, and water repellant material, this jacket is a minimum outer shell layer. When combined with body armor, the Army combat uniform (or both), the jacket improves the moisture-wicking performance of the insulation layers. It has two sleeve pockets and a mock turtleneck. Two chest-level pockets are designed with mesh pocket linings to help with ventilation while wearing body armor. This is best worn as wind protection during cool days. (See figure 2-7.)

*[Figure 2-7. Cold-weather wind jacket — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-27. Level V: soft shell cold-weather jacket and trousers (outer shell layer). Made of a highly water resistant, windproof material, it increases moisture vapor transfer over current hard-shell garments and provides a reduction in weight, bulk, and noise signature during movement. The increased breathability of the jacket improves performance of the insulation layers by decreasing saturation due to moisture accumulation. It has a storable hood that works with the ballistic helmet, and two hand-warming pockets on the chest with mesh lining to aid in ventilation. The jacket also has pit zippers and two sleeve pockets. Draw cords on the bottom prevents snow and wind from entering the system. Wear this jacket when the average temperature is below 19°F (–7°C). The necessary base and insulation layers depend upon the temperature, wind, and activity level. (See figure 2-8.)

*[Figure 2-8. Soft outer shell wind jacket and trousers — illustration not reproduced.]*

**Notes**. This is a TTP: attach a piece of parachute cord long enough to go under the instep of the boot (like a stirrup) to the two loops on the leg opening of the trousers. This helps keep the trousers from riding up or letting snow fall into clothing. Attach a 4-inch (approximately 10 centimeter [cm]) loop of gutted parachute cord to the zipper pull at the waist of the trouser drop seat. Thread the hook and loop waist adjustment tabs and secure. This keeps the zipper from migrating down and opening the trousers to the elements. Attach a 4-inch loop of parachute cord to the zipper pull of all layers. This helps when wearing gloves or mittens. (See figure 2-9.)

2-28. Battle buddies should team up if anyone is having difficulty reaching the zippers. Struggling only creates aggravation. Restore lost body water with NSN 8030-01-408-9446, or other available products.

*[Figure 2-9. Securing trousers to the belt, boots, and zipper extension — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-29. Level VI: extreme cold/wet weather jacket and trousers outer shell layer. A waterproof layer for use in prolonged or hard rain and cold-wet conditions, this jacket has two pass-through chest pockets for ventilation and a storable hood that works with the ballistic helmet. Wear this jacket when the average temperature is above 19ºF (–7°C) and the weather is alternating between freezing and thawing. The base and insulation layers needed depends upon the temperature, wind, and activity level. (See figure 2-10.)

*[Figure 2-10. Extreme cold/wet soft outer shell wind jacket and trousers — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-30. Level VII: extreme cold-weather parka and trousers. Providing superior warmth, the parka and trousers compact easily with low weight and volume. These garments are highly water resistant and windproof. Sized to fit over body armor during static activities requiring maximum insulation, the trouser incorporates full side zippers for donning and doffing over boots and other layers. Designed to be the last level of protection worn in extreme cold weather over all other layers, it is primarily meant for static positions. (See figure 2-11.)

*[Figure 2-11. Extreme cold-weather parka and trousers — illustration not reproduced.]*

**Notes.** This is a TTP: place Layer 7 directly under the flap of the rucksack for easy access and storage during long halts while on movements. This takes less than 1 minute to perform. Leave the hood unrolled to quickly cover the head and hold in body heat. This also serves to keep snow from entering clothing. When cold, put it on and do 5 to10 minutes of full-body exercise. The inside pockets can hold bottles of hot water to aid in retaining heat. Camouflage overwhites are added over the top of the outermost shell, as conditions warrant.

### Hand Wear

2-31. Gloves are better for activities that require dexterity. (See figure 2-12.) Soldiers should always wear contact gloves. This is a very thin liner glove that shields the hands from direct contact with cold objects. Mittens can then be worn on top of the contact glove. Be aware that snow sticks to wool gloves and melts when hands are placed back into the mittens.

2-32. If activities call for handling snow or ice, use a shell or the intermediate cold-weather glove, and brush off all snow that sticks to the glove. Do not wear regular gloves or mittens for fueling operations. Fuel takes on the ambient temperature, so if a Soldier is splashed by fuel, frostbite can be the result. To protect the hands, Soldiers should wear fuel handler’s gloves.

*[Figure 2-12. Arctic mitten and intermediate cold-weather gloves — illustration not reproduced.]*

**Note**. This is a TTP: if hands are getting cold, bend at the waist and flap both arms as if trying to fly. The pressure of blood being forced into the fingertips can be felt. Another method is to vigorously rotate arms at the shoulder or rotate the body at the hip. The idea is to force blood back into the fingertips.

## Camouflage System

2-33. Camouflage in a cold-weather environment uses techniques appropriate for different snow-covered terrain. All Arctic Soldiers are issued an overwhite uniform. Simply putting these items on as the weather turns cold does not constitute good camouflage technique. Some thought needs to go into blending into the surroundings. Changes may be frequent to match the changing terrain.

**Note**. Camouflage paint is not worn when temperatures are below 32ºF (about 5.7ºC). The main reason is that it becomes impossible to monitor Soldiers for cold-weather injuries such as frostbite when skin camouflage is worn.

- These are some of the combinations that are typically used in snow-covered terrain:
  - In thickly wooded areas, wear the Universal Camouflage Pattern/Operational Camouflage Pattern (UCP/OCP) pattern uniform or UCP/OCP pattern with white trousers.
  - In low brush or light scrub areas, wear the overwhite parka top with UCP/OCP pattern trousers.
  - In forested areas, wear the UCP/OCP pattern top with overwhite bottoms.
  - When above the tree line in open areas, wear full camouflage with all parts of the overwhite camouflage.
- Additional considerations:
  - Avoid routes requiring numerous changes in the camouflage pattern.
  - Have camouflage garments handy so changes in pattern can be made fast and on the move.
  - In thickly wooded areas, use the UCP/OCP ECWCS parka and the overwhite trousers. (See figure 2-13.)

2-34. Like anything else, there are exceptions. For example, after a heavy snowfall, complete overwhites may be appropriate for any of the above listed terrain.

*[Figure 2-13. Camouflage in a thickly wooded area — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-35. When in approximately waist-high vegetation, use the overwhite helmet cover, parka, and mitten covers. The fighting load carrier (FLC) is broken up with white engineer tape. (See figure 2-14.)

*[Figure 2-14. Camouflage for low brush or light scrub areas — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-36. On trails or road systems, Soldiers wear overwhite trousers, ECWCS parka, and the standard helmet cover. (See figure 2-15.)

*[Figure 2-15. Mixed camouflage for roads and trails — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-37. The entire overwhite system is used in open areas or when operating above the tree line. (See figure 2-16.)

*[Figure 2-16. Snow camouflage above the tree line or in open areas — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-38. Different materials can be used for camouflage in a cold-weather environment. Tape is good but can become brittle and fall off. In this case, use cloth tape. Other resources include—

- Old overwhites or cloth.
- Spray paint (with the unit commanders’ approval).
- Engineer tape.

2-39. Use as much white as necessary to make a pattern that blends in with the surrounding snow cover. Snow-covered terrain is rarely solid white. Leaving some black exposed on the weapon blends better than completely covering it with white.

**Note**. Take care not to interfere with moving parts, or normal equipment and weapons operations.

## Clothing Wear and Maintenance

2-40. Hang clothing separately from the drying lines inside the tent. Do not place anything too close to a stove and do not hang anything directly above the Space Heater Arctic (SHA) in the tent. If it falls off the line and lands on the heater it can cause a tent fire. Do not place wet clothing in sleeping bags. This only makes the bags wet (which is harder to dry than clothing). Small items can be placed between the sleeping bag and the sleeping mat under the legs. larger items can be allowed to freeze and then Soldiers can beat the ice out of them, which is very effective.

2-41. Use safety pins to attach damp socks or glove liners between the base layer and insulation layer. An ideal time for this is during the rest cycle. Clothing should be kept clean for it to function properly. The unit supply system can coordinate for bulk laundry, but this is time and resource intensive. For small items such as socks, turn a 5.56-milimeter ammunition can into a laundry machine.

2-42. This method also works for base layers, but the layers should be done one item at a time. Water can be from a creek or lake. Do not use the drinking water. To make a laundry machine—

- Place no more than five pairs of socks into the can.
- Fill with water and a couple of shavings from the bar of soap at the hand washing station.
- Place on top of the SHA for a few minutes to make the water warm to the touch.
- Close the lid of the can and shake vigorously for 5 minutes.
- Dump the water in the latrine area and squeeze the socks out.
- Repeat twice with rinse water.
- Hang to dry.

2-43. For shell layers, grasp two sides and rub them together vigorously to remove mud or dirt. This is called dry rubbing.

## Cold-Weather Equipment Modular Sleep System

2-44. The Modular Sleep System and other components are designed to provide comfort in temperatures ranging from 50°F to –30°F (10°C to –34.4°C) when wearing various layers of extreme cold-weather clothing. This is a sized item, regular and long length. Individuals should ensure they check their system for fit. If the bag is too small or too big, the cold becomes more noticeable. The four-part modular sleep system (NSN 8645-01-547-2757) includes the—

- Patrol bag.
- Intermediate bag.
- Waterproof bivouac (bivvy) cover.
- Large stuff sack.

### Parts of the Modular Sleep System

2-45. The patrol bag is designed for use in temperate climates ranging from 30° to 50°F (–1°C to –15°C). The intermediate bag is designed for use in cold climates ranging from –10° to +30°F (–10°C to –1°C). The waterproof bivouac cover is the shell that protects the other layers and blocks wind.

2-46. When combined with the bivvy cover as the outermost layer, the system is designed for temperatures down to –20°F (–28.8°C) with the user wearing the Level I lightweight undershirt and drawers or Level II mid-weight shirt and drawers, both with standard cushion sole socks. It protects to –30°F (–34.4°C) when the user is also wearing the Level III fleece jacket and Level V soft shell trousers. These ratings are approximate and depend on the age and maintenance of the bags, along with the user’s tolerance to cold. Some people sleep warmer than others.

2-47. The large compression stuff sack is constructed with water-resistant materials and enough adequate volume for all three layers. This sack compresses the system into one cubic foot. (See figure 2-17.)

*[Figure 2-17. Modular Sleep System — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Staying Warm

2-48. To stay warm, keep the bag dry. Sleeping bags become wet from outside moisture, water vapor, and human sweat.

2-49. Use the insulated sleeping pad under the sleeping bag for warmth and comfort, and to keep the sleeping bag dry. If the sleeping pad is unavailable, place insulation between the bag and ground.

### Temperature in the Tent and Water Vapor

2-50. To avoid outside moisture such as rain and snow, or even an overly heated tent that creates mud, keep the tent fairly cool inside. If the snow is melting, reduce the heat from the heater system, spread a poncho on the ground under the mat, and use the bivvy cover to keep snow off the sleeping bag. Ensure boot soles are clean and free from snow before putting them inside the bag to keep them from freezing. Water bottles can also be put into the bag to prevent freezing but make sure the caps are secure.

2-51. When in the field, water vapor happens continuously throughout time and is difficult to avoid since humans produce moist heat over their bodies just by being alive. At least once a week, hang sleeping bags from the center pole of the tent to allow them to dry out. Separate all the layers individually to ensure thorough drying. Faces should not be placed inside the bag for warmth as this increases the amount of water vapor that condenses in the bag. Even the outside of the bag gets wet just from breathing. Allow water vapor to freeze, then dry rub or brush it off. There may also be frost on the underside of the bivvy cover. Brush this off before stowing the bag. Avoid wearing too many clothes to prevent sweating.

### Transporting the Modular Sleep System

2-52. Always carry the sleeping bag in the issued carrying bag. This protects the bag and helps keep it dry. Ensure that the top of the bag is securely tied to prevent snow or water from getting inside and making the sleeping bag wet. It should not be strapped to the top of the rucksack, if possible. This exposes the bag to the elements and possible damage.

**Notes**. This is a TTP: eat just before getting into the sleeping bag. This generates energy and helps Soldiers stay warm. Soldiers should relieve themselves before going to bed. The act of getting out of a warm sleeping bag and going outside can be a chilling experience. To keep feet warm, zip the Level VII parka closed and slide it over the feet before getting into the sleeping bag. (See figure 2-18.) Immediately put the warm parka on after exiting the sleeping bag.

*[Figure 2-18. Use the Level VII parka to keep feet warm — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Packing the Rucksack

2-53. Properly packing the fighting load and approach load should follow the A-B-C’S principle:

- Access (to mission critical items).
- Balance.
- Compress.
- Streamline.

### Access and Balance

2-54. Mission critical items (ammunition, medical kits, and other items) should be readily retrievable at any given time. The location of these items is standard across the unit so they can be found in a hurry by anyone, anywhere. Keep similar items together. For example, several Soldiers carrying crew-served weapon parts should stay within close proximity to each other. Material for rope installations (artificial anchors, webbing, rope, and other items) should be carried in close proximity to the team that sets up the installation. Consumables such as water, batteries, and other needed articles should come out of the pack first, instead of the FLC. This way if a Soldier gets separated from their rucksack, resources are still available. In extreme cold, the heaviest insulation layer should be on top of the rucksack to quickly put on if needed during halts.

2-55. A rucksack that is lopsided and ill-fitting is misery. Soldiers must properly fit the rucksack to their bodies. (Refer to TM 10-8465-236-10 for more information.) Ensure that the body armor, FLC, and rucksack all work together. Remember that ounces make pounds and pounds make pain. Police Soldiers to reduce nonessential and redundant equipment. Strive to use multifunctional equipment as much as possible. Rations can be field stripped, and packaging removed from items. However, waterproofing is absolutely necessary. A drenching rain can more than double the weight of equipment very quickly. Do not load the sustainment pouches in lieu of the main compartment as they can flop around. Consider using the daypack or consolidating loads between two or more Soldiers.

### Compress and Streamline

2-56. Utilize the compression straps to keep the internal load from shifting. This also keeps the load closer to the body. Carry the load on the hips and legs, not the shoulders. Tighten the waist strap to a very snug fit just above the bony portion of the hips. The rucksack should remain in place on the pelvic area without any help from the shoulder straps. The straps should be tightened enough to keep the pack snug against the body.

2-57. Hanging equipment on the outside of the rucksack is a very poor practice. Heavier items can be felt swinging with each step. Items can snag on brush, make excessive noise, and possibly break or be lost in a tumble. When rock climbing, they get caught on nubbins and in cracks, causing a loss of balance or a fall. In snow-covered areas, a sled can be used to transport this equipment.

2-58. Items such as hydration bladders and assault packs act like a lever, increasing the perceived weight. Place these directly on top or inside, close to the body. Mortar tubes, man-portable air defense system (MANPADS), and other items may be better carried by hand with a sling, especially in heavy brush. Equipment such as a machine gun tripod or ice axe is usually carried on the outside of the pack. Attach them in a secure manner where they are least likely to create problems while on the move.

2-59. When loading the rucksack for skiing or climbing, pack heavier items toward the top and next to the frame. This places the weight on the hips, which is necessary for good balance. Place hard or sharp objects inside the load where they do not rub on the bag and the back. Articles that are often needed are placed in the outside pockets for easy reach. (See figure 2-19.)

*[Figure 2-19. Properly packed rucksack — illustration not reproduced.]*

2-60. Adjust the straps so that the top part of the body can move and arms swing freely. Adjust the shoulder straps so that the lower back straps fit just above the belt line.

## Load-Carrying Equipment

2-61. The modular lightweight load-carrying equipment (MOLLE) rucksack is used to carry the approach load and any additional items for the fighting load. (See figure 2-20.) In cold-weather operations, follow the packing list set by the unit SOP when organizing the rucksack. The main consideration is to avoid unpacking during halts to retrieve frequently needed items. The following illustrates a method of organizing the rucksack:

- Detachable sustainment pouches contain rations to be eaten for morning and evening meals, and—
  - Pack extra socks and a spare hat.
  - The openings behind the pockets can be used for skis or other items.
- External attachment points are used to secure additional pouches (such as the entrenching tool) to the side of the pack.
- The top flap contains camouflage overwhites and the pack cover:
  - Stow the Layer 7 parka directly under the top flap, allowing Soldiers at a halt to reach under the flap and don the parka directly over the FLC; they do not get cold and can immediately restow as movement begins.
  - These actions should take less than a minute.
- In the main compartment, place the sleeping bag at the bottom of the rucksack:
  - Spare clothes are placed around the sleeping bag to completely fill spaces; empty voids cause loads to shift.
  - The top section of the pack should be used for rations and mission items such as combat lifesaver bags.
  - Put small arms ammunition in the radio compartment separate from other items, so it can be found in a hurry and is not subject to falling into spaces.
- When wearing the overwhites, a white cotton cover is provided to camouflage the entire rucksack, excluding the shoulder straps.
- Check the rucksack for damage before and after an operation:
  - Repair any damage immediately or turn the rucksack in for a replacement through supply channels.
  - Ensure the rucksack fits properly before going to the field.
- When quick removal of the rucksack is needed, unsnap the fastener, pull upward on the quick-release fasteners on the shoulder straps, and let it drop to the ground.
- To attach skis to the side of the pack, slide the ski behind the sustainment pouches and secure the shovels with cord or rubber bands to keep them steady.
- Remember that with skis attached, the Solider is now taller and the skis can hit things overhead.

## Moving Equipment over Snow

2-64. Before being instructed to use snowshoes or skis, the chain of command first determines if movement by foot is possible. In shallow snow (8 inches [20.3 cm] or less) or heavily crusted snow, movement on foot might provide better maneuverability with the least effort. In deeper snow, snowshoe or ski flotation is just as important as weapons and clothing.

### Snowshoes

2-65. The military snowshoe is light and durable, providing the needed flotation to travel over snow. Approximately 22 inches (55.8 cm) long and 8 inches (20.3 cm) wide (without flotation tails) and weighing less than 4 pounds (1.8 kilograms), military snowshoes are made with a hard plastic deck, and steel crampons and bars to aid traction. (See figure 2-23.)

2-66. After traversing a hard-packed area, it is recommended to keep the tails on to prevent unexpected falls into deep snow without the adequate flotation to stay upright. The addition of ski poles helps a Soldier maintain balance, aid with propulsion, and can be used as a weapon rest when engaging targets.

*[Figure 2-23. Snowshoe binding attached to the boot — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Skis

2-67. Current standard military skis come in three lengths. The appropriate length for each Soldier is determined by individual body weight. Soldiers weighing less than 165 pounds use the 180 cm (5 feet, 9 inch) ski. Those between 165 and 200 pounds (74.6 and 90.7 kilograms) use the 190 cm (6 feet, 2 inch) ski, and Soldiers over 200 pounds use the 200 cm (6 feet, 5 inch) ski. These skis have a hole in the tip for attaching a lanyard to drag behind when in an assaulting a position. Skis can also be used to construct an expedient litter. The metal edges along the sides of the skis help grip the snow when turning.

2-68. A groove in the tail allows climbing skins to be attached. Climbing skins are detachable lengths of cloth with directional plush. They attach to the base of the ski with a loop on the tip and a hook on the tail. The center is held in place with renewable glue. These skins provide traction for ascension up very steep slopes or when pulling heavy loads.

2-69. Ski poles are extremely useful in steep terrain, providing balance and aiding propulsion. They can also be used as a weapon rest when engaging targets. The length of the pole is adjustable. Usually, adjusting the ski pole to the height of the name tape on the shirt is a good length. Ski poles should also be used with snowshoes.

## Miscellaneous Equipment

2-70. Sunglasses and canteens are essential items every Soldier should carry with their cold-weather gear. Sunglasses and goggles prevent serious eye injury, canteens prevent dehydration, and hydration bladders can help warm sleeping bags.

### Sunglasses

2-71. Polarized ultraviolet-blocking sunglasses are always worn when the ground is covered with snow, especially on bright days. Failure to wear sunglasses, even on overcast days, may result in snow blindness. This is very painful and can cause incapacitation for up to 48 hours.

2-72. Should this situation occur, a fellow Soldier is assigned to help the injured Soldier with the simplest of tasks. Goggles are double paned. The air space between the two lenses prevents fogging. Goggles with mirrored finishes reflect the sun, giving away the position of the Soldier.

### Canteens

2-73. In cold weather, do not fill the canteen over two-thirds full to allow for expansion if the water freezes. Fill the canteen with hot water, if available, and place it upside down in its carrier so the cap does not freeze shut. This can be comfortably carried inside the insulation layers to keep water potable.

2-74. Hydration bladders also freeze if left outside but can be placed inside insulation layers to keep the water thawed. Any water container placed in the sleeping bag is checked for leaks, tight filler caps, and valves.
