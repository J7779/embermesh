# Eating, Drinking and Staying Healthy in the Cold

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 4. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Eating and Drinking in a Cold Environment

4-1. Most of what humans eat and drink is used to maintain body heat, while only a small portion is used to produce energy for physical work. Adequate caloric intake in cold-weather operations is essential. About 4,600 calories per day are necessary for Soldier’s performing physically demanding work in this type of environment. Efficiency drops rapidly if this level is not maintained.

4-2. The body loses liquid quickly in extremely cold conditions, regardless of how carefully clothing is adjusted and ventilated. The exertion of moving on foot, preparing bivouac sites and defenses in the snow, and other manual tasks, take a toll with sweat and loss of moisture due to breathing. These liquids are regularly replaced, preferably by hot drinks that provide extra calories when they contain sugar.

### Rations

4-3. The standard operational ration is the meal, ready to eat (MRE). One ration provides about 1,300 calories. Four MREs per day are needed to meet the required calories. The downside to this ration is that most of its components freeze.

4-4. The meal, cold weather (MCW) has many pocket items that should be readily accessible throughout the day. This ration provides about 1,450 calories per bag. The modular operational ration enhancement contains all pocket items to supplement additional calories throughout the day. Each packet provides 1,100 calories. The unitized group ration series of rations has an Arctic supplement component that provides an additional 914 calories. The MCW is a restricted ration meant for short durations. (More information can be found in AR 40-25.)

### Liquids

4-5. Insensible and sensible fluid loss refers to two types of ways the body loses liquid through various bodily functions. Insensible fluid loss cannot be perceived or measured directly. Sensible loss can be perceived and measured.

### Insensible and Sensible Fluid Loss

4-6. Insensible fluid loss is evaporation by breathing and perspiration. This is continuous and can account for approximately 1.5 to 2.25 quarts (approximately 1.4 to 2.1 liters) of moisture loss per day. The human body is constantly giving off moisture through the skin. To illustrate this on a small scale, tape a clear plastic bag to the hand. The cloudiness that forms is moisture. That loss happens over the entire skin area.

4-7. Sensible fluid loss is what can be seen leaving the body, such as urine or sweat. This loss varies based on temperature, humidity, and physical exertion. Soldiers need approximately 3.75 to 5.25 quarts (approximately 3.5 to 5 liters) of water per day. This includes the water present in rations.

### Maintaining Rations

4-8. One MRE has close to 8 ounces (approximately .25 liters) of liquid per ration. One MCW has close to zero water in it because MCWs are freeze-dried and require a little more than 1 quart (approximately 1 liter) of water to reconstitute the entrée and beverages. Assuming each Soldier consumes four MCWs a day, one squad needs at least 8 gallons (approximately 30.3 liters) of water per day. At the high end, it is almost 12 gallons (approximately 45.5 liters) per squad. To visualize this, a company needs one high mobility multipurpose wheeled vehicle for carrying water cans.

### Maintaining Adequate Water Supplies

4-9. While liquid water from a creek is always the best option, gather snow or ice chunks to make water when necessary. Ice produces more water in less time than snow. When melting snow, first put a small amount into the cooking pot and melt it, then more can be added. Continue this process until there is enough water. It is purified by bringing it to a rolling boil. When heating water, use all available stoves since this is a very time-consuming operation. Areas designated for ice or snow that are to be used for water are situated far upwind and uphill of the latrine and garbage disposal sites.

4-10. Before going to bed, ensure that stoves are filled with fuel and enough water is prepared for melting. The fire watch should have the additional duty of continuously melting snow or ice to ensure the squad has water for the next day. After compiling enough snow, ice, or water—

- Place the two big pots from the squad cook sets on the SHA full of snow and ice.
- Allow the snow or ice to melt (or at least become slushy).
- Transfer these pots to the squad stoves to boil while the two small pots are used to produce the initial melt on the SHA.

**Note**. The two initial pots of boiling water are primarily used to sanitize the pots. The water can be used for hand washing but do not drink it.

- Keep rotating the pots between the SHA and squad stoves until enough water is made.
- Provide everyone with a hot drink and hot food (a major priority) by:
  - Filling canteens on the FLC before filling the 5-gallon water can in the ahkio.
  - This can remain in the tent until it is time to pack and move.
  - If possible, the water should be hot to prevent freezing.

4-11. Keep Soldiers full by continually consuming and replacing water. Pockets are used for storing grazing food that is also eaten continuously throughout the day. In addition to checking for cold-weather injury and overall health, leaders check the full status of their Soldiers. Additional considerations are—

- Hot food is easier to swallow than cold food.
- Hot beverages are morale builders and are used to increase fluid intake.
- Utilize insulated bottles.
- If using MREs, carry the next meal entrée against the body to thaw.
- Calorie intake should be constant:
  - Military rations are designed to be portable.
  - MREs, MCWs, and modular operational ration enhancements are primarily pocket food requiring little preparation.
- Try for unitized group rations, if the mission allows.
- Bulk water should be transported hot and in a heated truck, with the water buffalo ideally kept in a heated shelter.
- Bartering with unwanted consumable items is better than tossing them in the trash.
- Designate a squad urine tree and check it daily.
- Inspect squad trash bags to ensure rations are being consumed.
- Use squad stoves with common fuels such as JP-8 or diesel.

**Note**. National stock numbers for recommended supplies are: Modular Operational Ration Enhancement, NSN 8970-01-581-2505 (Cold/Altitude) and Arctic Supplement for Unitized Group Ration, NSN 8970-01-470-5075.

## Cleanliness

4-12. Special attention to hygiene is especially needed in cold climates. Cleanliness in the field is particularly important. It is easy to go for long periods of time without bathing, but to be part of an effective combat force, keep clean.

4-13. Face, hands, armpits, and crotch should be washed daily. Remember that alcohol-based hand sanitizer takes on the ambient air temperature and tends to dry out skin. Shave regularly, daily if water is available, or every 2 to 3 days if water is in short supply. Shaving just before the rest cycle allows maximum recovery time before going out into the cold, reducing the possibility of superficial frostbite. Teeth should be cleaned daily. If a toothbrush is not available, a clean piece of cloth wrapped around a finger, or a chewed twig can be used as a substitute.

4-14. Base layers should be changed at least twice a week. If washing is not possible, crumple it, shake it, and air it for at least 2 hours. Also, ensure that all eating and cooking utensils are cleaned after every use.

### Trench Foot or Immersion Foot

4-15. Trench foot and immersion foot are injuries caused by prolonged exposure of skin in cold or wet conditions. In extreme cases, the skin dies, and amputation of the foot or leg may be necessary. The following helps prevent trench foot or immersion foot:

- Change into dry socks at least twice daily.
- Dry and massage feet regularly with foot powder.
- Wear VB boots.
- Weari dry leather boots whenever possible, if worn.

4-16. In the early stages of trench or immersion foot, feet and toes are cold, numb, and difficult to move. Walking becomes challenging, feet swell and become painful, and skin appears wrinkled (like a prune). In the early stages, keep feet dry, clean, and exposed to the air. In later stages, evacuate the victim to a medical facility.

### Constipation and Diarrhea

4-17. At very cold temperatures, people tend to put off the natural urge to have a bowel movement, which can easily result in constipation. Some common symptoms are stomach cramps, dizziness, and headaches. If symptoms persist, get medical help. The following helps prevent constipation:

- Eating regularly, and including fresh or canned fruit.
- Drinking plenty of liquids.

4-18. Diarrhea is another problem that can become serious. If sickness does occur, seek medical attention and redouble efforts to keep it from spreading to others via hand-to-hand contact. Ways of preventing diarrhea include—

- Washing hands frequently.
- Ensuring food and water originate from sanitary sources.

## Preventive Health

4-19. When in the field, access to comprehensive medical care can be quite distant from a Soldier’s location. Knowledge of preventive measures is especially helpful.

4-20. Should a medical situation arise, knowing the steps to take for immediate relief is important for the well-being of Soldiers. Potential physical problems prevalent to a cold-weather environment include snow blindness, sunburn, tent eye, heat cramps and heat exhaustion, hypothermia, frostbite, dehydration, and carbon monoxide poisoning. These subjects are addressed in the following paragraphs.

### Snow Blindness

4-21. Snow blindness is caused by the ultraviolet and ultra blue rays of the sun reflecting from a snow-covered surface into the eyes. This condition occurs even in cloudy conditions. Oddly, it is more likely to occur in hazy, cloudy conditions than in sunny conditions.

4-22. Sun blindness prevention is easy: wear the issued sunglasses. (See figure 4-1 on page 50.) If sunglasses are not available, make glasses with slits from cardboard, thin wood, tree bark, or similar materials. Common symptoms of snow blindness include—

- A scratchy, sandy feeling under the eyelids.
- Red and watering eyes.
- Headache.

4-23. Adhere to the following for proper treatment:

- Blindfold the Soldier using a dark cloth.
- Reassure the patient.
- Evacuate the casualty to a medical treatment facility.

### Sunburn and Tent Eye

4-24. Sunburn is a serious hazard due to the reflective qualities of snow, especially at higher elevations. Require Soldiers to use sunscreen and lip balm to prevent sunburn.

4-25. A major symptom of sunburn is skin redness with slight swelling. Prolonged exposure to the sun may cause pain and blistering. In severe cases, chills, fever, and headaches occur. Soothing skin creams may be helpful if the swelling is not severe.

4-26. Tent eye is an eye inflammation caused by fumes from stoves and lanterns used in a poorly ventilated shelter. It is prevented by providing proper ventilation, and the treatment is fresh air.

### Heat Cramps and Heat Exhaustion

4-27. During cold-weather training, it is possible to experience heat cramps or other injuries typically associated with hot climates. Heat cramps and heat exhaustion are two medical issues that can occur due to sustained hard physical exertion such as breaking a trail.

### Heat Cramps

4-28. Heat cramps are caused by an excessive loss of salt in the body. The loss of salt does not allow the muscles to relax, causing spasms. After prolonged physical activity in a wet-cold environment, the following signs and symptoms of heat cramps could appear in the arms, legs, or stomach:

- Painful muscle cramps and spasms, usually in the legs and abdomen.
- Heavy sweating.

4-29. To treat heat cramps, do the following:

- First, have the Soldier drink one-quarter of a canteen filled with water.
- Second, have the Soldier drink a canteen of water mixed with one packet of salt over a 30-minute period.
- Third, if muscle cramping continues, evacuate the Soldier to the nearest medical treatment facility.
- Apply firm pressure on cramping muscles or gently massage to relieve the spasm.

**Note**. Do not give saltwater to a Soldier who has an upset stomach. In this situation, only provide a canteen of fresh water.

### Heat Exhaustion

4-30. It is also possible to experience heat exhaustion in a wet-cold-weather environment when undergoing extreme physical exertion. Dehydration leads to heat exhaustion. Heat exhaustion is caused by not consuming enough water. Symptoms include—

- Increased heart rate.
- Increased respiratory rate.
- Headache.
- Dizziness.
- Nausea and vomiting.
- Thirst.
- Fatigue.
- Profuse sweating with cool, clammy skin.

4-31. The following is a recommended treatment for heat exhaustion when in a cold-weather environment:

- Have the Soldier drink one canteen of water over the course of 30 to 45 minutes.
- If cramps occur, give the Soldier a canteen of water with one packet of salt mixed in and instructions to drink the salt solution over a 30-minute period.
- Ventilate excess heat but do not allow the Soldier to become chilled.
- Loosen all tight-fitting clothes.
- Elevate feet above the level of the heart.
- Intravenous fluids may be necessary and should be administered in a warm shelter.
- If the Soldier is unable to drink water due to an upset stomach or if the symptoms have not improved within 20 minutes, evacuate the Soldier to the nearest medical treatment facility.

## The Effects of Wind Chill

4-32. Wind chill is the combination of wind and low temperatures creating a condition known as wind chill. For example, with calm wind and a temperature of –20°F (–28.8°C) there is little danger of wind chill. If the temperature is –20°F (–28.8°C) and wind is blowing at 20 miles per hour (mph) (32.1 kilometers per hour [kph]), the equivalent chill temperature is –63°F (–52.7°C). Under these conditions there is increased danger. Exposed skin can freeze within 6 minutes. Wind is also created when walking, running, or skiing. Walking creates a wind speed of about 5 mph (8 kph) and running 10 mph (16 kph). Wind is also created by moving vehicles, aircraft propellers, and rotors.

4-33. The wind-chill chart (see table 4-1 on page 52) shows how wind and temperature combine to create wind chill. Cold injury can disable a Soldier like an enemy bullet. To use the wind chill chart, get the ambient air temperature, estimate the wind speed, and then multiply by 1.5. This is necessary to make the true wind speed at 33 feet (10 meters), the world meteorological standard for measuring wind speed. Find these numbers on the chart, and where they intersect is the wind chill.

### Hypothermia

4-34. Hypothermia occurs when the core body temperature falls below 95ºF (35ºC). Hypothermia is characterized as mild, moderate, or severe. Mild hypothermia occurs when the core body temperature is between 90° (32.2ºC) and 95ºF (35ºC). Moderate hypothermia occurs at a core body temperature of 80° to 89ºF (26.6° to 31.6ºC). Severe hypothermia exists when the core body temperature falls below 80ºF (26.6°C). Rectal temperature measurement is the only way to determine an accurate core body temperature. As it is unlikely this method can safely be used in the field, obvious signs and symptoms are used to make a diagnosis. All levels of hypothermia are potentially life-threatening health emergencies requiring immediate care in a medical facility.

### Levels of Hypothermia

4-35. As the core body temperature begins to fall, shivering becomes the most noticeable symptom. Shivering alone is not a sign of hypothermia, but it does indicate that the body is having a problem with the cold. Mild hypothermia symptoms include—

- Shivering.
- Lack of sound judgment; confusion, apathy, and mild stupidity.
- Pale, cool skin.
- Increased heart and respiratory rates.
- The umbles: fumbles, stumbles, tumbles, grumbles, mumbles:
  - Fine motor skills decrease (fumbling with tasks that require manual dexterity such as manipulating a rifle).
  - Next, the gross motor skills start slipping (stumbling when just walking).
  - Finally, intellectual and personality changes occur, including grumbling and incoherent mumbling.

4-36. Moderate hypothermia symptoms include—

- Uncontrollable shivering.
- Worsening the umbles.
- Increased confusion.
- Increased heart and respiratory rates.
- Cold and pale skin.

4-37. Severe hypothermia symptoms include—

- Cessation of shivering.
- Muscle rigidity.
- Stupor progressing to unconsciousness.
- Slow or nonpalpable pulse and respiration.
- Cold, bluish skin.

**Notes**. Perform first aid for mild and moderate hypothermia. All cases of hypothermia are evacuated to a medical facility for treatment.

### Treating Hypothermia Victims Prior to Evacuation

4-38. If available, use the Hypothermia Prevention & Management Kit (NSN 6515-01-532-8056). If not, a hypothermia wrap can be made with commonly available items. Follow these procedures:

- Change the environment the casualty is in from cold and wet to warm and dry.
- Replace damp clothing with dry clothing.
- Add a windproof or waterproof layer and/or place the Soldier in a shelter.
- Add extra insulation under and around the casualty.
- Provide food and warm liquids.
- Exercise mildly hypothermic patients.
- In the following order, package a moderately hypothermic casualty in a hypothermia wrap:
  - Place a poncho flat on the ground.
  - Lay an insulating pad on top of the poncho.
  - Place a closed sleeping bag on top of the insulating pad.
  - Add an open sleeping bag on top of the first one.
  - Put the patient inside with a hot water bottle to the chest area (do not place it directly against the skin) and zip the sleeping bag closed.
  - Place a third, open sleeping bag on top of the second.
  - Fold the poncho around the patient (like a burrito).
- Perform first aid for severe hypothermia:
  - Handle with care because rough treatment can cause the casualty’s heart to stop.
  - Contact medical personnel immediately.
  - Use supplemental oxygen (if available) or begin rescue breathing if breathing has stopped or is barely detectable.
  - Breathe for the patient for 3 to15 minutes before moving or beginning evacuation.
  - Change the environment the Soldier is in from cold and wet to warm and dry.
  - Carefully remove damp and wet clothing.
  - Package in a hypothermia wrap.
  - Evacuate using the gentlest means available.

### Frostbite Symptoms and Treatment

4-39. Frostbite results when tissues freeze from exposure to temperatures below 32°F (5.68°C). The severity of frostbite injury depends upon the wind-chill factor, length of exposure time, and proper protection. Soldiers with a history of cold injury are more prone to frostbite. There may be a tingling, stinging, aching sensation, or a cramping pain. The skin first turns red and then becomes pale gray or waxy white. Frostbite classification ranges from superficial to deep.

### Preventing Frostbite

4-40. When each Soldier takes care of themselves, it is possible to prevent frostbite. It seldom occurs to people maintaining proper body heat and is commonly associated with overall body heat loss. This is the result of poor equipment, reduced food intake, dehydration (water loss), overtiring, injury, or a combination of factors. Feet, hands, ears, and exposed areas of the face are the most easily hurt by frostbite and need constant care. The following rules help prevent frostbite:

- Do not wear tight boots and socks.
- Use the buddy system to check exposed areas, especially when the wind is blowing.
- Carry extra socks and mitten liners.
- Do not stay still for long periods of time.
- Use caution when cold and wind are combined.
- Check feet during halts.
- Consume good food and hot drinks as often as possible.

### Levels of Frostbite

4-41. Superficial frostbite appears as white, waxy, and pale in lighter skin types: red and pale in darker skin types. There is numbness with the skin moving over the underlying tissue. When dented, the skin rebounds.

4-42. Deep frostbite skin discoloration presents in much the same manner as superficial frostbite. There is no tactile sensation at all. The Soldier might describe it as feeling wooden. Skin does not move over the underlying tissue and when dented, does not rebound. Skin can also feel hard to the touch. After rewarming, a layer of dark-colored skin may form over the affected area.

### Recommended Treatment for Frostbite

4-43. If the exposure time in the cold is short, the frostbite is probably superficial. If the exposure time is long, the frostbite is probably deep. The following is a recommended treatment for frostbite:

- Move the casualty to a warm and sheltered area.
- Do not rub the frostbitten area with snow or apply cold water soaks.
- Do not rewarm the affected area by massaging or exposing it to open fire.
- Rewarm the face, nose, or ears by placing hands on the frozen area.
- Rewarm frostbitten hands by placing them under clothing and against the body.
- Close the clothing to prevent further loss of body heat.
- Rewarm feet by removing boots and socks, then:
  - Place bare feet under clothing and against the abdomen of a buddy.
  - Once the feet are warmed, put on dry socks and boots, if available.
  - If the victim must wear wet socks and boots, exercise the feet by wiggling the toes.
- Loosen tight clothing and remove jewelry.
- Improve circulation by exercising.
- Do not allow the victim to use alcohol or tobacco:
  - Alcohol increases the loss of body heat.
  - Tobacco causes blood vessels to narrow in the arms and legs.
- Reassure the victim.
- Protect frozen tissue from further cold or trauma.
- Evacuate the casualty to the nearest medical treatment facility.

4-44. Deep frostbite is most common and harmful to the feet and less common to the hands and ears. When deep frostbite does occur, the Soldier should be moved to a sheltered area and evacuated to a medical treatment facility immediately. If possible, do not allow the victim to walk if the feet are frozen, and avoid treating or thawing the affected area. Thawing deep frostbite in the field increases pain and invites infection, greater damage, and possibly gangrene. Do not try to thaw deep frostbite; let the physician do it.

### Dehydration

4-45. Dehydration can cause serious physical problems and make Soldiers more susceptible to other difficulties such as frostbite and hypothermia. The following are suggestions to prevent dehydration:

- The minimum daily liquid requirement for Soldiers performing demanding physical work in the cold is approximately 3.75 quarts (3.5 liters) per day.
- By the time a Soldier feels thirsty, they are already dehydrated and should drink water whenever possible, particularly during halts.
- Ensure that canteens are full before any type of movement and fill with hot water, if possible.
- During periods when water is scarce, do not eat snow as it can lower body temperature and the air it contains can cause stomach aches:
  - Diuretics such as coffee should be avoided because it passes through the body very quickly.
  - Water should be the first choice.
- Check urine in the snow for dark yellow or brown spots that indicate dehydration.

4-46. Common symptoms of dehydration are—

- Red skin indicating a severe condition needing immediate medical evacuation to the nearest medical treatment facility.
- Lack of appetite.
- The mouth, tongue, and throat are dry.
- Stomach cramps and/or vomiting.
- Headaches.

4-47. Recommended treatment for dehydration is—

- Keeping the victim warm.
- Giving plenty of liquids.
- Making the Soldier rest.

## Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

4-48. Whenever a squad stove fire, fuel-fired heater, or internal combustion engine is used indoors, there is danger of carbon monoxide poisoning. Fresh air in living and working quarters is vital. Carbon monoxide is a deadly, odorless gas that displaces oxygen in the blood stream. To prevent carbon monoxide poisoning—

- Ensure that stoves and lanterns are functioning properly.
- Do not let Soldiers warm themselves by engine exhaust.
- Always crack the windows in vehicles when a heater is in use, and do not allow anyone to sleep in a running vehicle.
- Use a tent guard or shut the stove off when sleeping.

4-49. If a Soldier is found unconscious in an enclosed shelter, carbon monoxide poisoning should be suspected. Common symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning are—

- Headache, dizziness, confusion, yawning, weariness, nausea, and ringing in the ears.
- Bright red color on lips and skin.
- Drowsiness and sudden collapse.

4-50. Adhere to the following for proper treatment of carbon monoxide poisoning:

- Move the Soldier to open air.
- Keep the victim still and warm.
- If the Soldier is not breathing, administer mouth-to-mouth resuscitation.
- Administer cardiopulmonary resuscitation, if necessary.
- Immediately evacuate the Soldier to the nearest medical treatment facility.
- Immediately administer oxygen, if available.
