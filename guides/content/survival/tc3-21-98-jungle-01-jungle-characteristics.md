# Jungle Characteristics: Climate, Jungle Types, Swamps and Terrain

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 1. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

The jungle presents a uniquely challenging environment for military operations due to its dense vegetation and complex terrain. This chapter introduces the diverse characteristics of jungles worldwide, including their regional variations and distinct types. This chapter examines key features, terrain complexities, and the plant and animal life found within these ecosystems. Understanding these characteristics is crucial for successful planning and execution of missions in jungle environments.

## Section I – Jungle Regions

1-1. Jungles are masses of tropical vegetation that are often tangled, overgrown, and impenetrable. Successful jungle operations require modifications to equipment and tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTP) as the environment can profoundly affect military operations. Primary, secondary, and savanna are the three main types of jungles based on the terrain and vegetation. Primary and secondary jungles can be further classified as tropical or deciduous based upon the predominate trees. Common land features in the jungle are swamps, savannas, and cultivated areas. The large amounts of annual precipitation support incredible plant and wildlife density and diversity as well as making jungles the most ecologically rich and vibrant environments on the planet. They occur most commonly in the tropical areas of the world, mainly Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America. (See figure 1-1 for locations of the major jungles.)

## Jungle Types

1-2. Jungles are classified by their vegetation structure and climate. The primary classifications are primary jungle, secondary jungle, and savanna. Primary and secondary jungles can be further categorized by their dominant foliage as either tropical rain forest or deciduous forest.

## Primary Jungle

1-3. Depending on the type of trees growing in the forests, primary jungles are classified either as tropical rain forests or as deciduous forests. Primary jungles consist largely of old growth trees undisturbed by logging, farming, or other human activities and have tall trees that form a dense canopy. As such, little light penetrates to the jungle floor and undergrowth may be relatively thin. A primary jungle has generally remained untouched by humans and has a thick canopy and clear floor enabling reasonably easy movement and visibility of around 20 meters. The canopy can be three-tiered causing a reduction in light and heat.

### Tropical Rain Forests

1-4. Tropical rain forests consist mostly of large trees whose branches spread and lock together to form canopies. These canopies, which can exist at two or three different levels, may form as low as 10 meters from the ground. The canopies prevent sunlight from reaching the ground, causing a lack of undergrowth on the jungle floor. Extensive above-ground root systems and hanging vines are common. These conditions, combined with a wet and soggy surface, make vehicular traffic difficult. Foot movement is easier in tropical rain forests than in other types of jungle. Except where felled trees create a gap in the canopy of the rain forest, observation from the air is nearly impossible. Ground observation is generally limited to 50 meters or less.

### Deciduous Forests

1-5. Deciduous forests are found in subtropical zones where both wet and dry seasons exist. In the wet season, trees are fully leafed; in the dry season, some of the foliage dies. Trees generally grow less densely in deciduous forests than in rain forests. This thinner canopy allows more rain and sunlight to filter to the ground, producing thick undergrowth. The deciduous jungles’ thick undergrowth tends to canalize foot movement due to the deliberate efforts required to break new trail. In the wet season with the trees in full leaf, observation both from the air and on the ground is severely limited, the full leaves impact human observation, optics, and detection from aviation assets.

## Secondary Jungle

1-6. Secondary jungles grow predominantly at the edge of tropical rain forests and deciduous forests and in areas where jungles have been cleared and abandoned. Secondary jungles exist in areas that have been previously cleared for farming or logging and are characterized by younger, shorter trees and thicker undergrowth. Secondary jungles appear when the ground has been repeatedly exposed to sunlight. These areas typically display heavy overgrowth with weeds, grasses, thorns, ferns, canes, and shrubs. Foot movement is extremely slow and difficult. Vegetation may reach a height of 6 feet which severely limits observation. A secondary jungle is generally hotter, especially when direct sunlight is not blocked due to the lack of a canopy.

## Savanna

1-7. Savannas are natural grasslands, and the few trees present are often grouped together in small groves or thickets. Savannas are generally located away from the equator but within the tropics. Savannas consist of broad, open grasslands with few trees. The thick grass has broad-blades and grows 1- to 5-meters high. Movement in the savanna is generally easier than in other types of jungle areas, especially for vehicles. However, the sharp-edged, dense grass and extreme heat make foot movement a slow and tiring process. Ground visibility varies depending on the height of the savanna grass. Concealment from air observation is poor for both Soldiers and vehicles.

## Section II – Jungle Features

1-8. Common jungle features include swamps, bamboo forests, and cultivated areas. Jungle features are diverse and depend on the specific jungle’s location and climate.

## Swamps

1-9. Swamps are forested wetlands. Common to all low jungle areas, swamps have lots of water and poor drainage. Swamps affect logistical considerations in littoral operations. There are two basic types of swamps—mangrove and palm. Swamps dramatically complicate logistical considerations in military littoral operations by severely degrading mobility, limiting infrastructure development, and demanding specialized equipment and sustainment methods. The unique characteristics of swamp environments force a departure from traditional logistics, requiring a greater emphasis on naval, air, and unconventional supply strategies.

### Mangrove Swamps

1-10. Mangrove swamps grow along coastal areas wherever tides influence water flow. The mangrove is a shrub-like tree, which grows 3- to 16-feet high. These trees have tangled root systems, both above and below the water level, which restrict movement to foot or small boats. Observation in mangrove swamps—both on the ground and from the air—is poor. Concealment is excellent. Swamps provide excellent concealment due to a combination of dense vegetation, murky water, and complex terrain. The thick growth of trees, shrubs, and aquatic plants offers visual cover from both air and ground, while the dark, unclear water can hide movement below the surface. The uneven ground, with its mix of land and water, further breaks up silhouettes and provides numerous natural hiding spots.

### Palm Swamps

1-11. Palm swamps exist in both salt and freshwater areas. Like movement in the mangrove swamps, movement through palm swamps is mostly restricted to foot (sometimes small boats). Vehicular traffic is nearly impossible except after extensive road construction by engineers. Observation and fields of fire are very limited. Concealment from both air and ground observation is excellent. Concealment from both air and ground observation in palm swamps is exceptionally effective due to a unique combination of a dense, multi-layered canopy and a complex, cluttered understory that obstructs sightlines from virtually every angle.

1-12. The large, broad fronds of palm trees are the primary reason for this. They create a thick, overlapping roof that is extremely difficult to penetrate visually from above, while the dense growth pattern at ground level provides horizontal cover.

## Bamboo Forests

1-13. Bamboo grows in clumps of varying heights in jungles throughout the tropics. Large bamboo stands are obstacles for wheeled or tracked vehicles. A bamboo stand is another term for a bamboo grove or a small bamboo forest. It refers to a dense grouping of bamboo plants growing together. Soldier movement on foot through bamboo is slow, exhausting, and noisy. Soldiers should bypass bamboo stands if possible.

## Cultivated Areas

1-14. Cultivated areas exist throughout the tropics and range from large, well-planned, and well-managed farms and plantations to small tracts cultivated by individual farmers. Rice paddies, plantations, and small farms make up the three general types of cultivated areas. These areas tend to be open, hot, dirty and swampy, and rubber and palm oil plantations, which offer similar characteristics to primary jungle except for the fact that the trees are in straight lines.

### Rice Paddies

1-15. Rice paddies are flat, flooded fields with muddy and soft bottoms. Farmers control the flooding of the fields with a network of dikes and irrigation ditches and fluctuate flooding with seasonal and agricultural cycles. The variable depth of flood waters directly impacts movement with higher flood waters severely restricting movement. However, travelling by vehicle is difficult even when fields are dry. Concealment is poor in rice paddies. Concealment in rice paddies is exceptionally poor primarily because they are man-made agricultural environments designed for maximum sun exposure and easy access, characteristics that are the opposite of what is required for effective cover. The terrain is flat, open, and lacks the natural complexity and density needed to hide from observation. The combination of low, sparse vegetation and a flat, gridded layout makes any person or object an immediate and obvious anomaly. The cover is limited to the dikes, and then only from ground fire. Observation and fields of fire are excellent. Foot movement is poor when the fields are wet because Soldiers must wade through water and soft mud that is often 20-inches deep. When the fields are dry, foot movement becomes easier. Dikes, about 6- to 10-feet tall, are the only obstacles.

### Plantations

1-16. Plantations are large farms or estates where tree crops, such as rubber and coconut, are grown. They are usually carefully planned and free of undergrowth (like a well-tended park). Movement through plantations is generally easy. Observation along the rows of trees is generally good. Cover and concealment can be found behind the trees, but Soldiers moving down the cultivated rows are exposed.

### Small Farms

1-17. Small farms exist throughout the tropics. Farmers often create small farms when they clear the secondary jungle to cultivate crops. These small, cultivated areas are usually hastily planned and after 1 or 2 years are abandoned leaving behind a small open area which slowly returns to secondary jungle. Movement through these areas may be difficult due to fallen trees and scrub brush. Generally, observation and fields of fire have fewer restrictions in cultivated areas than in uncultivated jungles. However, the cultivation removes much of the natural cover and concealment leaving Soldiers more exposed in these areas.

## Section III – Jungle Terrain

1-18. Jungle terrain is varied and ranges from nearly flat savanna to impassable mountain ranges. Jungle terrain can canalize operations due to poor cross-country mobility, limited hardened roads and trails, and limited bridges and hardened crossing points. Some areas affect cross-country mobility with poor trafficability due to soft soils, rocky areas, mountains, dense to impenetrable vegetation, marshy areas, streams, and major rivers. Roads are usually scarce, poorly maintained, and primitive. Limited infrastructure reduces hardened crossing points over or through water features. The following paragraphs describe OAKOC. OAKOC is used to conduct terrain analysis. This framework assists leaders evaluate the jungle terrain’s impact on operations, informing decisions about movement, maneuver, and logistics.

## Observation and Fields of Fire

1-19. Observation and fields of fire are generally restricted in jungles. Steep jungle terrain, extensive and dense vegetation, and intermittent and rapidly changing atmospheric conditions restrict direct-fire weapons well short of their maximum range. The minimum engagement range of weapon systems must be considered when emplaced and employing weapons.

1-20. Even though the landscape appears flat when viewed from above; the terrain under the dense vegetative growth can be undulating with relatively deep streams, riverbeds, and depressions creating significant dead space. Direct fire weapons must be emplaced to provide mutual support and cover dead space. Soldiers cover dead space by utilizing indirect fires paired with sensors, observation posts (OPs), or aerial fire assets.

1-21. When units cannot establish manned OPs from dominant terrain, the only means of observation may be manned aircraft systems, unmanned aircraft systems (UASs), or electronic sensors. However, dense vegetation and weather conditions can severely limit these platforms and systems.

1-22. UASs should be used in conjunction with other unmanned and manned reconnaissance and surveillance systems in a jungle environment. Successful UAS employment in a jungle requires adequate planning for dense vegetation and other environmental factors. Depending on the platform and mission, planners and operators may have to consider the environmental factors in four separate locations: a landing and recovery site, a transit route, a satellite relay site, and an anticipated target location.

1-23. Night vision devices (NVDs) are passive unless paired with an illuminator for active use. Passive NVDs can operate effectively in jungle environments when ambient visible light exists. However, cloud cover, fog, inclement weather, and dense overhead vegetation negatively impact passive NVDs.

1-24. Correction of indirect and aerial fires can be complicated in the jungle environment. Heavy tree cover can cause munitions to detonate in the overhanging branches. Soldiers can overcome this early detonation by using special fusing. Soldiers may also fail to observe the impact of rounds due to limited observation ranges requiring adjustment of fires to be conducted using other methods.

## Avenues of Approach

1-25. Roads and trails crisscross many jungle areas, the majority of which are unimproved and not all-weather capable. Improved all-weather roads that support wheeled vehicles are rare; complex road systems beyond simple commercial links generally do not exist. For centuries, locals have used simple roads and trails to connect centers of commerce, collection points, and natural resources such as mineral deposits and timber resources. Roads and trails in jungle regions support dismounted movement. Speed of movement varies depending on the weather, ground composition, wet gap crossings, and topographical influences.

1-26. The relatively few improved all-weather routes in many jungle areas canalize movement of wheeled and tracked vehicles. Logistics units are heavily impacted by this scarcity and channelization as they require improved routes to maintain the line of communications (LOC) for their large, mission-essential vehicles that have limited off-road trafficability.

1-27. In jungle regions, rivers and streams are primary corridors for moving personnel and material, especially over long distances. These rivers and streams often have heavy overgrowth providing concealment from ground and aerial observation. The threat of fluctuating water levels due to precipitation, or the lack thereof, pose significant danger to Soldiers and their equipment as they traverse rivers and streams. Flooding may occur even when it is not raining in the immediate area. Before crossing rivers or streams, units reconnoiter the far side of the linear obstacle for both trafficability and security. Enemy forces in covered and concealed positions can easily engage exposed Soldiers during gap crossings.

1-28. Normally mangrove and palm swamps prove impassable to all but the lightest equipped forces. Regardless, any force requires detailed reconnaissance to assess trafficability. Many jungles have swamp areas associated with rivers and streams as well as along the seacoast. Old trails or paths may cross the swamp, which are visible from the ground but invisible from the air. Trafficability of swamps directly relates to the depth of the water, which can vary widely based upon precipitation and the time of the year.

1-29. Exploration for and production of oil and other natural resources means wells, pipelines, refineries, quarries, and crushing plants may be of strategic importance in the jungle. Pipelines installed 3 feet (1 meter) off the ground inhibit movement. Subsurface pipelines can also create an obstacle. In Southwest Asia, maps generally indicate locations of subsurface pipelines. These pipes are often buried at such a shallow depth that heavy vehicles can damage them when traversing them. Furthermore, if a pipeline ruptures, not only is the spill of oil a consideration, but the fumes may be hazardous as well.

1-30. Agriculture in jungle areas typically has large effects on trafficability. The dike systems associated with rice paddies can limit surface mobility and create choke points. Large plantations create areas with great amounts of trafficability for mounted and dismounted forces. Abandoned small farms can create small pockets of secondary jungle which significantly decreases trafficability.

## Key Terrain

1-31. Key terrain in the jungle can be any man-made feature, water crossing, water way, or high ground. Because there are few man-made features in the jungle, those that do exist can become important, perhaps even key. Bridges over waterways, regardless of size, often become key terrain due to their relative scarcity. The high ground in the jungle can be key terrain, but limited observation and fields of fire may reduce their importance. In jungle regions, navigable waterways are extremely likely to be key terrain. They often offer the only way to move large amounts of people, supplies, and equipment significant distances relatively rapidly. The meandering routes and variability of water flows complicates their utilization and security.

## Obstacles

1-32. The jungle is generally a large natural obstacle to wheeled and tracked vehicles as well as dismounted troops. Waterways thick with impenetrable vegetation, steep slopes, swamps, water saturated soils, and mountains all hinder cross-country movement. The few trafficable areas in the jungle are ideal for employing man-made obstacles as the natural terrain denies readily available bypass routes. Buried mines of all types are incredibly effective in jungle regions due to the ability of vegetation to hide any evidence of their emplacement.

1-33. Rivers and streams can pose a danger to moving units, as they can appear unexpectedly, and vehicles and personnel can fall off the elevated embankment. The embankments of a river or stream may have heavy erosion and collapse under the weight of personnel or vehicles. River and stream embankments are particularly difficult to detect when conducting operations using NVDs with limited ambient illumination and thick vegetation.

## Cover and Concealment

1-34. In jungle terrain, concealment is exceptionally effective due to the dense, multi-layered vegetation that obstructs views from both the air and the ground. The thick upper canopy, formed by interlocking trees and vines, acts as a natural roof, blocking aerial surveillance and diffusing thermal signatures. This same canopy plunges the forest floor into deep shadow, where a chaotic undergrowth of shrubs, fallen trees, and complex root systems provides abundant hiding spots, breaking up silhouettes and masking movement from ground-level observation.

1-35. This natural cover allows soldiers to effectively hide themselves and their equipment, making it difficult for an enemy to detect their presence. The use of natural foliage for personal camouflage further enhances a soldier's ability to blend into the visually disruptive environment. However, this dense vegetation also presents challenges, as it severely restricts mobility and fields of fire, making it difficult to locate both friendly and enemy forces. The very features that provide excellent concealment also create a claustrophobic and disorienting environment where an enemy could be meters away and remain unseen.

## Section IV – Jungle Life and Its Hazards

1-36. There are four main groups of jungle hazards: effects of the climate, flash floods, deadfall, and flora and fauna. There is very little to fear from the jungle environment. Fear itself can be an enemy. Soldiers must be taught to control their fear of the jungle. Properly prepared Soldiers should have no reason to fear the jungle. The jungle is neutral and affects combatants equally. The combatant whose personnel and equipment are best prepared for jungle operations has a distinct advantage.

## Effects of the Climate

1-37. The discomforts of tropical climates are often exaggerated, but it is true that the heat is most persistent, and the high humidity can be very uncomfortable. In regions where the air contains a lot of moisture, the effect of the heat may seem worse than the same temperature in a dry climate. Leaders and Soldiers must understand that the jungle environment is neutral; it affects both friendly and enemy soldiers the same. Leaders and Soldiers should reference ATP 2-01.3 when preparing estimates on the effect of terrain and weather on operations. Once acclimatized, most people prefer the regular climate of these areas to the frequent weather changes in colder climates. It is still possible to experience the cold, although of course very low temperatures do not occur. Chilly days and nights are common in hilly areas and in some months a sleeping bag or blanket is required for a good night’s sleep. Temperatures in the jungle may drop 10 degrees (°) or more at night. At minimum, Soldiers should replace wet clothing worn during the day with dry clothing at night. Tropical downpours are usually followed by clear skies and in some areas the rains are predictable at certain times of the day. Except in those areas where rainfall may be continuous during the rainy season, there are few days when the sun does not shine part of the time. Units who operate in the tropics should plan their activities so that they are able to stay under shelter during the rainy and hotter portions of the day.

### Flash Floods

1-38. After heavy rain, small streams and dry creeks can rapidly become impassable, and storms further upstream create similar conditions. Never sleep on the bank of a sizeable stream or river.

### Deadfall

1-39. Fatalities occasionally occur from falling trees or branches. In the primary jungle, large trees can be heard falling daily. Always check a laying up point (LUP) site for deadfall, look for leafless trees or those whose trunks have been eaten by termites. Also check for hang ups where dead trees have fallen but are being supported by other healthy trees. In addition, after periods of prolonged rain, tops of trees can fill with water becoming top heavy. This can bring healthy live trees down without any warning.

## Flora and Fauna

1-40. As types of jungle differ around the world’s tropical regions, so do the types of flora and fauna. Local knowledge is required by Soldiers to be aware of these differences and any potential hazards.

### Worms

1-41. There are a variety of skin worms that can be picked up in the jungle. Their presence can cause intense itching and red, weeping skin.

### Insects

1-42. There are various insects that propose a risk to Soldiers operating in the jungle environment to include mosquitoes, wasps, centipedes, spiders, ants, and ticks.

### Mosquitoes

1-43. Malaria-carrying mosquitoes are probably the most harmful of the tropical insects and proper precautions must be taken. Mosquitoes are most prevalent early at night and just before dawn. Soldiers must be very cautious especially during these times. Malaria is more common in populated areas than in uninhabited jungle, so Soldiers must also be especially cautious when operating around villages.

### Wasps/Hornets/Bees

1-44. Wasps/hornets/bees are common in some places, but these rarely attack unless disturbed. In the event of an attack, the victim should move away to a safe area as soon as possible. Anaphylactic shock is a real danger if an individual is stung badly.

### Centipedes/Scorpions

1-45. Centipedes/scorpions can inflict stings which are painful but, in general, are not fatal. These insects like dark and dry places, so it is advisable to shake out blankets before sleeping at night and to clear clothing and shoes.

### Spiders/Ants/Ticks

1-46. Spiders are commonly found; their bites are painful but not fatal. Spider bites often require medical treatment due to necrotic tissue and secondary infection. Ants can be a danger and a great annoyance; wounded or incapacitated Soldiers must be kept free from them. Ticks are abundant. They tend to attach themselves to the sweaty areas of the neck, armpits, and crotch.

### Leeches

1-47. Leeches are common in many jungle areas. They are not poisonous, although their bites itch considerably and may become infected if not cared for properly. They release themselves if touched with insect repellent, a moist piece of tobacco, the burning end of a cigarette, an ember from a fire, or a few drops of alcohol. De-leeching should be a standard practice, in buddy pairs, especially in the evening before washing, if permissible. Trousers should be securely tucked into the boots with mosquito repellent rubbed well into the clothing and boot tops to prevent leeches entering clothing. Leeches are found in swampy areas, streams, and moist jungle country. Soldiers operating in the jungle should watch for leeches on the body and brush them off before they have had time to bite.

### Snakes

1-48. Snakes are generally more frightened of humans than the Soldier is of them, and the Soldier will probably see few if any, and even then, the snake will make every effort to escape. This does not mean that snakes can be ignored, and particular care should be taken when clearing ground. All snake bites should be treated as venomous. If a Soldier should accidentally step on a snake or otherwise disturb a snake, it will probably attempt to bite. The chances of this happening to Soldiers traveling along trails or waterways are remote if Soldiers are alert and careful.

### Crocodiles and Caymans

1-49. Although there are few recordings of crocodiles and caymans attacking humans, most of the large rivers in the tropics contain them. Caymans found in Central and South America are not likely to attack unless provoked.

### Wild Animals

1-50. The larger mammals are rarely seen. When encountered, they make every attempt to leave the area as quickly as possible. However, all large animals can be dangerous if cornered, wounded, or suddenly startled at close quarters, especially if they are with their young. In the event of an encounter, the formation must withdraw from the area.

### Thorny/poisonous Vegetation

1-51. A wide range of plants are supported by defensive mechanisms. These range from thorns, spines and stinging hairs to poisonous saps. The percentage of plants with defenses varies from region to region. For all operations it is advisable to enlist the early help of locals who can advise on what to avoid. On operations some of the dangers can be avoided by applying the correct dress rules.
