# Hauling and Lowering Systems

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 12. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Mountain warfare requires moving Soldiers, weapons, and equipment up, over, and down steep, rugged terrain. It is vital that Soldiers understand the principles and fundamental concepts of hauling and lowering heavy loads both efficiently and safely.

## Section I – Hauling Techniques

12-1. Mountain operations involve many situations that require hauling systems. A hauling system is a technical rope system used to move a load up steep or vertical terrain comprised of rigging that generates increased mechanical advantage (MA) and captures progress. Properly using hauling systems allows Soldiers to move heavy loads through terrain obstacles that would have been impossible to overcome otherwise. Common situations requiring these systems are equipment recovery, resupply, and rescue operations. Choosing the right system for a given situation requires understanding the principles of MA, categories of systems, and solid judgement.

## Mechanical Advantage

12-2. **A mechanical advantage is a reduction in the force required to move a load, which comes at the cost of time and distance**. **Specific rope rigging techniques in mountaineering generate mechanical advantage.** During hauling operations, MA can be created using a rope through several rigging techniques. The advantage gained reduces the amount of force that is necessary to move a load vertically. This makes hauling heavy loads easier. When choosing and constructing a hauling system, the goal is to use the lowest level of MA to move the load. As MA increases, efficiency and load sensitivity decrease. Efficiency is lost throughout the system anytime the rope touches a surface (including the edge of the cliff), moves through carabiners or pulleys, and rubs on itself in the system. Load sensitivity is the ability of the Soldier pulling on the rope to feel what is happening to the load on the end of the rope. For example, a Soldier pulling a heavy load up a slope by hand will know immediately if the load becomes stuck on an obstacle. However, a Soldier moving the load using a high level of MA would not feel this until there was a large amount of tension in the system. This is important when moving casualties or sensitive equipment. These systems require time, materials, and personnel to construct and operate. The intent is not to make easy work of moving the load. The lowest level of MA is desired to maximize load sensitivity, efficiency, and time.

12-3. The more MA a rope system produces, the less efficient it will be. For example, a Soldier operating a 5:1 system only needs to exert 1/5 of the pulling force to move the load. However, that Soldier will need to pull 5 feet of rope through the system for the load to move 1 foot. A balance between work and efficiency must be achieved when selecting an appropriate MA for a given situation.

## Hauling System Components

12-4. The hauling system has five basic components. Understanding how they work to move the load together (see the list below) allows Soldiers to achieve greater efficiency during hauling:

- Anchors—the anchors required for hauling a heavy load must be unquestionable (see chapter 9). Anchors should be located away from the edge of the slope or cliff to maximize the platform and working space.
- Load—the equipment, weapons, or casualty to be hauled up the terrain.
- Load strand—rope that serves as the connection to the load.
- Pull strand—rope to which pulling force is applied.
- Progress capture (ratchet)—hauling systems require a method of maintaining lift distance gained. A progress capture will only allow one-way rope movement and will maintain tension on the mainline. This may be because the hauler needs to move to the edge for communication or simply because the system has collapsed completely, and pulleys need to be reset. Progress captures can be created using friction hitches, such as a prusik, or mechanical devices, such as a rope grab or pulley with a built-in ratchet. Some plaquette and ABDs may be used, but they may cause an increase in friction.
- Moveable friction hitch or rope grab device (tractor)—these friction hitches or devices allow the system to collapse toward the anchor, and then are reset outward toward the load to allow continual hauling. Take care with respect to rope-grab devices with teeth. Over tensioning a stuck load with these devices can tear the sheath of the rope.
- Force—hauling systems are multipliers of force. They are expressed in terms of output versus input (for example, 2:1 and 3:1). This means that when Soldiers pull on the haul strand, the unit of force they exert is multiplied throughout the system until it reaches the load. When appropriate, multiple Soldiers may be used on the haul strand to increase the initial force put into the system.

12-5. Static or low-stretch ropes should be used for hauling operations whenever possible. Dynamic ropes are less efficient due to increased rope stretch.

## Mechanical Advantage Calculation

12-6. It is vital that Soldiers constructing and operating hauling systems understand how to calculate MA. This ensures systems are constructed correctly and that Soldiers understand the forces they are applying. Memorizing rope systems does not constitute deliberate decision making or guarantee the correct solution to a problem. It is difficult to calculate all the factors that take away from MA. However, we can easily calculate the ideal MA to make decisions.

12-7. To calculate the ideal MA of a constructed system, the tension method—or T-Method—is used. This method assigns one unit of tension, identified as T, to the pull strand of the system. That unit is then followed through the system to the load. To calculate the theoretical MA, compare the amount of tension the load receives to the initial input of tension. (See figure 12-1 and figure 12-2, page 306.)

12-8. Static or low-stretch ropes should be used for lowering operations whenever possible. When dynamic ropes are used, care should be taken to preload the system prior to edge transfer due to increased rope stretch.

## Categories of Hauling Systems

12-9. The three categories of hauling systems are simple, compound, and complex.

### Simple Hauling System Characteristics

12-10. Simple hauling systems can be identified by the following characteristics:

- One rope moving continuously and alternately between fixed and traveling pulleys.
- Any traveling pulleys move toward the anchor at the same speed.
- Examples are simple 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, and 5:1 systems. (See figures 12-3 and 12-4, and figure 12-5, page 308.)

### Compound Hauling System Characteristics

12-11. Compound hauling systems can be identified by the following characteristics:

- One simple system acting on another simple system.
- Any traveling pulleys move toward the anchor at different speeds.
- Examples are compound 6:1 and 9:1 systems. (See figures 12-6 and 12-7.)

### Complex Hauling System Characteristics

12-12. Complex hauling systems can be identified by the following characteristics:

- Systems that do not fit into either category.
- Examples are complex 5:1 and 7:1 systems.
- These systems have limited practical application for military operations and, therefore, are beyond the scope of this training circular.

## Techniques to Reduce Friction

12-13. Friction reduces efficiency of rope movement and can drastically reduce MA. It is important to identify points of friction within the system and strive to reduce them. Efficiency is lost throughout the system anytime the rope touches hardware. Friction can be minimized by using pulleys. Pulleys are used to change direction in rope systems. Pulleys should be small, lightweight, and rated for mountaineering applications. They should accommodate the largest diameter of rope being used. Pulleys are made with several bearings, different-sized sheaves (wheels), and metal alloy side plates. (See figure 12-8, page 310.) Always avoid plastic pulleys. The side plate should rotate on the pulley axle to allow the pulley to be attached at any point along the rope. For best results, the sheave diameter must be at least four times larger than the rope’s diameter to maintain high rope strength. Pulleys with side plates designed to accommodate prusik knots are referred to as prusik minding pulleys. These pulleys are designed with tolerances that allow the ropes to run through them but block the passage of a prusik hitch, preventing it from being caught in the pulley.

*[Figure 12-8. Types of pulleys — illustration not reproduced.]*

12-14. Pulleys are estimated to be approximately 90-percent efficient, while carabiners are considered only 60-percent efficient. The difference between a system that uses pulleys at direction changes and the same system that only uses carabiners is significant. If the team only has a few pulleys, they should be used at the direction changes closest to the system’s haul strand. Pulleys in these locations will maximize the force put into the system initially and carry 90-percent efficiency farther through the system.

12-15. Friction also occurs where the mainline contacts the edge of the slope or cliff. If a suitable anchor can be found near the edge, this friction point can be minimized. The mainline can be lifted and routed through a high directional at the top of the slope or cliff to reduce rope contact and friction at the edge. (See figure 12-9.)

## Vertical Hauling and A-Frame

12-16. The vertical hauling line is an installation used to move heavy equipment and supplies up vertical or near-vertical terrain. The vertical haul line is constructed to eliminate the friction caused by the loads contacting the terrain, rope friction at the cliff edge, and to increase the efficiency of moving loads. Although no real theoretical MA is gained, the function of the system is to reduce the friction against terrain. This system is more of a permanent installation and is most often used on a main supply line. The description below is generalized and based on concepts. Soldiers should seek further instruction and training prior to system use.

12-17. The first and most important task is to determine where to construct the vertical hauling line. (See figure 12-10, page 312.) The site must have a top anchor that is secure enough to hold the system and load. Loading and unloading platforms should be easily accessible, natural platforms that provide a safe working area. Most sites will require the construction of an A-frame at the cliff edge to suspend the load away from the cliff face. The site should also have sufficient clearance to allow for space between the slope and pulley rope for easy hauling of equipment.

12-18. A-frames should be secured to an anchor behind the edge. (See figure 12-11.) A master point is created in the apex of the A-frame, the leg bottoms joined with cord and dug into the ground. (See chapter 13 for A-frame construction.) A rope is joined into a continuous loop and placed into a pulley in the master point so that the loop reaches the base of the cliff. The A-frame is positioned so that it is leaning out over the edge. This allows loads to hang freely during hauling. Loads are attached to the rope and hauled upward to the cliff top. This specific system requires time and experience to construct and operate. It should only be used if other options are unavailable.

## Redirected Hauling Systems

12-19. Some situations may require redirecting the hauling system away from the pathway of the load. This is done when the characteristics of the terrain allow for more work or pull space in an alternate or perpendicular direction. It can be achieved by first routing the load strand through an anchor to change direction. From this point, the hauling system can be constructed as before but using the more optimal workspace. This workspace makes pulling easier. When sufficient rope is available, longer systems can be constructed, reducing the number of resets needed during operation. A second technique involves constructing the hauling system in line with the load strand, but then redirecting just the hauling strand through a change in direction at the anchor. This can also be built upon with the use of piggybacked systems.

## Offset Compound Hauling Systems

12-20. Offset compound hauling systems involve attaching a second, separate hauling system to the haul strand of the first system. This technique allows for creating systems that collapse at relatively the same time, which minimizes resets. It also allows the haul team to operate further back from the edge increasing workspace. This technique requires sufficient rope length to construct. (See figure 12-12.)

*[Figure 12-12. Redirected and offset compound hauling system — illustration not reproduced.]*

12-21. Operating and constructing hauling systems require training, experience, and judgement. Soldiers must understand both the advantages and disadvantages of using MA to apply these skills correctly. Efficiency can be improved by making practical and simple changes during operation when conditions change.

## Section II – Lowering Techniques

12-22. **A lowering system is a technical rope system used to move a load down steep or vertical terrain in a controlled fashion using friction hitches or mechanical devices**. These sorts of systems are used to control the speed of descent for personnel or equipment using rope. They are an integral part of both high- and low-angle rescue. (See chapter 15.) Lowering a victim or a load is always preferred over hauling if the terrain and situation allows. No two situations are identical. Each new situation will dictate which lowering techniques and systems are appropriate. Understanding basic lowering concepts is key to achieving a safe and efficient solution to the problem.

## Lowering Concepts

12-23. There are many devices and techniques available depending on the weight of the load. The bottom line for any lowering system is that enough friction must be created to safely control the descent of the load. Different terrain demands different methods of creating friction. When the terrain is steep and broken, selecting a device or technique that provides varying levels of friction is preferred.

12-24. All lowering systems should be closed by securing the free end of the rope (the end not attached to the load). This prevents lowering the load off the end of the rope if the distance of the lower versus the length of rope is misjudged.

12-25. When lowering Soldiers, a hands-free backup for the system should be used. (See figure 11-15, page 300.) This can be achieved in multiple ways. Hands-free backups provide additional measures of protection against human error and allow the Soldier or team operating the lowering system flexibility while controlling the brake strand.

## Techniques and Devices for Creating Friction

12-26. There are numerous ways to create friction for lowering. The least technical solution to safely lower the load is the goal. Three basic methods are the terrain feature, Munter hitch, and redirected belay device.

### Terrain Feature

12-27. This method involves simply wrapping the rope attached to the load around a natural object, such as a tree or boulder. (See figure 12-13, page 316.) The number of wraps depends on the amount of friction desired. This technique is used for gentle, low-angle terrain, when speed is needed, or when equipment is in short supply. It also provides variable friction but may often require more than one Soldier on the brake strand to operate safely.

*[Figure 12-13. Terrain lower — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Munter Hitch

12-28. The Munter hitch provides enough friction for a one-person load. The Munter hitch can be easily converted to a super Munter if more friction is desired. (See figure 12-14.)

**Note.** Take care not to open the gate of a fully loaded carabiner. This could cause potential failure of the carabiner.

12-29. The super Munter provides an enormous amount of friction; it is used for lowering only. In most circumstances, the super Munter provides more friction than needed. Therefore, its use is limited to when a single Soldier is lowering a heavy load.

*[Figure 12-14. Munter hitch — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Redirected Belay Device

12-30. A redirected belay device provides enough friction for up to two people and causes fewer twists in the rope than a Munter hitch. (See figure 12-15, page 318.)

## Techniques for Creating Hands-Free Backup

12-31. Hands-free backups are an important component of lowering systems. They provide a critical element of safety when Soldiers are being lowered. There are two main options for creating hands-free backups: a friction hitch on the load strand or friction hitch on the brake strand.

12-32. Friction hitches on the load strand are used when the Soldier operating the lowering system does not have a harness on. (See figure 12-16.) This can be achieved by tying a prusik with the middle of a cordelette to the lowering rope, then clipping a fixed loop tied with the tails to the master point of the anchor. If the backup must be releasable, a releasable knot such as a Munter mule can be used instead of a fixed loop. Deciding which technique to use is based off the terrain and the situation. Soldiers must be careful to keep the prusik close to the Munter hitch during the lower so that it does not grab the lowering rope and stop the operation unintentionally. They must also ensure that the wraps of the prusik do not become loose. This could prevent the prusik from automatically gripping the rope if control of the lower is lost. This requires careful operation from a single rescuer or the assistance of a second Soldier to mind the prusik.

*[Figure 12-16. Friction hitch on load strand (releasable) — illustration not reproduced.]*

12-33. Friction hitches on the brake strand require the Soldier operating the lowering system to be wearing a harness. (See figure 12-17, page 320, and figure 12-18, page 321.) The preferred technique is an autoblock in a carabiner attached to the belay loop of the harness. This technique allows for Soldier flexibility in knot passes and load transfers for operation when moving away from the anchor toward the edge for better visibility and communication. Friction hitch backups on the brake strand are simpler to manage and preferred when a harness is available.

## Concepts for Passing a Knot

# Knot Pass Special Note

12-34. Situations arise when a knot must be passed through the lowering system. This can occur when there is a damaged section of the rope that has been isolated with a knot or when ropes have been joined to lower longer distances. If passing a knot is necessary, it is usually conducted or overseen by a level 2 mountaineer. It is preferable to construct the lowering system to facilitate passing the knot prior to beginning the lower. (See figure 12-19.)

*[Figure 12-19. Isolating a damaged section of rope — illustration not reproduced.]*

12-35. The simplest way to pass the knot occurs when the terrain and situation facilitate safely unweighting the lowering system temporarily. This is generally in low-angle terrain where most of the load is resting on the ground. This unweighting enables creates enough slack to place a new friction hitch or device behind the knot, and then remove original friction hitch or device from in front of the knot, so that lowering can continue.

12-36. If the terrain and weight of the load do not allow the lowering system to be safely unweighted, a weighted load transfer is required to pass the knot. These techniques are best learned with formal hands-on training and should only be conducted by level 2 and 3 mountaineers.

12-37. The construction and operation of lowering systems requires training, experience, and judgement. Soldiers and leaders must understand what they are doing and why they are doing it. Strive to use the right amount of friction for the terrain, situation, and load.
