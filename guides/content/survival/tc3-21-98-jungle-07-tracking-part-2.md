# Tracking: Reading Sign and Following a Trail (Part 2 of 2)

*TC 3-21.98, Chapter 7, continued.*

## Element of Tracking 4 — Information Gained from Sign Tracking

7-76. For a tracker to make intelligent, accurate and logical assumptions about a target, it is essential that the tracker has a detailed knowledge of the enemy. Commanders at all levels must ensure that the tracker receives a full intelligence brief on the enemy prior to any patrol action. The more the tracker knows, the more the tracker will find out. The target’s mission will rarely be known at the beginning of a tracking task and is normally determined by intelligence cells based on the information provided by the tracker and from previous target information. The information required prior to a patrol should come from the following sources:

- Own intelligence cell.
- Previous patrol reports.
- Local population.
- Police and other security forces.

7-77. Intelligence: the tracker should conduct a detailed study of the enemy’s background and in particular the tracker should look at the following:

- Enemy traits.
- Enemy doctrine.
- Enemy habitat.

7-78. Traits: these are the attributes distinguishing one target from another. A study of the target’s lifestyle provides excellent information for the tracker as follows:

- Attitudes relate to the general moods or emotions of the enemy as certain nationalities display particularly aggressive or emotional attitudes.
- Beliefs refer to the target’s primary beliefs and principles:
  - These beliefs are likely to influence the targets’ behavior.
  - For example, a fundamentalist enemy will often suffer more hardship than a less motivated enemy.
- Habits refer to the target’s tendency or disposition to act in a particular way, for example always resting in the afternoon. These habits become evident to a tracker over a period of time and will normally assist the tracker in the pursuit.
- Mentality relates to how the target is likely to operate. Does the tracker suffer hardship for a prolonged period, for example, live on hard routine, or does the tracker have certain expectations.
- Physique refers to the physical build of the enemy. Knowledge of the enemy’s general physique (tall, small, muscular, slim, and so forth) will assist the tracker to identify the target’s sign in areas of foul track. It will also assist in determining the presence of foreigners amid a target group, for example, the sign left by a European amid a group of Asians should be distinctive.
- Diet relates to food and drink that the target regularly consumes. An understanding of the target’s diet assists the tracker once again in identifying the targets’ sign and may well assist the tracker in making assumptions as to the target’s routines and future intentions.

7-79. Doctrine: Interlocked with the target’s traits, consideration should be given to the target’s doctrine, or teaching/thought pattern as follows:

- Customs, Traditions, and Religious Practices: this relates to the target’s usual or long-term habitual practices. Customs may differ from target to target, but a common thread amongst groups is likely to be evident. The target’s traditions may well affect his daily movement and tactics, for example stopping to bathe daily or centrally feeding. A target’s religious beliefs and teachings may even dictate halt times and rest periods.
- Military Practices: a thorough understanding of the target’s drills and procedures will assist the tracker on all tasks. A tracker should immediately recognize the type of enemy the tracker is pursuing by the tactics employed and the numbers being tracked.

7-80. Habitat: a tracker should know where the target comes from. A study of the target’s natural habitat will assist the tracker in the task by speeding up the ability to interpret information gained. In jungle regions, the target’s habitat would normally be one of the following:

- Remote: a target from a remote region is most likely to possess excellent fieldcraft skills and be adept in living off the land, however, tradition and superstitions may well influence him. An enemy from a remote region may be difficult to track as he is more than likely to be able to self-track.
- Rural: a target from a rural area would traditionally be a farmer or peasant. In most cases the target has good fieldcraft skills and will probably be able to travel light and live off the land. The target is also used to hardships which may result in leaving minimal signs.
- Suburban: a target from a suburban area will generally be far less aware of ‘fieldcraft’ and field skills than a rural counterpart and is usually easier to track.
- Urban: a target from an urban area will undoubtedly possess far fewer fieldcraft skills than a target from a remote or rural area. The urban target is therefore likely to require more luxuries and suffers less hardship than someone from another habitat.

### Information Gained

7-81. It is difficult for even small groups to move through an environment without leaving sign noticeable to the trained eye. The tracker must be able to gain as much information as possible from any track. A VT, depending on the state of the track, can gain the following information:

- Direction of travel.
- Numbers being tracked.
- Age of the track.
- Speed and load of people being tracked.
- Rations being used.
- Types of weapons carried.
- Tactics employed.
- Habits/routine.
- Sex of target.
- General health.

### Direction of Travel

7-82. From a given length of track, the VT can assess the general direction that the enemy is moving. Based on a good ground and map study the tracker/intelligence cells can begin to identify the following:

- Possible enemy targets.
- Possible locations of enemy camps or resupply points.
- Likely enemy intentions.

7-83. To ascertain the direction of travel a tracker must be able to identify those types of sign that can indicate the direction in which the target is moving. The following are used as direction indicators:

- Use of pointers.
- Transfer from one area to another.
- High grass and fern pushed down in the direction of movement.
- Heel marks or boot prints in favorable situations (indicator paces).

### Numbers of Being Tracked

7-84. A knowledge of the enemy strength is essential, and the tracker should try to gain this information as a matter of urgency. Determining the following factors may assist the tracker in estimating numbers:

- The number of sleeping areas identified in a patrol base/LUP area.
- The number of resting places at a rest halt.
- Different types of boot patterns (sign pattern).
- Using a heel count in soft ground where boot prints are distinct using one of the following methods:
  - Measure off a distance of 24 inches and count the number of heel marks noting that one human rarely places the feet down twice within 24 inches.
  - 24 inches can be measured from the butt of the SA80 to the end of the hand guard.
  - Measure off one overstretched pace, then count the number of heel marks within that pace and divide by two; this gives an estimate, for example, five heel marks, two, possibly three people.
  - Measure off two normal paces, then count the number of heel marks within the two paces and divide by two; again, estimate five heel marks, two, possibly three people.

### Age of Track

7-85. Knowing the age of sign enables the tracker to ascertain how far ahead of the tracker the target is and enables both the tracker and intelligence cells to determine where the enemy may be when combined with knowledge of the enemy’s speed of travel. Indicators to judge the age of sign include:

- The state of surrounding vegetation.
- Impressions in the mud (comparison).
- Obliteration by rain (bracketing).
- Cracks in bent grass or leaves (comparison).
- Game tracks are superimposed.
- Leaves covering tracks (previous days’ climatic conditions).

### Speed and Load of the Target

7-86. Sign that indicates speed, and sign that indicates load, are intricately combined. Knowing the speed and load of the target is essential to the tracker/intelligence cells for numerous reasons (see figure 7-36 on page 96), for example:

- If the direction of travel is known, to gain on the enemy, the track can be isolated by moving at speed off track before picking it back up and continuing task.
- If the plan is to destroy the enemy being tracked, then knowing the speed and direction of travel will assist in determining where to site ambushes on his projected route.
- Soldiers travelling light are likely to need resupply or early extraction—
  - The tracker or intelligence cell must ask how, when and where this may take place.
  - If either can be identified and patrols tasked early enough the likelihood of success against the enemy is significantly increased.

7-87. A tracker can estimate the speed and load of the enemy by utilizing the following indicators:

- The distance between rest/LUPs/meal halts will indicate how far the enemy can travel in one day or between meals.
- In soft ground the distance between paces can indicate speed, for example, large stride—fast moving, small stride—slow moving.
- The depth of pace (in comparison to the tracker’s) can indicate heavy loads being carried, which means they may be carrying some sort of pack.
- Studying the amount of sign left behind indicates speed, for example, are they taking time to cover it up.
- The route used and obstacles encountered will indicate speed and load:
  - The enemy may go around an obstacle rather than over or through it.
  - Heavy loads, or the enemy may stick to ridgelines indicating a possible requirement to move quickly.
- From the information gained regarding the speed of the enemy, the tracker must ask; is the enemy travelling light and moving fast, or is the enemy moving slowly and why?

### Rations

7-88. A tracker can gain the following information from discarded rations:

- The nationality of the target if not known:
  - Certain foods may indicate origin.
  - For example, continually finding rice might indicate the target is of Asian origin.
- Who has supplied the rations, for example, are they issued rations or locally supplied rations and if locally supplied, where are the rations coming from?
- Types of rations being used, for example, heavy rations such as tinned food, or lightweight rations such as freeze-dried products. By determining the type of rations used the tracker can ascertain the following:
  - Lightweight rations may cause the enemy to LUP next to water or carry out frequent water resupply drills; the enemy normally needs to cook these rations.
  - Heavy rations might indicate why the enemy are carrying heavy loads and perhaps why his speed is reduced; the enemy might also eat these rations cold and therefore leave less sign, for example, fire marks, and so forth.

### Types of Weapons

7-89. If the weapons used are not known, identifying the weapons the enemy may be carrying is very difficult and is only possible under favorable conditions. The following can assist the tracker:

- Information from a contact area such as:
  - Discarded ammunition cartridges.
  - Sign located during the initial cast of incident area.
  - From friendly forces in the area of the contact.
- A sighting by a TT.
- Discarded ammo cartridges along the track/or in LUP areas.
- Flannelette located in rest/LUP areas, for example, after cleaning weapons.
- Butt or bipod marks on the ground around rest/LUP/halt areas.
- Sight marks on trees where weapons may have been rested.

### Tactics

7-90. Identifying enemy tactics is a fundamental part of tracking, especially when information reporting. A tracker must therefore determine the tactics employed by the enemy. Knowing the tactics employed not only assist in determining how friendly forces operate against the enemy but assist the tracker in finding information. For example, knowing if the enemy posts sentries while laying up could affect when or how a follow-up force engages the enemy, or knowing that the enemy always travels with flank protection could determine how far from a track ambushes are sited. Likewise, the knowledge that the enemy snap-ambush before halting assists the tracker when determining where to look for information, such as numbers of enemy, and so forth. Assessing the enemy’s tactics is done as the track picture is built up through intelligent interpretation of the targets:

- Tactics at halts.
- Tactics at obstacles.
- Tactics at LUP/patrol base areas.
- Deception tactics, if employed.

### Habits/routines

7-91. A knowledge of the enemy’s day-to-day patrol routine is vital to the tracker. After tracking the target for a sustained period idiosyncrasies may be detected which help to identify the patrol, especially if an area of foul track is encountered. The tracker should identify and recognize habits such as:

- The habitual siting of LUPs/patrol base on or near certain topographical features such as streams:
  - Knowing this assists in identifying future enemy locations for either track isolation or follow-up purposes.
  - Such a habit might also indicate the requirement for water for cooking or fishing for food.
- Negotiating obstacles or bypassing thick vegetation in a certain manner or direction. If this can be identified, then ambushes can easily be sited in these locations.
- Carrying out halts or tasks at regular periods assists the tracker in knowing where to look for incidents:
  - This helps the tracker to anticipate enemy actions, for example, meal halt followed by a comms halt, and so forth.
  - This speeds up the tracker’s ability to interpret incidents and subsequently spend more time on track.
- Patrol members’ idiosyncrasies may also be observed, for example, smokers, twig snapping, and so forth, (sign pattern) which assist in identifying this patrol from other patrols.

### Determining Enemy Sex

7-92. This is less likely to be identified than the other information gained but is detectable by the following means:

- Enemy forces with female soldiers normally have separate sleeping areas and toilet areas, particularly those who have stricter moral codes, for example, as was the case with the Vietcong and Malay Communist Terrorists.
- Females urinate squatting thus leaving a deeper depression in the earth between the feet than a standing male.
- Evidence may be found of the debris associated with female monthly periods.
- Females tend to have more neutral pitch angle.

### General Health

7-93. The target’s general health can sometimes be detected by some of the following methods:

- Examination of toilet areas and feces, for example, diarrhea or dysentery.
- Vomit or bloody sputum indicates ill health.
- Blood-stained bandages or other medical debris indicate injury or ill health.

### Summary

7-94. The TT should always be on the lookout for information. It is essential that the patrol receives a full intelligence brief prior to the action, and they should report any information considered vital immediately. Each day a radio report is sent including a summary of the information gained, and at the end of the patrol action the patrol should be fully debriefed and complete a full patrol report. Interpretation of the facts gained starts with the TT on the ground. However, intelligence cells must conduct their own close examination of the intelligence provided to ensure that vital information is not missed.

## Section IV – Tracking Techniques and Procedures

7-95. This section explains the tracking team’s tasks and goes into detail on the importance of the reporting procedures to create actionable information.

## Tactical Tracking

7-96. The drills and procedures listed in this section should be applied in conjunction with the basic principles of patrolling and the routine drills contained in Infantry tactical doctrine. This section is designed to illustrate how a TT conducts its routine patrolling and how it conducts a pursuit. When employed on an information reporting task, the TT works purely under the command of the team commander. However, it is when the team is employed in the pursuit role, attached to a follow-up force that a greater understanding by commanders of tracking techniques is required.

### Tracker Team Composition

7-97. In general terms, any TT can conduct either information reporting or pursuit tasks. Usually, a team tasked to conduct an information reporting patrol will consist of VTs. The addition of a tracker dog will assist the VTs especially in urban areas. While for a pursuit a tracker dog is attached to the team. Anything other than this will reduce the TT’s efficiency. A TT consists of three elements: the command, the tracker, and the protection element.

### Organization

7-98. The ideal TT is organized and responsibilities allocated as follows:

- The command element consists of the team commander and a radiotelephone operator who is not required to be tracker trained.
- The tracking element consists of two VTs:
  - The first VT is employed as the lead scout (see lead scout duties section).
  - On occasions the tracking element includes one or two tracker dogs and handlers, with one or both VTs acting as CM for the dog and handler.
- The protection element consists of:
  - Two additional VTs who will relieve the tracking element as and when required.
  - If fully trained VTs are not available, then riflemen who are used to operating as lead scouts may be used.

### Capabilities and Limitations of a Tracker Team

7-99. In general terms, a TT has the following capabilities.

- Deployment: the team inclusive of a dog can be deployed by any means including helicopter abseil.
- Duration: the team can ideally carry food and equipment to operate for up to 5 to 6 days. Any increase in duration without resupply adds weight and slows down the follow-up. Providing the team can be re-supplied they can continue to operate indefinitely.
- Age of track: when tasking a VT, sign up to 72-hours old should be used as a planning figure. This time varies depending upon conditions. For a tracker dog 24 hours is a good planning figure although up to 72 hours is not beyond a dog’s capabilities in favorable conditions.
- Communications: the team must be capable of communicating on both very high frequency (VHF) and HF nets.
- Intelligence: the team can locate, identify and pursue sign and from intelligent interpretation of the facts pass on information to commanders.
- Reporting: the team can report the information it has gained by either radio, patrol report, hot debrief or presentation depending on the requirements of the commander.
- Pursuit: the team can pursue sign following either a contact or sitting to gain or maintain contact as required by the commander.

7-100. In general terms, a TT has the following limitations:

- Night: the team is unable to track at night through close country in a tactical manner.
- Weather: rain and weather extremes degrade the sign/scent left by the enemy and slows trackers down.
- Contact: TT is vulnerable to contact when tracking an enemy who is tracker aware.
- Time: a track that is more than 24-hours old slows the pursuit.
- Foul track: in areas used frequently for patrol or civilian movement enemy sign is difficult to identify due to tracks being overused.

### Lead Scout Duties

7-101. One of the most physically and mentally demanding tasks in the jungle environment is that of the lead scout, because as an individual, both the lead scout’s personal patrol skills and powers of observation must be of the highest caliber. While tracking an enemy the likelihood of contact is greatly increased because at any time the TT may encounter the enemy they have been tracking. Due to the restricted visibility in close country, encounters with the enemy can happen at very close quarters with little or no warning.

7-102. Acting as a lead scout goes together with being a VT as the VT not only tracks the enemy but has the added burden of acting as the lead scout for the team. The VT is supported by the CM. They must work in unison with the CM protecting the VT at the appropriate times so that the VT can conduct the track pursuit drill if the team is to maintain its security. There are many occasions when a VT will act as a lead scout but will not necessarily be tracked, for example when patrolling to a cast site, or when patrolling to an LUP.

### Considerations

7-103. Equipment: due to the taxing nature of being a lead scout and the requirement for heightened senses, the equipment/load needs to be kept to a minimum. The lead scout should not be carrying any team equipment such as the radio, medical kit, or any support weapons.

7-104. Position and spacing of the lead scout: the scout is the eyes and ears of the team and 90 percent of the time a contact is going to come from the front, unless a well-laid ambush is encountered. Therefore, the scout’s position and spacing is critical; it goes without saying that the scout is going to be the first person in the team. However, spacing is the responsibility of the second person. Normally this spacing is slightly greater than that of the remainder of the team, enabling the scout to use the senses fully. However, when tracking, at times the CM must move further forward to provide security for the VT. This difference is only marginal, and the CM is constantly adjusting the distance depending on the visibility.

7-105. Rotation of the lead scout: the lead scout can very rapidly become mentally and physically exhausted due to the nature of the task. Commanders need to recognize this fact and consequently rotate the lead scout on a regular basis.

### Summary

7-106. During the Malaya Emergency and Borneo Confrontation most friendly force casualties were lead scouts. Fortunately, these were relatively low due to the relentless training carried out by British forces. The soldiers involved trained to a point of being intimate with their weapons, being able to detect a stoppage by sound or feel alone. This is the level that all Infantry Soldiers operating in a jungle or close country environment need to achieve. A good lead scout should be able to move silently and communicate with the minimum fuss with the team and detect any presence, be it friendly or foe, without first being seen. Through hard, dedicated, and realistic training all Infantry Soldiers should be able to attain this standard.

### Tracker Team Routine Drills

7-107. The movement of the VT element is very slow, and all team members must maintain high levels of alertness for long periods of time. On occasions, for example, when in pursuit, the TT would be supported by a section or platoon from a rifle company. Their main task while on the move would be to provide immediate support then take over from the TT when contact is made. The TT should always seek the safety of a larger friendly force where possible when laying up.

### Team Movement

7-108. The team moves cautiously and deliberately, covering all arcs as they move. The speed at which they move can be slow dependent on the skill and experience of the VT and the amount of sign created by the target. A well trained and disciplined target will leave little sign and may use deception tactics or select a route over terrain which is not conducive to leaving good sign. The target may also place snap ambushes and booby traps en route and may move along topographical features, such as ridgelines, where sign is more difficult to follow but the tracks are generally easier to site ambushes. The VT and tracking team commander must strike a balance between the requirement to gain on or maintain pace with the enemy, and the requirements for stealth and security. The rate of advance also varies depending on the terrain and conditions encountered.

7-109. The TT generally moves in a single file to allow the VT to carry out the drills without being foul tracked by other team members. The VT and CM will track, the other team members are to stay in visual range, with the team commander being the link person, and everyone remaining alert. When necessary, the remainder of the team closes to provide protection for the VT and CM (see figure 7-37).

*[Figure 7-37. Tracker team order of march — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-110. The order of march will frequently change within the team due to the rotation of the lead scout/VT. The order of march (see figure 7-37) will be the tracking element (VT followed by the CM), the command element (team commander and radiotelephone operator), then the protection element (the next pair of VTs). The CM determines the distance between the lead scout and the CM. VTs will be rotated as required. When attached, the dog and handler, when not tracking, are located within the command element.

### Routine Drills

7-111. The TT is extremely vulnerable while conducting an obstacle crossing, especially in open areas or places that are dominated by high ground. If the enemy is aware that they are being tracked, they may leave an ambush party to defeat the TT. An obstacle is an ideal ambush site because the team may well be exposed at this point. To cross an obstacle, the TT will utilize the caterpillar method as this enables the VT to remain on sign and prevent it from being fouled by other members. The drill is as follows:

- VT1 indicates the obstacle and the remainder of the team goes firm and conducts a listening halt. The VT and CM then track forward to the entry point and cover the obstacle.
- The maneuver commander and RM move up to the VT and CM and relieve them by covering up and down the obstacle while VT3 and VT4 cover the rear. The team commander can control the crossing from a central position.
- The VT and CM track the sign across the obstacle ensuring that they confirm the exit point. They then move, remaining on sign, to the limit of visibility on the far side of the obstacle and then go firm.
- VT3 and VT4 then move forward and relieve the maneuver commander and RM who subsequently cross the obstacle. On reaching the exit point they go firm and cover up and down the obstacle.
- Finally, VT3 and VT4 cross the obstacle and rejoin the team. Once complete the commander gives the order to continue tracking.

7-112. The tactical halt drill is conducted as described in chapter 4:

- The VT marks the last definite sign and back-tracks several meters before breaking track.
- On completion of the halt, the team moves to an LUP or back to the last definite sign to continue on track if the light conditions allow.

7-113. The TT should remain on sign for as long as possible. This is normally from the point after first light when the sign can be identified, up until the point when light has faded and sign can no longer be distinguished. Timings for when sign can be read cannot be dictated and will vary depending upon the terrain, weather and the ability of the VT. It stands to reason that the TT may have to occupy an LUP sometime before the last light and cannot commence tracking until sometime after the first light. The LUP or overnight halt should follow after the meal/communications halt.

### Change Over Procedure

7-114. The VT cannot track indefinitely and must not track when tired. There is no hard and fast rule as to when the VT must hand over and sometimes handing over may be tactically unsound, for example, on arrival at an incident site, or if the VT is still fresh. Flexibility therefore is required. It is essential that when the VT hands over to another VT that the track is not lost, and that security is maintained. In simple terms, the VT will hand over to the CM then become VT4, VT3 will take over as CM and VT4 will move forward one place to become VT3. It is not recommended to swap the VTs in pairs because even though both are fresh, neither will be tuned in or will be fully aware of the track picture.

7-115. When the VT states that a rest is needed or is told to change over by the team commander, the VT will go firm, and the CM will close up. The team commander will cover them. The VT briefs the CM as follows:

- The VT shows the CM the sign the VT is on, which should be conclusive, and indicates the furthest sign.
- The VT informs the CM of what key sign the VT has been following.
- The VT updates the CM on the track picture, for example, any information that the new VT may not be aware of, anticipated incidents, and so forth.

7-116. The CM then tunes in and observes the sign. If the CM is content that the VT is on conclusive sign the CM will take over. However, if CM is not content, the VT recommences on track until the next conclusive sign, where the procedure is repeated until the CM is confident that the VT is on track. The VT reconfirms all the information that was previously briefed to the CM on and steps off track. The CM tunes in and is now ready to continue as the VT. It is important in maintaining patrol security that the CM continues the duties until ready to take over as the VT.

7-117. The VT3 moves forward ahead of the team commander to cover the new VT while VT3 tunes in. The VT3 will be fully briefed by either the team commander or the old VT. The VT3 then continues on task until it is time to become the VT.

7-118. The original VT becomes the last person and VT4 will step up one place. This ensures that each VT has an equal amount of rest and that the task of being the CM is spread equally amongst the VTs, and that the CM is always fresh.

7-119. Tracking is an extremely difficult task, especially when near the enemy. It can be very slow and tiring and it is vital that the team carries out these drills proficiently as this ensures that their security is maintained.

### Kit and Equipment

7-120. The TT should travel as lightly as possible; however, the team commander must ensure that the team has all the equipment required for the team action. The team should carry all the equipment normally carried for the environment they are operating in. Depending on the tactical situation, especially during pursuit tracking, it may be advisable to employ a light machine gun in the team.

## Tracking Reports

7-121. For trackers to fulfil their potential as an intelligence gathering asset the information gained by a TT must be reported, collated and acted upon in a timely and efficient manner. On tasking any TT, they must be given a mission stating clearly a desired end state, for example, to gather information on the enemy to conduct subsequent offensive operations against them. Subsequent offensive operations based upon the information provided by trackers will normally be in the form of either an ambush or a deliberate attack. Ambushes will be set on enemy routes and cache locations and attacks will be mounted against enemy camps. The success of any follow-up action will be affected greatly by the intelligence upon which it is planned.

### Signals Reports

7-122. The ability to communicate by radio is crucial to a TT. Radio communications enable the team to relay information without returning to a friendly force, and to call support whenever they need it. The TT normally uses location schedules sending a situation report twice per day. Other urgent information must be passed without delay. The team signaler must be proficient at both HF and VHF communications.

### Tracking Report

7-123. The signal’s tracking report is used at the cast site or when sign is first located and should also be used to send any other information as and when required for example, as a daily situation report or critical information gained on track. Not all the serials have to be sent, especially if the serial contains information that has already been sent or where there is no change. Alternatively, “No Change” may be sent during difficult communications, which keeps the report running in sequence. The information sent is a summary of the information gained. This is referred to as the LNDATA report. If available, the following information is sent:

- Location.
- Number of personnel.
- Direction the quarry is moving.
- Age of the track.
- Type of track.
- Additional information.

7-124. The signals reports must be sent at the correct times so that the intelligence can be collated as it becomes available. The team sends a tracking report on initially locating sign and then in accordance to the allotted radio schedules.

### Hot Debrief

7-125. The hot debrief is utilized on the completion of a patrol as a means of reporting the main information gained. For a TT the hot debrief is normally delivered to the team’s platoon commander, leadership, an intelligence officer, operations officer or if attached to a company the company commander. Before the hot debrief, the team commander debriefs the team and collates all the relevant information. The hot debrief should be conducted no later than 1 hour after the team returns, and the commander must ensure the passing of key information. If possible, the complete team should be present during the debriefing.

### Visual Tracking Report

7-126. The signals reports and the hot debrief are fundamental in reporting the information gained from the track. However, this information is often collected by more than one agency. The hot debrief is probably not written down and recorded except in the patrol notebook. This information over a period of time may become mislaid and intelligence will be lost. The visual tracking report is the primary means for recording information. When produced the final product will include a full record of the information gained on the enemy. Furthermore, the visual tracking report is archived and recorded as part of the overall operational intelligence picture and can be used to inform planners of future operations.

## Section V – Countertracking and Antitracking

**7-127.** Antitracking refers to deceptive techniques used to disguise one’s own tracks or to fool the tracker. The following techniques may be helpful to increase the time-distance gap or to momentarily confuse pursuing parties. However, a well-trained tracker will always overcome one’s efforts. Antitracking techniques include:

- Walking backwards to mislead the direction of travel; normally employed when the ground is soft and footprints are easily visible.
- Brushing track to leave minimal sign; for example, covering up leaves along the route or washing off mud after a river crossing. Commonly employed in track traps.
- Stone hopping to avoid footprints. After a stone has lain for some time, a wall of dirt and sand will build up around its base. If stepped on, this wall will crumble and appear as a gap between the stone and the surrounding ground.
- Crossing or walking in a stream.
- Fading out, where personnel are left behind or trail behind the main body to create misleading intervals between signs.

- Counter tracking refers to offensive techniques employed to harm the pursuing tracker.

7-128. Countertracking techniques include:

- Booby traps or improvised explosive devices:
  - Modern examples include tripwires and pitfalls.
  - Historical examples include punji sticks and bamboo whips.
- Ambush.
