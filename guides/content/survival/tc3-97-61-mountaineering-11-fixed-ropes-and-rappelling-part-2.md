# Fixed Ropes, Rappelling and Ascending (Part 2 of 2)

*TC 3-97.61, Chapter 11, continued.*

## Techniques

11-137. Conducting transition techniques without special equipment is not recommended. Not using the appropriate equipment will require mountaineers to transition alone and uninspected, utilizing a complex series of rigging steps while at risk of catastrophic fall. This situation should be avoided whenever possible and only attempted by mountaineers who are experienced in ascending and descending and load-transfer techniques.

## Special Equipment

11-138. Mechanical ascender—see paragraphs 11-15 through 11-18, page 277 for more description. Whereas these devices are not necessary for other fixed-rope movement situations, they should always be used when hanging transitions are planned.

11-139. Assisted braking device (ABD)—the ABD allows mountaineers to change direction without changing primary attachment to a fixed rope. It can be used with single-rope systems for belaying, rappelling, or capturing progress during ascending. The ABD uses a toothless cam to grip the rope when loaded. Rope can be pulled through it to capture progress or allowed to move in the opposite direction under the control of a release lever providing variable friction. ABDs are not recommended for use in a winter environment because they can get jammed up with snow and fail to work properly. (See figure 11-15, page 300.)

### Transitions on Single-Strand Systems

11-140. The following situations require or are likely to require ascent or descent transitions while hanging:

- Descent route finding or reconnaissance. Obstacles such as overhanging cliffs longer than a rope length can prevent continued descent. Mountaineers may need to descend numerous possible routes before finding the best way down. When dead ends are discovered, they must ascend back up before trying the next path down.
- Top-down fixed rope establishment when a mountaineer realizes that their path is a dead end and must ascend to re-route the rope on a different descent path.
- Confined space reconnaissance. This involves descending into wells, tunnels, or shafts to look for caches or evidence of enemy activity, and then ascending back to the surface after.

11-141. Whenever an ascent or descent transition is considered likely, then a single strand of fixed rope should be used. This allows the use of an ABD and mechanical ascender to establish the safest and most efficient transition possible.

### Rapid Ascent and Descent Method

11-142. This fixed-rope attachment method allows mountaineers to either travel up a rope and then transition to descent, or descend a rope and transition to ascent, all while remaining safely attached to the rope.

**Transitioning from Ascent to Descent**

11-143. Route the fixed rope through the ABD properly, then attach an ABD to the harness with a locking carabiner. Attach an ascender to the rope above it with a cordelette or other long material hanging from it. Use this material to create a fixed loop for a foot stirrup. (See figure 11-16.)

11-144. Ascending—to use the system, place one hand on the ascender and one on the free strand of rope. Place one foot into the foot stirrup. The foot used should be on the same side as the hand on the ascender. Pull down on the ascender while simultaneously standing up in the foot stirrup and pulling rope slack up through the ABD. Once the rope is tight, sit down and the ABD will grip the rope and capture progress. Slide the ascender back up to full arm’s length away and repeat. It is helpful to push both down and back with the leg when using the foot stirrup to keep body weight forward.

11-145. Ascending (variation)—the pull strand of rope can be redirected into a carabiner attached to the ascender to change to a downward pull as shown below; however, this can limit the amount of vertical gain with each movement. (See figure 11-17, page 302.)

11-146. Stopping on the rope—back-up stopper knots should be tied in the fixed rope close to the ABD whenever the mountaineer stops on the rope for a period. This is especially true if the rope is unweighted.

11-147. Transitioning—to transition to descent, the mountaineer only needs to disconnect the ascender and stow it.

11-148. Descending—descent by rappel can then be conducted using the already attached ABD. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for use. (See figure 11-16.)

**Transitioning from Descent to Ascent**

11-149. Route the fixed rope through the ABD properly, then attach it to the harness with a locking carabiner.

11-150. Descending—rappel using the ABD.

11-151. Stopping on the rope—back-up stopper knots should be tied in the fixed rope close to the ABD whenever the mountaineer stops on the rope for a period. This is especially true if the rope is unweighted.

11-152. Transitioning—to transition to ascent, the mountaineer only needs to attach an ascender with a hanging foot stirrup to the fixed rope.

11-153. Ascending—ascending can be conducted using the methods described in paragraph 11-154 or 11-155.

### Improvised Rapid Ascent and Descent Method

11-154. The same process for transitioning from ascending to descending, or descending to ascending, can be used if either piece of specialized equipment is not available (the ascender or ABD) using other commonly carried equipment. The ascender can be replaced with a prusik, and a carabiner attached to a fixed loop tied close with the tails. The tails are then used to create the foot stirrup. The ABD can be replaced with a belay or rappel device that can be used in autolocking mode. The device is attached to the Soldier’s harness in autolocking mode (see figure 10-20, page 266, for rigging techniques); then the brake strand is routed through a carabiner attached to an ascender (or prusik, as described above in this paragraph).

11-155. These improvised techniques require extensive practice before use, as they are somewhat more complex to rig and more difficult to operate during ascending. Soldiers employing them should use back-up attachments to the rope throughout the transition process to prevent a fall should a rigging mistake be made.

### Transitions on Two-Strand Systems

11-156. The following situations require an unplanned transition from rappelling to ascending while hanging on a double-strand system:

- The rappel ropes do not reach the ground, and there are no anchors available from which to establish another rappel pitch. Often, it is not possible to see if the ropes reach the ground due to overhanging terrain until it is too late. The rappeller must then ascend back to the top.
- Occasionally during multipitch rappelling, the lead mountaineer misjudges anchor possibilities below and rappels past the last viable anchor on the pitch. This requires ascending back up to that anchor before the rest of the group can rappel down and the next pitch can be rigged.

### Prusik Ascent Method

11-157. Mountaineers can transition to ascending from rappelling using the prusik ascent technique described in this chapter.

11-158. Descending—ensure stopper knots are tied in the rappel ropes. Use a basket hitch to extend the device and an autoblock for a hands-free backup. A shorter extension will make transitioning easier.

11-159. Stopping on the rope—when mountaineers realize they cannot reach the ground, they should engage their autoblock and look for anchors that can be used to establish another rappel pitch. If none are available, or they must ascend to reach one, they will need to transition to ascent.

11-160. Transitioning—to transition to ascent, mountaineers should tie a fixed loop a few feet below their autoblock with both rappel ropes and clip it to their harness using a locking carabiner. This serves as a backup for the whole transition process. The progress capture can then be tied around both ropes above the rappel device and attached to the harness with a locking carabiner. Once double-checked, mountaineers then release the autoblock and rappel until the progress captures prusik entirely supports their weight. The rappel device and autoblock can now be disconnected from the rappel ropes and stowed. The mountaineer then attaches the progress prusik to the rappel ropes below the progress capture and establishes a foot stirrup.

11-161. Ascending—ascending is conducted as with prusik ascent. Back-up knots should be tied every 10 to 15 feet and attached to the harness prior to untying the previous ones.

### Other Methods

11-162. Additional techniques for transitioning from rappelling to ascending exist. Some are more efficient but heavily dependent on specific circumstances and available special equipment. It is not feasible to cover them all in this training circular. Seek additional formal mountaineering training to expand upon the techniques covered above.

### Other Transition Situations and Techniques

11-163. Other more complex situations requiring ascent and descent transitions, such as climber rescue, are more involved and beyond the scope of this chapter. To obtain formal training or other specific transition-related problems that advanced military mountaineers may expect to encounter in the performance of their duties, consult with qualified mountaineering instructors.

11-164. Opportunities for formal training are available from one of the Army’s three mountain training organizations by unit request (see chapter 2).

11-165. An experienced level 3 mountaineer should supervise all sustainment and improvement training on these transition techniques.
