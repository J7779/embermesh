# Belaying and Rope Systems for Climbing (Part 1 of 2)

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 10. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

There is an inherent risk to moving in the mountains. Much of the terrain is high consequence, where a misjudged foothold, a loose rock, a slip, trip, or fall can kill. However, troops fighting in the mountains are often exposed to greater risk by not being able to move through the terrain. Examples exist throughout history. Modern examples include the assaults on Riva Ridge and Monte la Difensa in Italy, both examples of failed conventional assaults, where defensive positions in the heights were taken by assaults through more difficult terrain. More recently, the Indians retaking hilltops in the Kargil Conflict display all the same principles. Successful offensive operations in the mountains require troops who can move in difficult terrain and under poor conditions. The alternative is costly, often unsuccessful attacks where the defenders hold significant tactical advantage. To manage the risk involved with movement through these types of terrain, Soldiers must be trained in a variety of rope systems for climbing.

## Section I – Risk Mitigation

10-1. Fighting in the mountains requires exposure to mountain hazards. The mountain environment is defined by its hazards and difficulties. Often, the control measures used to address one hazard increase the exposure to another. Installing or using ropes to protect against a fall increases movement time and increases the exposure to compromise by enemy forces.

## Managing Risk

10-2. Managing risk in the mountains is a dynamic process, where the most appropriate measures for managing risk change with the terrain and situation.

10-3. Control measures target either the consequence or the likelihood of an incident. Protective equipment, such as a helmet to protect against rockfall, only reduces the consequence. The likelihood of being hit by rockfall is addressed by avoiding areas of loose rock and routing Soldiers so that they do not travel above one another.

## Reducing the Likelihood of a Fall

10-4. The mountaineer has several technical tools for managing fall hazards. However, while the consequence of a fall can be reduced, it cannot be eliminated. Even a short fall while rock climbing can cause severe injury. To manage the risk, target factors that will reduce the likelihood of a fall.

### Judgement

10-5. The safety of a mountaineer comes from the ability to read the terrain. Judge the rock, snow, or ice, and see where it can be climbed and where it cannot. This includes identifying features and weaknesses that can be linked together. Judgement is developed through time spent climbing in a controlled environment and through experience and mentorship.

### Technique

10-6. Soldiers must possess the physical ability to make the moves, use footholds, and maintain balance as each move is made. As with judgement, climbing technique is the product of extensive training.

### Load

10-7. Increased Soldier load rapidly increases the difficulty of climbing, even in easy terrain. Operations in the mountains are subject to the mindset that occupying high terrain requires more equipment for sustaining life than at lower elevations. However, to maintain maneuverability, the Soldier’s load must be reduced. Soldiers conducting offensive operations, reconnaissance patrols, or any maneuver in high-consequence terrain need to travel light.

### Footwear

10-8. Proper footwear will enhance the military mountaineer’s ability to move safely and efficiently through the rugged terrain found in the mountains. (See chapter 4 for footwear considerations.)

### Rope

10-9. Ropes are used to reduce the consequence of a fall. Ropes can be fixed in place to manage or reduce the consequences of a fall (see chapter 11) or can be used to provide belays (see section III in this chapter).

## Section II – Considerations for Moving Unroped

10-10. Moving unroped is the fastest and most efficient method of moving through terrain. It requires little equipment and is quiet. Team members can move together, making communication quiet and easy. In instances where the greatest hazard is not falling, but rock or icefall, impending severe weather threat, enemy observation, or failing to meet an operational timeline, moving unroped may be the safest way to move. (See figure 10-1.) Moving unroped in terrain above 3rd-class should only be done after thorough risk analysis has been conducted and speed has been identified as a critical control measure for managing overall operational risk.

*[Figure 10-1. Soldier moving unroped — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-11. Moving unroped should only be done in 4th-class terrain or lower. It should never be considered in 5th-class terrain.

10-12. Moving unroped exposes Soldiers to very high consequences in the event of a fall and could have catastrophic results including death. A rescue or body recovery in this terrain would likely become its own operation. Moving unroped should not be the default choice. The risk of a fall must be balanced against the other hazards. If the choice is made, there are measures to manage the risk.

- Target likelihood—maximize the use of every control measure to reduce the likelihood of a fall. Everyone in the team should have good movement skills. Loads should be reduced to the essentials. The team should be wearing appropriate footwear for climbing.
- Conservative route selection—the difficulty of the route must be within the comfort zone of the entire group. It is necessary to have the ability to adjust to a more conservative movement strategy, which requires harnesses to be worn and rope accessible.
- Route planning—use all available tools to build route plans. Often, climbing unroped goes badly not because a route is too difficult, but because a climber ends up off route in more difficult terrain.
- Pace—climb at a slow, deliberate pace. An easy pace allows full focus on the movement.
- Attention—keep attention focused on the climbing. Every foothold and handhold are critical. Stop often and assess the terrain ahead. Do not commit to features to which a Soldier cannot back off.
- Communication—a nontechnical risk management tool is coaching. Communicating a hazard focuses awareness on the hazard. During a movement, check in with group members and reassess their comfort with the risks at rest halts and decision points.
- Descent plan and tools—down climbing is more difficult than climbing up. Always have a multilayered descent plan and a rope and equipment to get down.
- For most units, there is limited terrain where climbing unroped may be appropriate. Average combat loads are heavy and issued combat boots are poorly suited for climbing. Limited steps of 3rd-class terrain, without exposure, are the upper limit for most units, and even then, should be assessed and managed carefully. Without individual mobility training and teams selected for their movement proficiency, there is a high risk of someone falling.

> **WARNING**
> - Never climb unroped in 5th-class terrain.
> - Climbing unroped in 4th-class terrain requires high skill, extensive experience, and superior confidence. Unroped climbing is only an option when the need to move quickly due to exposure to enemy or environmental hazards exceeds the risk of an unroped fall.
> - Unroped climbing is a high-risk activity that requires a deliberate risk mitigation process and commander approval.

## Section III – Belayed Climbing

10-13. **A belay is a system of using a rope as a backup in steep terrain to arrest a Soldier’s fall should one occur**. This section will discuss an overview of various strategies for using ropes and belays.

## Types of Belayed Climbing

10-14. When terrain is too dangerous to move unroped, a team will transition to belayed climbing. Belayed climbing is usually done in a party of two, with each climber tied into one end of the rope. One team member is always anchored and providing a belay for the other.

### Top Rope Climbing

10-15. Military mountaineers are first introduced to top rope climbing. Top rope climbing is where the rope is run through an anchor at the top of a climb and kept relatively snug to avoid longer falls. It is a training tool used to teach climbing technique. (See section IV for more information on top rope climbing.)

### Lead Climbing

10-16. As military mountaineers progress, they are later introduced to lead climbing. **Lead climbing is climbing a route while attached to the end of a rope to place devices in rock, ice, or snow for fall protection with a belayer located below**. When lead climbing, one climber leads climbs, placing protection and clipping the rope through it as they go. The belayer (sometimes referred to as the second) belays and is prepared to catch a fall. Lead climbers climb to another secure stance, build a solid anchor, and then belay second climbers as they climb to the new stance. Each length of climbing is called a pitch, which is a section of a climb that comprises the distance between two belay stations, which can be up to a standard rope length (60 meters) on easy ice and snow. In more broken rock, it may be just 10 to 15 meters, to minimize rope drag and aid communication.

10-17. Lead climbing provides security for both members of the party. Leaders accept more risk because they are climbing above their protection. Any fall will be well past the last piece of protection they placed before the rope begins to come tight, so it is still imperative that leaders have good movement skills. The second has a top rope unless the route traverses. The overall security of the team is good. The team always has a solid attachment, and the belays can be placed in sheltered stances, with solid anchors. (See section V for more information on lead climbing.)

10-18. Most climbers will protect themselves with belays in 5th-class terrain. In 3rd- or 4th-class terrain, in darkness or bad weather, belayed climbing is the most conservative strategy for protecting the team and provides the greatest margin of safety.

### Aid Climbing

10-19. If the climbing is too difficult for the leader, **aid climbing is a climbing technique in which standing on or pulling oneself up via devices attached to fixed or placed protection is used to make upward progress**. (See figure 10-2.) Aid climbing is when climbers use anchors and protection to support their weight as they climb. In a recreational setting, climbers on big walls may do extensive aid climbing, with webbing ladders called etriers (or aiders) that can be clipped to pieces of rock protection and climbed, and daisy chains or webbing tethers, to hang from. Aid climbing can also be as simple as pulling on pieces of protection to get through difficult sections of what would otherwise be a free climb.

10-20. Aid climbing is slow. It may take hours to climb one pitch. Aid climbing requires significantly more rock protection. An aid rack may have three times the equipment used by leaders to protect themselves. Advanced military mountaineers should be familiar with aid climbing but expect to improvise the tools to make it happen.

*[Figure 10-2. Aid climbing — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Simul-climbing (Running Belay)

10-21. **Simul-climbing is a belay technique involving two Soldiers climbing simultaneously while attached to the ends of a rope which runs through the fall protection**. It is a variation of belayed climbing, where leaders continue to climb rather than stopping to belay the second. Second climbers climb simultaneously, removing protection as they go. Leaders continue to climb while trying to keep a few pieces of protection between the two climbers at all times. If one falls, they may pull their teammate off their stance, but they will be caught eventually, counterbalanced through the protection with the other climber. Simul-climbing is faster than belayed climbing and useful for covering easy ground quickly. (See figure 10-3, page 240.) Simul-climbing provides more security than soloing. Climbers may find it necessary to shorten ropes between 20 and 30 meters to help with communication and prevent rope drag.

10-22. When both climbers are moving together, both share the consequence of a fall. Climbers should only use simul-climbing where a fall is unlikely and with partners of similar ability. If rockfall or icefall is a concern, it is not appropriate because the second is exposed to anything dislodged by the leader. Simul-climbing is best suited for easy alpine ridges where rocks fall off the feature. Simul-climbing is an appropriate technique for easy climbing, 3rd class through easy 5th class, with well-matched partners.

10-23. Simul-climbing is a technique used when the environmental or tactical situation presents a greater threat than the fall hazard. Simul-climbing sacrifices some level of fall protection to gain speed of movement.

*[Figure 10-3. Simultaneous climbing: two Soldiers climbing together with intermediate — illustration not reproduced.]*

> **WARNING**
> - Only highly experienced advanced or master military mountaineers are qualified to simul-climb. They should only simul-climb in terrain that is well below their ability level, when speed is needed due to enemy contact, or when environmental hazards outweigh the risk of a long-roped fall.
> - Simul-climbing is a high-risk activity that requires a deliberate risk mitigation process and commander approval.

### Short Roping

10-24. Short roping is a technique that may have some use in training military mountaineers. (See figure 10-4, page 242.) Short roping is used by more experienced mountaineers for protecting less experienced Soldiers in easy but exposed terrain. The climbing rope is shortened to just a few meters, and the leader holds the rope close to the other’s harness and the two move together. Short roping cannot arrest a fall. It manages risk by putting the senior mountaineer close to the other climber, where the senior mountaineer can coach and model. If someone slips, leaders pull the rope connected to their harness to prevent the slip from becoming a fall. The only anchor is the senior mountaineer’s stance. Short roping is appropriate only in terrain where leaders are confident climbing unroped and do not need their hands to climb.

10-25. Short roping has limited application for military mountaineers. Short roping requires practice and the leader’s full attention. In an operational environment, the military mountaineer’s focus must be forward, looking for upcoming hazards or enemy contact. All Soldiers operating in the mountains, especially those working in small teams, should have the mobility skills to move in easy terrain.

> **WARNING**
> - Only highly experienced master military mountaineers are qualified to short rope. They should only use short rope as part of a formal risk management process that identifies short roping as the appropriate risk mitigation method for the circumstances of the movement.
> - Short roping is a high-risk activity that requires a deliberate risk mitigation process and commander approval.

*[Figure 10-4. Short roping — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Short Pitching

10-26. Experienced leaders often combine short pitching with short roping as they manage less experienced Soldiers in 4th-class terrain. It is used when leaders need to use their hands to climb and cannot provide security with a short rope. Leaders place others in a safe position, and then scramble up the more technical section to a stance. From above, they can anchor or brace themselves to provide a belay as they climb. Often, because 4th-class terrain is more stepped, with plenty of ledges and stances, the leader can use simpler belay techniques and anchors than when rock climbing in 5th-class terrain and use a much shorter rope.

10-27. Most of the military mountaineer’s time is spent avoiding the steepest and most difficult terrain. Operationally, they are more likely to use terrain where short pitching and simul-climbing would be most efficient. The techniques used in short pitching are faster than belayed climbing in longer pitches. A shorter rope helps with communication and fast transitions.

10-28. Short pitching requires training and practice in techniques to become safe and efficient. These techniques may not be as commonly practiced as other belay techniques, but they still have a place in alpine terrain, with good stances, and in positions where the second will not be stuck, hanging from the belay.

10-29. Hasty belays may be necessary during short pitching where anchoring options that are more substantial are not available. (See figure 10-5.) These involve leaders using their own body, positioned in a good stance, as the anchor or partial anchor. Ideally, the leader is also attached to a back-up anchor of some type, even if it does not meet the usual standards for a climbing anchor. This technique should only be used by experienced advanced military mountaineers when operationally necessary or when no other secure anchoring or belaying technique is possible.

*[Figure 10-5. Hasty belay — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Shortening the Rope

10-30. When climbing in variable terrain, most pitches are short. This type of terrain is usually associated with 4th-class terrain. A full-length climbing rope is often too long and slows every transition with extra rope management. Teams may choose to carry shorter ropes if the terrain can be descended easily without long rappels, or they may choose to shorten their full-length climbing rope. Ropes are typically shortened for simul-climbing, short roping, and short pitching.

### Kiwi Coil

10-31. The Kiwi coil is the most common method of shortening a rope for climbing. (See figure 10-6, page 244.) After a climber ties into the end of the rope, the rope is coiled in a circular fashion, and then put over the shoulder of the climber. To secure this coil, a bight is run through the belay loop and around the coils. The bight is then tied off to the strand going to the other climber with an overhand on a bight, and a carabiner secures the bight to the harness. After the rope is shortened, team members can begin movement and adjust the length between them as necessary. Since the climber is tied into the end and the rope was shortened towards the other climber, it is easy to adjust the distance between climbers. This distance between climbers is usually dictated by the terrain. To lead a longer pitch, the coils can be removed from around the body. To stay closer to one another, the rope can be shortened.

*[Figure 10-6. Kiwi coil — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Operational Considerations

10-32. The various strategies and techniques described above for moving while roped will compromise speed and security. In a training environment, safety and conservative decision making must be the primary focus. As military mountaineers progress in their training, they may be exposed to alpine terrain above tree line. Weather and environmental hazards can pose a greater threat than falling. Each strategy for protecting the movement of the team is a compromise that should match the difficulty of terrain and the consequence of a fall. It should not expose the team to greater risk from another hazard. For combat operations, military mountaineers may need to compromise on protection and belays even further. Speed, stealth, and the ability to react are critical if enemy contact is expected.

## Section IV – Top Rope Climbing

10-33. A top rope is a belay in which both rope ends are on the ground and the rope runs up to the top of the cliff (or climb) and passes through a pre-established anchor point. This enables one Soldier to climb while the other Soldier belays. The belayer keeps the rope tight to prevent the climber from falling more than a short distance. This system also enables the belayer to lower the climber back down to the ground after completing the climb. (See figure 10-7 for an example of top rope climbing.)

*[Figure 10-7. Top rope climbing — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-34. A top rope is a training tool. It allows Soldiers to learn the fundamentals of tying in and belaying and to practice individual climbing techniques without the consequences of a long leader fall or unroped fall. It allows one instructor to coach and supervise both the climber and the belayer. It also facilitates learning the climbing technique because all participants can observe one another.

## Equipment Considerations

10-35. The UIAA develops safety and testing standards for mountaineering equipment. Equipment used for any mountaineering training should meet these standards as outlined in chapter 8 and appendix C.

### Helmets

10-36. Helmets will protect a Soldier from rockfall and debris. They also offer some protection from impacts in a swinging fall. Climbing helmets are lightweight and certified to standards set by the UIAA.

10-37. Most units do not have access to climbing helmets. The combat helmet, while not rated or designed for impact protection from rockfall or in climbing falls, is already issued to most Soldiers and provides adequate protection.

### Harness

10-38. There are many types of climbing harnesses. Harnesses must be made and tested to UIAA standards, and users must ensure that wear and use conforms to manufacturer recommendations. The high-angle mountaineering kit (HAMK) is issued to units where most Soldiers have no formal mountaineering training. The harness that was selected for the HAMK (see figure 10-8), is a harness with a single, reinforced tie-in portion. It is a simpler harness than most recreational climbing harnesses. It is one-size fits all and easy to put on and inspect. It has one gear loop on the left side, which can be used to rack equipment. When worn, it should be tight around the waist, with the leg loops snug enough to take some of the force while falling or sitting into the harness.

10-39. If harnesses are not available, tubular webbing can be used to make field expedient harnesses, although this is not preferred. Soldiers must ensure their seats are tied correctly, and military mountaineers acting as safeties must carefully inspect several more points. This introduces several more points for human error.

10-40. The rappel seat (Swiss seat) should not be used as a climbing harness as it is not designed for this purpose. In addition to being uncomfortable, it quickly loosens with repeated loading and unloading.

*[Figure 10-8. High-angle mountaineering kit harness tie-in — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Rope

10-41. Dynamic ropes are used for all climbing training. Dynamic ropes stretch and reduce the peak impact force felt by a climber in a fall. Falls while top roping should not generate as much force as a lead fall; however, if slack develops in the belay, such as from a stuck rope or slow belayer, there is potential for generating significant force.

10-42. Top rope training puts significant wear and tear on ropes. New climbers fall often, and because a rope may be used on the same climb for the entire day, there may be significant wear in the same sections of rope. Heavy-duty climbing ropes in the 10-mm range are recommended for top rope training. The 10.2-mm dynamic rope included in the ACTKs provides good durability.

## Preparation

10-43. Advanced and master military mountaineers should plan and supervise mountaineering training that involves roped climbing and rappelling. They advise the commander on training requirements, priorities, and equipment needs, as well as plan, organize, and conduct training.

### Site Reconnaissance

10-44. Visit the training area before the scheduled training date and conduct a rehearsal or walkthrough with key personnel. This will help proof the plan, identify hazards, and develop controls. It is also an opportunity to save time with set up by identifying anchors and cleaning the area of loose rock or other hazards ahead of time.

### Time Management

10-45. Ensure the training plan provides ample time to cover movement, set up, demonstrations, and training time. Extra time for contingencies is often needed.

### Communication

10-46. All key personnel should have reliable communication with each other to communicate changes to hazards or conditions or to coordinate the response to emergencies.

### Participant-To-Instructor Ratio

10-47. Ensure an adequate number of trained personnel are available to effectively supervise the given number of participants. In cases where there are only one or two trainers working with a much larger group, it may be necessary to limit the number of Soldiers being trained at one time.

### Organization or Placement of Key Personnel

10-48. Critical points must be identified, and key personnel must be placed or distributed to facilitate supervision of the training site.

### Emergency and Medical Evacuation Plans

10-49. Emergency and MEDEVAC plans must include special equipment and must consider the nature of the terrain in the training area. These plans must be rehearsed to ensure the viability of the plan.

## Training Site Consideration

10-50. The following are important considerations when managing a top rope training site.

### Ease of Access

10-51. The top of the cliff must be accessible to safely establish the anchors and hang ropes. Otherwise, a climb may have to be led prior to rigging. The bottom of the cliff must be easily accessible for the group and have adequate space to safely move around and store equipment.

### Length of Climbs

10-52. The length of the climbs must be less than half the length of the available ropes. Longer climbs can be used by joining two ropes together but is more complex and should be avoided, if possible, when working with beginners.

### Difficulty

10-53. Ensure the difficulty of climbing is appropriate for the Soldiers. A common mistake is to use climbs that are too difficult for Soldiers to learn the basic movement skills and establish confidence. It is best to have a variety of difficulty levels available.

### Distance Between Climbs

10-54. Climbs should be far enough apart so that climbers are not falling on or swinging into other climbers; however, they should not be so far apart that supervision and control are hindered. With climbs in close proximity, the instructor can often maintain adequate supervision of more than one climb.

## Training Site Anchors

10-55. Anchors at training sites are built stronger and more redundant than others used in climbing. A top roping anchor is used for long durations of time and is often out of the user’s sight. It either is loaded frequently, with climbers falling or as each climber is lowered back down to the ground. Extra care should be taken to avoid sharp edges and add padding where necessary.

### Location of Anchors

10-56. Anchors should be located directly above the climb. If the anchor is not above the climb, during a fall climbers will pendulum, swinging across the face until they are in line with the anchor. This is more pronounced the closer to the top the climber gets.

### Construction of Anchors

10-57. Anchors must be strong and redundant. Developed climbing areas often have bolted anchors at the top of climbs. A top roping anchor can be built with these with a cord as for other applications. (See figure 10-9.) Locking carabiners may be substituted for non-locking carabiners to ensure that the anchor is secure, even if it is checked only a few times during the training day. If anchors are unavailable at the cliff edge or are not in line with the top of the climb, static rope can be used to extend anchors out over the edge of the cliff and create a master point that is in line. Anchor ropes should be at least 9.5 mm in diameter.

*[Figure 10-9. Top rope climbing set up — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Inspections

10-58. Inspection is one of the most critical components of any technical mountain training event. Many accidents can be attributed to lack of inspection or instructor error during the inspection process. Level 2 or higher military mountaineers should inspect all rigging. The site should be organized in such a way that participants are unable to climb or proceed towards edges or dangerous terrain without first being inspected. Trainers must have a clear, logical sequence to follow when conducting inspections, whether it is anchor points, harnesses, tie-ins, rappel hook ups, or other. These should be conducted hands-on, not viewed from a distance.

## Top Rope Belay

10-59. Top rope training develops two main skills. One is the individual mobility skill of the climber. The other is belaying. Belaying is the most important for safe training.

### Positioning the Belayer

10-60. The best position to catch a fall is directly below the anchor. The belayer will be pulled straight up and not off-balance. However, belayers should be offset enough so that the climber will not fall on them. They should also be positioned so that the rope will not interfere with climbers as they climb the route or entangle the climber in the event of a fall. The belayer should be protected from hazards, such as falling rock or ice. They should be placed into a protected feature under a roof or bulge or offset out of the path of any falling objects.

### Anchoring the Belayer

10-61. Anchoring belayers can keep them from being pulled off their stance during a fall. It is not always necessary, but the decision not to anchor should be deliberate. It is recommended that the belayer is anchored when—

- Belayers are inexperienced and may lose control of the belay if they are pulled up.
- The climber severely outweighs the belayer.
- It is necessary to keep them from being pulled into hazards and obstructions.

### Back-Up Belayer

10-62. Using back-up belayers can achieve several things. Back-up belayers improve the overall security of the climbers, since it decreases the likelihood that a Soldier could be dropped to the ground. Back-up belayers help to convey the importance of the belay when learning is first taking place and keeps additional Soldiers occupied and focused when unit trainers are working with large numbers of participants. To provide a back-up belay, one Soldier stands just behind the primary belayer, on the brake-hand side, and keeps additional control of the rope while making sure not to interfere with the primary belayer’s movements.

### Belaying with a Tube-Style Device

10-63. A belay device acts as a brake on the climbing rope by applying friction to it. The device, plus the belayer’s braking hand, keeps tension on the rope in the event of a fall and helps protect the climber at the other end. It is an essential device for climbing safety.

10-64. Tube-style devices are the most used belay devices. (See figure 10-10.) They are lightweight, simple, and do not twist the rope. The brake position with a tube-style device is down, with the brake hand below the device, creating two sharp bends in the rope that provide the necessary friction. Attention must be paid to the belay technique, as there is very little friction outside of the brake position.

- Place a locking carabiner into the tie-in portion of the harness or into the belay loop. Thread a bight of the climbing rope through the tube slot of the device opposite the side of the keeper loop. Attach both the bight of rope and the keeper loop to the carabiner and lock the gate.
- To take in the rope, grasp the brake strand a few inches from the device and with the thumb pointed towards the device. Reach out and place the guide hand on the load strand with the thumb pointed up. Pull down with the guide hand and up with the brake hand simultaneously until the brake strand is approximately 1 foot away from the waist. Return the brake strand immediately to the brake position (below the device). Move the guide hand down to the brake strand just underneath the brake hand and slide the brake hand back to within a few inches from the device. Reach back up to the load strand with the guide hand and repeat.
- Never let go of the brake strand. The climber’s life depends on it.
- To lower the climber, remove all slack from the system. Hold the brake strand in the brake position with both hands, and feed rope slowly back through the device until the climber is safely on the ground. (See figure 10-11, page 252.)

### Belaying with a Munter Hitch

10-65. The Munter hitch is rarely used for top roping, but for units that are not fielded mountaineering kits, it provides an adequate belay. It has the advantage of requiring no extra equipment and maintaining a sufficient level of friction to stop a fall with the brake strand in a variety of positions. Its main disadvantage is that it twists and kinks the rope.

- Place a locking carabiner into the tie-in portion of the harness. Tie a Munter hitch in the climbing rope and clip it into the carabiner for either a right-hand or a left-hand brake. Lock the carabiner.
- To take in rope, grasp the brake strand with the brake hand a few inches away from the Munter hitch and with the thumb pointed up. Pull down with the guide hand and up with the brake hand simultaneously until the brake hand is just below the guide hand. With the guide hand, grasp both ropes just above the brake hand, and then slide the brake hand back down to its original place, a few inches from the Munter hitch. Reach back up the rope with the guide hand, and repeat.
- During a fall, the bight of the Munter hitch will pass from the brake side of the carabiner to the guide-hand side. The hitch creates enough friction for the belayer to easily hold the climber’s body weight. The brake position for the Munter hitch is with the brake strand and the load strand parallel.
- Never let go of the brake strand.
- To lower the climber back down, place both hands on the brake strand and feed the rope slowly back through the Munter hitch until the climber is safely on the ground. (See figure 10-12.)

**Note**. Do not put the brake hand too close to the Munter hitch. Fingers caught in the Munter hitch could cause a mountaineer to lose control of the brake, dropping the climber.

## Climbing Commands

10-66. Using standardized verbal climbing commands is a necessary safety protocol to ensure both members are ready before any actions are taken. Commands while top roping also teach proper communication before introducing lead climbing, where communication may occur over a much longer distance. Clear communication minimizes the chance of human error and helps to ensure safe operation of the belay. All commands must be preceded by the other Soldier’s first or last name, like a radio transmission, to gain the other Soldier’s attention. This is especially important at training sites where there are multiple top rope climbs set up nearby to avoid confusion.

10-67. Units and schoolhouses should develop their own SOPs to determine appropriate commands. Consider the following when choosing these terms:

- Avoid using terms that sound alike or may be confused with another word (for example, GO, SLOW, and WHOAH may sound like NO and vice versa).
- Avoid using words that are not definitive (for example, GOOD is a vague term that does not define any specific action).

10-69. Lead climbing is where a roped team has one climber take the lead while the other climber follows. While ascending the route, the lead climber periodically connects the rope to protection for safety in the event of a fall. This protection can consist of permanent bolts to which the climber clips quick draws, or removable protection, such as nuts and cams. One of the climbers below the lead climber acts as a belayer. The belayer gives out rope while the lead climber ascends and stops the rope when the lead climber falls or wants to rest.

10-70. In military mountaineering, the primary mission of a roped climbing team is to fix a route with some type of rope installation to assist movement of less trained personnel in the unit. This duty falls upon the most experienced climbers in the unit, usually working in two- or three-man groups or teams. Even if the climbing is for another purpose, roped climbing should be performed whenever the terrain becomes difficult and exposed.

10-71. Units or schoolhouses should develop their own SOPs for lead climbing commands and establish alternative means of tactically communicating actions while lead climbing. Examples of climbing commands are described in table 10-1 in the top rope section.

## Equipment Considerations

10-72. The ACTK contains the equipment necessary for lead climbing operations and includes mountaineering harnesses, chocks, and cams in a wide variety of sizes, locking and nonlocking carabiners, dynamic climbing rope, webbing and accessory cord, ascenders, and belay devices. The specific use of this equipment is described in chapters 8 and 9.

10-73. The snow and ice mobility kit (SIMK) is designed to outfit a platoon with safety and mobility equipment for operations in snow- and ice-covered mountainous terrain. This kit will be required if moving through this type of terrain and the specific use of this equipment is described in chapters 6 and 15.

### Helmets

10-74. As outlined in chapter 6, lighter weight or climbing-specific helmets offer several advantages and are recommended for lead climbers.

### Harness

10-75. The ACTK contains three basic mountaineering harnesses. These harnesses are a bare minimum, and lead climbing in any type of field expedient harness is not recommended. Lead climbing requires much more gear than general mountaineering tasks, so harnesses with additional features can increase efficiency and functionality. (See figure 10-13.) Typical features of harnesses well-suited for lead climbing are—

- Adjustable leg loops with buckles.
- Four or more gear loops designed to hold extra gear.
- Thick and durable padding increases comfort when spending a long time in the harness.
- Extra lumbar padding helps to stabilize the lower back and waist.

### Rope

10-76. Lead climbing can be protected by different rope systems. The UIAA develops safety and testing standards for mountaineering equipment. It sets standards for three different types of climbing rope: single ropes, half ropes, and twin ropes.

### Single Ropes

10-77. Single ropes are the most common type of climbing rope. Diameters can range from 8.9 to 11.1 mm.

### Half Ropes

10-78. Half ropes are a set of two ropes that are clipped separately into points of protection. This reduces friction and rope drag during long wandering routes and serves as a second rope for longer rappels. The disadvantage to half ropes is the increased complexity required for both the climber and belayer to manage these ropes separately. The diameters for half ropes can range from 8 to 9 mm. Half ropes are used for a party of two in wandering alpine terrain, on rock, or on ice.

### Twin Ropes

10-79. Twin ropes are also a set of two ropes, but both strands are clipped into each point of protection and managed as one rope. Diameters for twin ropes range from 7 to 8 mm. Twin ropes are primarily used on routes needing two ropes for rappels.

**Note**. Some ropes may hold more than one, or all, of these certifications, which can add to confusion during rope selection. Weight, versatility, and durability should be the primary considerations. In most cases, lightweight, single ropes offer the best balance of these factors.

### Protection

10-80. The amount of protection the lead climber brings depends upon the character of the climber and the skill of the lead climber. In easy alpine terrain, the rack may be just a few pieces of rock gear to protect exposed steps and make simple anchors. Protecting sustained, multipitch rock may require a rack of doubles, two sets of the entire size range of cams.

10-81. The presence of pre-established anchors or bolts found in some commonly used training areas are some of the biggest variables in protection. Climbing a route that has bolts placed for protection, known as sport climbing, provides security and allows the climber to carry less equipment. Sport climbing is useful for training purposes only. Sport climbing provides a safe way to introduce climbers to lead climbing and allows more experienced climbers to work on climbing terrain that is more difficult without having to place traditional protection.

10-82. In winter and alpine environments, ice screws must be incorporated.

### Rock Shoes

10-83. Rock shoes are specialized pieces of equipment that are only necessary when moving through difficult technical terrain. The practical application of this equipment is limited operationally but can greatly enhance climbing training and should be utilized when building Soldiers’ skillset in rock climbing. These specialized shoes provide more friction, support, and precise footwork, allowing the Soldier to move more effectively through the terrain. (See figure 10-14.) It is a common misconception that climbing is about upper-body strength. Climbing requires a lot of upper-body strength, but the legs typically propel the climber up the wall. The reason Soldiers fall while learning how to rock climb is typically some failure of their foot placement. While lead climbing, especially when learning, rock shoes are invaluable in managing risk by increasing the security of foot placements and reducing the likelihood of a fall.

## Belaying a Lead Climber

10-84. Belayers must first identify a place to stand when belaying that is free from rockfall or other objective hazards they can trip over or be pushed into. Belayers must also achieve an adequate stance or brace their body positions to help absorb the energy from a lead fall. They should consider attaching themselves to an anchor near the ground to help prevent being pulled off their stance and losing control of the belay. (See figure 10-15.) The climbing rope should then be back stacked, and both climber and belayer will tie into the appropriate ends of the rope.

10-85. The best position to catch a fall is directly below the first piece of protection. Belayers will be pulled straight up and not lose their balance. However, they should be offset enough so that the climber will not fall directly on them. The belayer should be positioned so that the rope does not interfere with the climber as they climb the route, and they do not become entangled in it in the event of a fall.

10-86. As the leader climbs, the rope is fed out ensuring that climbers have full freedom to move, and they are not hindered by the rope. Ensure that there is always a slight sag in the rope, but no more. Anticipate the climber’s movements and be prepared to feed rope out quickly when protection is being clipped. If belayers cannot see the climber, they must feel the climber through the rope. Unless the climber says otherwise, the belayer can slowly give slack on the rope as the climber proceeds on the route. The belayer should keep just enough slack in the rope so the climber does not have to pull it through the belay. If climbers want a tighter rope, they can call for it. If belayers notice too much slack developing in the rope, they should take in the excess rope quickly. It is the belayers’ responsibility to manage the rope, whether by sight or touch, until the climber tells them otherwise.

10-87. To catch a fall, immediately drop the guide hand down next to the brake hand and lock the rope off in the brake position. Bend the knees slightly to help absorb the impact.
