# Moving over Snow and Ice: Snowshoes, Skis, Sleds and Ice Crossing

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 6. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Moving over Snow

6-1. Snow affects movement in many different ways. Heavy snow cover slows movement, either cross-country or on roads. Snow cover also blankets many terrain features and hides obstacles. The effect of snow on movement varies greatly with its depth and physical characteristics.

6-2. Soldiers on foot cannot travel well over flat terrain or roads when snow is deeper than approximately 1 foot (30 cm). However, when snow cover is deeper, using skis or snowshoes improves movement. (See table 6-1.) Flotation on snow is equally as important as the weapon. Do not allow Soldiers to become separated from their flotation. Arriving at the destination soaked in sweat is a failure. Set a pace that generates just enough heat to be comfortable.

**Table 6-1. Comparison of skis and snowshoes**

| SKIS | SNOWSHOES |
|---|---|
| Faster | Slower |
| Less work | More work |
| Harder to learn | Easier to learn |

### Skis and Snowshoes

6-3. Skis provide greater moving speed, particularly over cleared trails. When troops are mounted on skis and towed behind small unit support vehicles or similar vehicles, it is known as skijoring. This is an effective means for rapid cross-country movement. In heavy vegetation, skiing is always more physically taxing than snowshoeing.

6-4. Utilizing snowshoes is the minimum that Soldiers in cold regions should learn. This skill is quickly learned but some technical points need to be addressed. The military snowshoe is light, durable, and provides flotation in snow. (See figure 6-1 on page 70.)

*[Figure 6-1. Snowshoes — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-5. Army snowshoes consist of a hard plastic deck with steel crampons and bars to aid with traction. They are approximately 22 inches long and 8 inches wide (approximately 56-cm long and 20.3-cm wide) without flotation tails, and weigh less than 4 pounds (1.8 kg). Other characteristics of this snowshoe include the—

- Shovels (1): the upturned front portion of the snowshoe is designed to help it ride toward the surface of the snow.
- Windows (2): the opening of the snowshoe that allows the toe of the boot to pivot for added traction, giving a natural foot movement while walking.
- Decks (3): this is the middle, flat portion of the snowshoe.
- Bindings (4): secure the snowshoe to the boot.
- Elevator bars (5): are metal bars that raise a Soldier’s heel in order to decrease the angle of the wearer’s foot when climbing steep terrain.
- Tails (6): this is the elongated rear of the snowshoe designed so the snowshoe tracks in a straight line while walking.
- Flotation tail(s) (7): are detachable additional decking at the rear of the snowshoe to provide greater flotation in powder snow.
- Crampons (8): are the metal structure under the deck, directly under the boot, that aids in traction on ice and hard-packed snow (as seen from the underside of the snowshoe.)
- Steel bars (9): are the serrated edges along both sides of the shoe that aids with traction on ice and hard-packed snow (as seen from the underside of the snowshoe).

6-6. To properly fit the military snowshoe:

- Lay the snowshoes on the ground with the running end of the heel strap to the outside of each foot.
- Kneel down and place one boot underneath the toe straps until the front of the boot is one-half to two-thirds of the way into the window.
- Starting with the front toe strap, tighten each strap until it is snug against the boot, but take care not to cut off circulation or overtighten to the point of breaking the strap.
- Secure the heel strap to the boot just above the heel lug so that the running end of the heel strap is on the outside of the boot to prevent tripping.
- Repeat with the other snowshoe.

6-7. To move on snowshoes:

- On flat or rolling terrain—
  - Walk with feet apart slightly wider than normal to prevent stepping on or catching the other snowshoe.
  - Raise the toe of the snowshoe just high enough with each step to clear the snow as the tail slides over it.
- On gentle slopes, climb straight uphill or descend directly downhill.
- On steep terrain—
  - Ascend by traversing and packing a level trail while going up the slope.
  - Descend using the same traversing technique.

6-8. To change directions on snowshoes, make a step turn or kick turn:

- To make a step turn—
  - Move the outside snowshoe a few inches toward the desired direction of travel.
  - Move the other snowshoe until it is parallel to the first one.
  - Continue the process until facing the desired direction of travel.
- A kick turn is used to change direction in tight or steep terrain and is done by:
  - Lifting one foot and placing the tail vertically on the ground next to the window of the stationary shoe.
  - Maintaining balance and allowing the snowshoe to fall backwards so the feet are pointing in opposite directions.
  - Not placing snowshoes on top of each other.
  - Bring the other snowshoe around and place it next to the first one.

6-9. Additional considerations when wearing snowshoes include:

- Using ski poles as a balancing aid, especially when carrying heavy loads or moving uphill:
  - Size the ski poles so that the length is approximately up to the name tape.
  - Poles are angled slightly rearward, providing an added boost when moving forward.
  - Hands are low without squeezing the pole grips: the wrists rest on the lanyards.
- When stepping over obstacles, place snowshoes parallel to the obstacle and straddle it one leg at a time.
- If a large obstacle cannot be avoided, step directly on it with as much contact as possible.
- Try not to bridge depressions with snowshoes since this places undue stress on the frames and can possibly cause breakage.
- The lead Soldier breaks the trail and works harder than those that follow, so periodically rotate trail breakers.
- When carrying a weapon, attach the sling to the rear sling swivel and the slip ring where the handguards attach to the receiver:
  - Hang the weapon over the neck and fire side shoulder with the muzzle down.
  - The weapon can be placed behind the canteen on the firing side hip to keep it out of the way while using ski poles.
  - Alternatively, attach the sling at the slip ring and the small of the butt stock and hang in the same manner.
  - Another method is to use a commercially available three-point sling.
- While donning snowshoes or adjusting bindings, kneel down to be closer to the ground to avoid bending at the waist:
  - Kneeling brings the Soldier closer to the bindings.
  - If possible, do not wear the pack as it causes imbalance and ratchets up frustration levels.
  - Should a fall occur while wearing the rucksack, get out of the pack before trying to stand up again.

6-10. In a survival situation when skiing is not recommended and snow is loose and deep (see table 6-2), improvise equipment for travel if. Make snowshoes of willow or other springy wood using a wood separator and thong, wire, or cord.

### Moving the Ahkio

6-11. It is easiest to pull an ahkio with snowshoes. However, if using skis, wax the bottom runners to provide more grip than glide on the snow. Climbing skins should also be utilized. Secure the following equipment:

- Eighteen-foot tow rope consisting of two sections:
  - One is 6 feet (approximately 1.83 meters) and the other 12 feet (3.65 meters).
  - There is a snap hook or carabiner at each end of the rope and a metal attachment ring in the middle.
- Three 6-foot (1.83 meters) traces with each trace having a snap hook or carabiner at each end.
- One 50 to 60 meter (approximately 4.5 to 5.5 yards) climbing rope.
- Four harnesses.

6-12. If wearing a rucksack, put it on now. Drape the harness over the head and fasten the metal buckle in front. The D-shaped ring at the rear of the harness is the connection point for pulling, and the ring in front is for braking. If the rucksack rides below the connection point, remove the harness and make attachments directly to the rucksack. Additional considerations include:

- Movements of the ahkio pulling team are coordinated by one team member.
- Ahkio pulling teams are rotated frequently to avoid exhaustion.
- The trail should be broken by a trail breaking team prior to pulling sleds, but consider that—
  - Heavy brush and forests can make pulling a sled almost impossible.
  - The trail-breaking squad can drag their own ahkio in less difficult snow conditions, but the platoon leader may elect to shift their ahkio to the trail squad.
  - The company commander may elect to have the company reserve pull the ahkios for the lead platoon.
- Pullers should stay close to the 18-foot (5.49 meter) tow rope:
  - This helps pack the snow allowing the sled to slide easily without digging in.
  - One technique is to keep one hand on the main line.

### Hauling a Scow Sled (AHKIO)

6-13. There are several different ways to haul a scow sled (ahkio). From the basic configuration, the ahkio can be pulled using specific formations for sustained downhill movement, sustained uphill movement, traversing a hill, and during traveling and overwatch movements.

6-14. To create the basic hauling configuration—

- Hook the 18-foot (approximately 5.5 meters) tow rope to the front of the sled with the 6-foot (1.8 meter) section closest to the sled and the end is in the No. 1 position.
- Hook one of the 6-foot (1.8 meter) traces to the ring on the tow rope for the No. 2 position.
- Hook one of the 6-foot (1.8 meter) traces directly to the sled for the No. 3 position (see figure 6-3).

*[Figure 6-3. Basic configuration for hauling a scow sled — illustration not reproduced.]*

- The No. 2 and No. 3 positions should be on opposite sides of the rope, so they do not step on each other’s snowshoes or skis, as the No. 3 position provides the most power in the pulling effort.
- Hook the remaining trace to the rear of the sled in the No. 4 position:
  - The No. 4 position provides braking power to keep the ahkio from running into the pullers if a slight downhill is encountered.
  - The No. 1 through No. 3 positions ensure they slow their movement on the downhill so the No. 4 position is not yanked off their feet.
- Pull in unison.

6-15. For more sustained downhill (steep) scow sled movement, allow gravity to move the sled by controlling the speed of the sled from the rear. (See figure 6-4.) To achieve this—

- The No. 4 position can hook directly to the ahkio or the rear of the No. 1 harness.
- If a Soldier hooks to the sled, care is taken not to step on the Soldier in the No. 3 position.

*[Figure 6-4. Sustained downhill (steep) configuration for hauling a scow sled — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-16. When terrain gets so steep that Soldiers cannot brake effectively, a short rope can be tied to the two D-rings on the side walls by passing the rope under the sled. The sled can also be belayed using the 200-foot (60.9 meter) rope.

6-17. When travelling on a sustained/steep uphill, the Soldier at the No. 4 position can hook directly to the ahkio or the front of the No. 1 position harness. Keep in mind—

- If hooking to the sled, care is taken not to step on the Soldier in the No. 3 position.
- If hooking to the harness in front, the trail must be straight to accommodate the additional length (see figure 6-5).

*[Figure 6-5. Sustained uphill (steep) configuration for hauling a scow sled — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-18. To traverse , a hill with a scow sled—

- Move the No. 2 Soldier from the ring position to the uphill D-ring on the ahkio.
- Soldier No. 3 remains hooked to the sled but moves to the uphill side of the tow rope.
- Soldier No. 2 can hook the trace to the opposite D-ring to keep the ahkio from rolling down the fall line, if needed (see figure 6-6).

*[Figure 6-6. Traverse a hill with a scow sled — illustration not reproduced.]*

*[Figure 6-7. Basic configuration for movement of the rifle squad — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-20. The lead fire team is responsible for—

- Providing security.
- Breaking the trail.
- Removing bigger impediments to movement.
- Navigation.
- Rotating into the hauling team.

6-21. Responsibilities of the trail fire team (hauling team) include:

- Maintaining noise and light discipline.
- Ensuring the sled moves without damage or loss.
- The brakeman provides rear security.

6-22. The squad leader is responsible for—

- Noise and light discipline.
- Navigation.
- Ensuring the fire teams are rotated into hauling positions
- Other tactical considerations.

6-23. If the squad is to operate independently, consider not hauling the ahkio. This allows the squad to move quietly and faster while accomplishing their mission. Careful analysis is given before attempting to operate in extreme cold without heat and shelter.

6-24. If the platoon is moving in bounding overwatch, consider having the company reserve bring the ahkios forward or consolidating them until the tactical situation allows for their retrieval. The ahkio is useful in transporting—

- Casualties.
- Crew-served weapons.
- Resupplies.

6-25. The ten-person tent is very useful as a casualty collection point. Casualties can be stabilized in a warm area before evacuation to a higher level of care.

## The Challenge of Land Navigation

6-26. In some cold-weather environments, accurate up-to-date maps may not be available, and landmarks can be few or nonexistent. When the terrain is snow-covered, one piece of ground looks like the other. Whatever the circumstances, find a way—if not by map reading, then by some other means of navigation.

6-27. Only poor roads may exist in some areas. These are of little use in cross-country navigation. If large-scale maps exist, they may be unreliable. Long nights, falling or blowing snow, and fog limits visibility in winter. Tracks and landmarks may be erased by heavy snowfall. Accurately identify landmarks via ground and air observation to prevent mistakes. Conditions of daylight are different from those found in more temperate regions. In winter, there are long nights and short days. The low angle of the sun can temporarily hinder visibility.

6-28. Magnetic disturbances may be common, making compass readings unreliable. Use several compasses at the same time during movements. Magnetic declinations can be extreme and vary considerably at close points. Many small lakes, ponds, and creeks are found that are not on the map. The size and location of waterways can vary from year to year and from season to season. They cannot be relied on to pinpoint a position. However, large lakes and rivers can be used as important aids to navigation and movement. The Global Positioning System (GPS) may be unreliable as batteries are quickly drained due to the extreme cold and high latitudes. (See TC 3-25.26 for more information.)

**Note**. Do not rely on one form of navigation when moving in extremely cold weather.

### Distance and Direction

6-29. When traveling from one point to another, it is necessary to know the distance between the two locations before starting out, then plot the journey accordingly. In temperate zones, pacing is the simplest method of measuring distance. In cold-weather areas, snow cover and the use of skis and snowshoes make pacing a challenge. Repeatedly practice this type of pacing. Assign several Soldiers within the unit the job of tracking their pace count, which is then averaged.

6-30. The best method for accurate ground measurement is for two Soldiers to use a piece of salvage field wire or 50-meter (approximately 54.5-yard) long parachute cord. The lead Soldier marches off in the desired direction, trailing the wire behind. The second Soldier signals the first by jerking the wire when the first has reached 50 meters (approximately 54.5-yards) and then moves forward to the lead Soldier’s position. The first Soldier marks the spot and continues forward for another 50 meters (approximately 54.5-yards). This is repeated until the desired distance is reached. Although very slow, this method is accurate in flat tundra areas.

6-31. If no other method of measurement is available, the length of time spent in steady progress along a route may be used to estimate the distance traveled. The accuracy of this method depends on how well the marching speed over all types of terrain on foot, skis, or snowshoes is known.

### Determining Direction

6-32. Using the compass is the most common way to determine direction. However, the magnetic field becomes weaker when the magnetic pole becomes closer.

6-33. Compasses can easily be affected by other objects. It is important to make sure that the compass is not compromised by iron fragments in the ground, vehicles, or metal objects on the Soldier. As in the pace count, several Soldiers within the unit should have their compass out, monitoring the direction.

### Navigation with the Sun and Stars

6-34. During daylight hours, there are two ways to determine direction by the sun. At noon in the northern hemisphere, shadows fall to the north of objects. In the southern hemisphere, shadows fall to the south of objects. Estimate direction by using a watch and the sun.

6-35. In northern latitudes, the hour hand is pointed toward the sun. An imaginary line halfway between the hour hand and 1200 points to the south. In southern latitudes, the procedure is different. Point the 1200 toward the sun. An imaginary line halfway between the hour hand and 1200 points to the north.

6-36. The North Star can be used to determine true north. (See figure 6-8.) This star is easily found by its position relative to the Big Dipper. In latitudes under 70 degrees when traveling north, the North Star makes a good steering mark since its bearing is usually only 1 degree from true north and is never more than 2 1/2 degrees away. In higher latitudes above 70 degrees, the North Star is too high in the sky to indicate good direction.

**Note**. Do not use the moon or stars as guides because they travel in a circle through the night.

*[Figure 6-8. Determining direction with the North Star — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-37. South of the equator, locating the Southern Cross constellation helps find the general direction South and any other direction. This group of four bright stars is shaped like a cross that is tilted to one side. The two stars forming the long axis, or stem, of the cross are called pointers. From the foot of the cross, extend the stem five times its length to an imaginary point. This point is the general direction of South. From this point, look straight down to the horizon and select a steering point. (See figure 6-9.)

### Navigation by Dead Reckoning

6-38. Dead reckoning consists of plotting and recording a series of courses from a known point, each measured for distance and direction. These courses lead from the starting point to the destination. This lets the navigator determine their position at any time by following the plot or by comparing the actual position on the ground to the plotted course. All the details are estimated and recorded.

6-39. Various items of equipment can be used when dead reckoning. They are:

- Maps are used for choosing and plotting the route taken as the march progresses.
- Aerial photographs are usually used, in addition to maps that are vertical photos taken during the same season in which they are being used.
- The compass is used as the main direction-finding instrument.
- The protractor is necessary in dead reckoning for plotting direction and distance on the map, aerial photo, or chart: remember to convert magnetic azimuth to grid azimuth.
- The purpose of a route card is to outline the plan of the proposed march by—
  - Keeping a mandatory log.
  - Recording the start of the mission.
  - Recording the distance and direction.
- The log documents are the traveling position at any time in relation to the—
  - Planned route.
  - Destination.
  - Starting point.

6-40. Knowing the starting and destination points, and after examining the map or aerial photo for the best route, prepare an unofficial route card. (See table 6-3.) This describes the distance and direction of each leg of the proposed route.

6-41. As the march continues, keep a careful record of every bearing taken, the distance travelled, and record this in the log. There may be a change to the planned route due to terrain or enemy action. All necessary changes are made using the map or aerial photo as a guide and recorded in the log.

### Navigation Tips

6-42. The navigator keeps the compass warm to speed up recording the position. A steering mark is an object or terrain feature on the line of azimuth the navigator can get a bearing on. If no steering mark exists, another Soldier can be used instead. The team using the pace count cord (as previously described) can be used. The navigator sends the other Soldier out on a bearing for a distance. This Soldier then stops and faces about. The lead navigator directs them left or right by hand and arm signal, or via radio. The unit then moves to that location. This is used in conjunction with the pace count cord in conditions of diminished visibility, wide open flat areas, and very dense forested areas where terrain features cannot be seen. These methods slow the unit to a crawl and can result in cold injuries if precautions are not taken.

6-43. When visibility is poor, only close-in steering marks are available. Under these conditions, try to pick up steering marks along the correct azimuth. This can be done accurately only when the route to each steering mark follows a straight line. Take compass checks often to ensure that the correct azimuth is being followed. Set the compass for a night course. Depending on the area of the world, other signs can be used. For example, in the barren and semi-barren lands of eastern Canada, snowdrifts point to the northwest and windblown trees to the southeast due to prevailing winds. In Scandinavia, scratches on rocks and cliffs caused by the glacial period (ice age) run northwest to southeast.

### Route Planning

6-44. In addition to the normal considerations regarding the tactical situation, leaders can use a map or imagery reconnaissance when selecting a route across cold, snow-covered terrain. The following should be considered:

- Going around terrain features may be faster than going over them:
  - Check the contours and select a route.
  - Choose a route with the minimum amount of ascent and descent time.
- Pay attention to the contour intervals on maps:
  - A 20-meter (approximately 22 yard) interval can hide a lot of very significant relief.
  - Large intervals tend to soften the terrain.
  - A sharp arrowhead reveals that a ridge is very narrow across the top, but a large contour interval can soften the appearance.
- Note the time of day, month, and year the imagery is taken, along with:
  - The time of year and time of flight on visual imagery that affects the way shadows highlight the lay of the land.
  - The season of the year, such as if it is the height of summer or middle of winter.
  - A photo taken in the winter may have significant features covered in snow.
  - Shadows that can be different in summer and winter, and at different times of the day.
- Old trail networks are more prevalent than expected:
  - They can be nothing more than a footpath through the woods or a single lane vehicle path.
  - Areas around population centers usually have trails for many miles in the surrounding countryside.
  - These may not be on any map but can show up on readily available imagery.
  - Control of trails has proven to be decisive in the past.
- Check the marginal information with particular attention to the data and drawing dates:
  - It is important that the data on the map matches the data entered into the GPS unit.
  - Most GPS default to WGS84, but many of the more remote regions of the world have not been updated to this new data.
  - The date of the map affects its accuracy, especially regarding built-up areas.
- GPS is affected by terrain masking in deeper valleys or draws. Latitude also affects the GPS due to fewer available satellites or being close to the horizon.
- Old river channels can also be used:
  - Vegetation may be less dense or easier to move through.
  - Keep in mind that rivers in cold regions tend to meander a lot, adding more distance.
- Vegetation can be estimated by color.
  - Dark green areas tend to be mature trees such as white spruce, pine, birch, and cottonwood.
  - These are commonly found immediately adjacent to river channels.
  - They may have an understory of dense brush.
  - Light green areas tend to be lower brush such as black spruce, alder, willow, and smaller birch.
- Wide open areas with little or no relief are addressed, as navigation can get difficult without landmarks.
- Consider where the sun is and how the shadows fall:
  - The south side of open areas have longer shadows.
  - These shadows help to conceal tracks.
- Consider whether Soldiers are on skis or snowshoes:
  - How proficient are they?
  - Are they more capable of negotiating the terrain along the route with one or the other?
  - Will Soldiers be carrying heavy rucksacks or pulling sleds?
  - What is the temperature expected to be during movement?
- If any vehicles are attached to the unit, what type of terrain and snow depth are they capable of negotiating?
  - How are the tracks going to be camouflaged and does this need to be done in barren areas, or areas above the tree line since tracks may be difficult, if not impossible to conceal.
  - Is the route feasible during conditions of limited visibility?
  - Does the route cross any potential avalanche areas?
- What obstacles can be anticipated?
  - Will streams and other bodies of water be sufficiently frozen to support troops and vehicles?
  - Do snowplowed roads perpendicular to the route have high banks of plowed snow?
  - Is the water level in streams so low that Soldiers have to negotiate high banks?

### Open Terrain

6-45. In open terrain, it is best to break only one set of tracks. Aircraft or elevated OPs can spot several tracks more easily than a single set of tracks. Follow the tree line as much as possible as this aids in concealment from the ground and helps hide tracks from the air. The sun stays in the southern sky for most of the winter. The very long shadows on the southern side of open areas can be used to conceal tracks.

### Tundra

6-46. In tundra areas of the far north there are very few forested areas. Walking through areas with knee-high brush conceals trails. Areas where large herds of moving caribou can also conceal tracks. River corridors may have some mature trees to help with concealment, but further north these dwindle. Assume that the enemy knows the locations of these tree lines and has them under observation. Relying on low ground such as creek beds to mask movements can help conceal movement.

### Hills and Mountains

6-47. When negotiating hills or mountains, use gentle traverses to ascend or descend. This makes it less fatiguing on Soldiers so they can fight after reaching the objective. As far as avalanche prone slopes are concerned, avoid them at all costs. Very specialized training is required to even come close to negotiating them safely. Some warning signs to be aware of in snow-covered mountainous terrain are taught in the stationery avalanche awareness class.

### Water Routes

6-48. Water routes are generally excellent for navigation. They can be superb avenues for movement after freeze-up. Very detailed reconnaissance is required before attempting to move on water routes. When on water routes, treat them as an open area and stay close to the shore. This helps to conceal Soldiers and tracks. Overflow is a condition common to most bodies of water where the water flows onto the ice. General rules for selecting routes on ice include—

- The outside bends of rivers generally have thinner ice due to the faster current.
- Edges of lakes generally have thicker ice as water freezes from outside to inside and top to bottom.
- Inlets, outlets, converging channels, and tributaries are high flow areas prone to thin ice.
- The ice in the middle of lakes and rivers tends to be thinner and the downwind side of lakes tend to have thicker ice.

> **DANGER**
>
> Failure to conduct a very thorough reconnaissance of a route over ice can lead to loss of life and equipment.

- Steam coming off the surface of the snow indicates that there is open water:
  - Isolated patches of frost form on the trees adjacent to water ways.
  - This indicates that there was open water there and the ice around that area may be thin.
- Frost flowers form due to water vapor condensing on ice or snow:
  - This comes from exposed water.
  - When an open lead freezes over, these flowers form on the ice.
  - This ice is quite thin but may be strong enough to support a load of snow, concealing its presence.
  - Avoid any depression in the snow while on a water route.
- As the winter progresses, water levels under the ice begin to drop:
  - This sometimes leaves an air space where the ice is no longer supported by water and may collapse.
  - Check for hollow sounds and the sound of flowing water.

| UH-60, CH-47 | 15 inches | 18 inches | 80 m (87.48 yards) |
| Legend HMMWV—high mobility multipurpose wheeled vehicle; m—meter(s); SUSV—small unit support vehicle |  |  |  |

**Notes**. A rule of thumb for armored vehicles: 16 inches (0.4 meters) of waterborne ice supports 16 tons, and each additional inch supports one additional ton. This does not apply to ice thicknesses under 16 inches. For example, 3 inches (7.6 cm) of ice does not support 3 tons (approximately 6,614 pounds) of weight. If ice is not supported by water (waterborne) because the water level has dropped, it is too weak to support heavy loads. If temperatures are above 14ºF (approximately –10 ºC), add 25 percent to all required ice thicknesses.

### Water Route Reconnaissance

6-50. After a tentative route is selected, an ice check is necessary. At least two Soldiers should do this. Equipment consists of a 25-meter (approximately 27 yard) rope, an auger or heavy ice chipper, and a measuring stick with an angled end. Both Soldiers wear personal flotation devices. If the tactical situation permits, fighting loads should be dropped or at least lightened to just a weapon. Gas-powered augers are faster but are loud and require two-stroke fuel. Tactically, hand-powered augers are best. Chippers and axes are excruciatingly slow. To reconnaissance in cold weather—

- The lead Soldier ties off to the end of the rope with a bowline just under the armpits:
  - If the rope is around the waist there is the possibility of being folded in half by a current and having the head submerged.
  - The other Soldier keeps the rope fairly taut.
- The lead carefully moves onto the ice no more than 10 meters (approximately 11 yards) from the shore, 1 to 2 meters (3.28 to 6.56 feet) downstream from the route (to prevent weakening the route for heavier loads) and drills the first hole:
  - Take note of the amount of resistance against the auger.
  - The water should flow up out of the hole and be approximately 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) below the surface of the ice.
  - This shows that the ice is waterborne.
  - Measure the thickness of the ice by inserting the stick and hooking the angled portion under the ice shelf.
  - It helps if the shaft has inch or centimeter marks on it.
- Both Soldiers move to the next area not more than 10 meters (1.1 yards) away: repeat the process.
- The last hole is no closer than 10 meters (1.1 yards) from the shore.
- Mark the lane.
- A speed limit of 10 mph (approximately 16 kph) is enforced if vehicles are to use the lane, especially when close to the shore landing:
  - Vehicles set up a pressure wave and can blow out the landing.
  - The heavier and faster the vehicle, the higher the risk.

### Night Movements

6-51. Leaders manage movements during periods of limited visibility to minimize the likelihood of breaks in contact and reduce the amount of time required to regain contact. In temperate regions, this is usually an annoyance that slows movement. In the cold-weather environment, long halts can produce cold-weather injuries. For this reason, the route should follow the easiest terrain possible.

6-52. The route should also be well marked, and guides placed where appropriate. Maximize the use of reconnaissance teams, well-defined travel lanes, and checkpoints. Reconnaissance teams may also be tasked to put up heated shelters along longer routes.

### Trail Breaking Procedures

6-53. Roads and trails depicted on maps or imagery may be completely obscured by heavy snowfall or drifting. A unit can completely miss these features if not careful. Mitigate by—

- Knowing the exact location at all times.
- Plotting azimuths to cross or join roads and trails at easy to identify points.
- Maintaining an accurate log of distance travelled to know when there should be crossing, or a joining of roads or trails.
- Looking at the tops of the trees in forested areas: there may be a definite open space in the canopy.
- Looking at the sides of the trees as well.

6-54. Dismounted consumes a considerable amount of time, effort, and energy. The first Soldier in line quickly becomes fatigued depending on the load carried and depth of snow. One-third of the unit is allocated to this task. The trail breaking party departs 1 hour earlier for each kilometer to be traveled. The leader is responsible for—

- Setting a course that maintains tactical security to the larger unit while maintaining ease of movement:
  - The general trace should be set using a map and aerial imagery.
  - Every effort should be made to stay in mature forests and avoid areas that are choked with brush.
  - These areas make movement excruciatingly slow, and the noise generated by clearing a route or just moving through it can easily alert the enemy.
- Ensuring the trail marking SOP is adhered to and side trails or back-tracking trails are cordoned off to prevent units from taking a wrong turn.
- Providing guides, if necessary.
- Ensuring pioneer tools are available:
  - The tools in an ahkio group are enough for a squad to cut a trail.
  - If water routes are to be used, additional tools such as augers and depth sticks are brought along.

6-55. The following is the procedure for the lead squad:

- The squad leader designates the direction, and the lead Soldier begins moving, establishing the initial track.
- The second in line does not step into the firsts’ tracks but steps opposite and flattens the tracks.
- The third and fourth Soldiers offset their steps left and right by at least one snowshoe width:
  - This widens the trail to allow the ahkios to be brought along.
  - Should it become necessary, these Soldiers are also the cutters.
  - Each one carries a machete to clear brush on the sides of the trail.
  - Care is taken to lop branches close to the main trunk, so they do not become spears.
- The squad leader’s primary focus is navigation:
  - A march table should be kept for tracking the unit location.
  - GPS should not be solely relied upon, as batteries are easily drained in the cold.
  - The squad leader ensures the two teams are rotated, as necessary.
- Trail team fills low spots with snow, straightens the trail if necessary, and provides security for the lead team.
- Lead squad in the platoon generally should not be dragging their own ahkio group if a trail is to be broken or cut in very thick brush or deep snow:
  - If the lead squad is dragging their own ahkio, breakers and pullers must rotate more frequently in deep snow.
  - Security can lapse very quickly when labor becomes difficult.
- Lead team rotation is a big consideration:
  - The lead fire team or squad moves forward for a certain amount of time or a certain distance, then rotates to the rear of their team or squad.
  - The same applies to squads within a platoon.
  - Trail breaking is a demanding physical and mental activity.

6-56. The M973 small unit support vehicle is quite capable of cross-country travel under most snow conditions. The planning procedures are much the same for mounted travel as they are for dismounted. The leader ensures—

- There are at least two vehicles travelling together and the crews are well-versed in recovery.
- That fuel is topped off and additional cans are carried.
- Basic issue items are present and serviceable:
  - Recovery equipment such as winches, tow cables, and snatch blocks are essential.
  - Wheeled vehicles are required to have tire chains.
- The route, number of personnel, and time of departure are understood and logged with the command center.
- That passengers have adequate equipment for sustainment overnight.
- The leader has navigational equipment, maps, compasses, GPS, overlays, and imagery. The procedure for breaking trail in a vehicle is as follows:
  - The lead vehicle sets the initial track, staying concealed as much as possible.
  - The second vehicle offsets their track to flatten out the trail, which keeps a hard ridge from forming in the center of the trail that can high center other vehicles.
  - Following vehicles also offset as much as practicable to widen the trail, harden the snow, and ease the passage for other vehicles.

6-57. A series of tripods can be set up quickly to mark the trace of a trail through open areas. They should be spaced so that from one tripod, the next tripod in line is easily seen.

6-58. Trail maintenance becomes a task repeated often to maintain lines of communication and supply. A significant snowfall or wind event can totally obliterate any trace of a trail. A well maintained and marked trail is far easier to re-open than breaking a new one.

### Planning Rates of Movement

6-59. The normal planning rate for dismounted troops on hard packed, gently rolling terrain is 2.5 mph (4 kph). When factoring in snow or hilly terrain, there is a table to help judge the rate of march. (See table 6-5.)

6-60. The movement rate on foot with less than 1 foot of snow in this table is the same as Soldiers on snowshoes traveling over more than a foot of snow. Skis are shown to be faster than snowshoes because they require less work to use. Finally, skijoring does not show a time for an unbroken trail in this table because Soldiers are behind a vehicle breaking the trail. The table assumes there is no work required to clear a trail.

**Table 6-5. Planning rates of movement**

| MOVEMENT MODE | UNBROKEN TRAIL | BROKEN TRAIL |
|---|---|---|
| On foot or less than 1 ft of snow (approximately .3 meters) | Approximately 1 to 1.75 mph (1.5 to 3 kph) | Approximately 125 to 1.75 mph (2 to 3 kph) |
| On foot or more than 1 ft of snow (approximately .3 meters) | Approximately .3 to 1 mph (5 to 1 kph) | Approximately 125 to 1.75 mph (2 to 3 kph) |
| Snow shoeing | Approximately 1 to 1.75 mph (1.5 to 3 kph) | Approximately 175 to 2.5 mph (3 to 4 kph) |
| Skiing | Approximately 1 to 3 mph (1.5 to 5 kph) | Approximately 3 to 3.75 mph (5 to 6 kph) |
| Skijoring | N/A | Approximately 5 to 15 mph (8 to 24 kph) |
| Legend: ft—foot; kph—kilometers per hour; mph—miles per hour; N/A—not applicable |  |  |

6-61. When gaining elevation, add 1 hour for every 1,000 feet (approximately 305 meters) of upward movement. This takes into consideration the extra time required for traversing and the more frequent halts that are to be expected. When descending, add 1 hour for every 1,600 feet (approximately .5 km) of downhill movement. While this is generally accepted for foot or snowshoe movement, proficient skiers take significantly less time to move downhill. However, more injuries occur when descending than ascending, usually affecting the lower leg.
