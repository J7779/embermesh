# Tracking: Reading Sign and Following a Trail (Part 1 of 2)

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 7. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

The ability to locate and follow enemy movement is a vital skill for any unit operating in a contested environment, especially within the dense confines of a jungle. This chapter details the art and science of dismounted combat tracking, focusing on identifying and interpreting signs of enemy activity. This chapter covers tracking techniques, countertracking measures, essential terminology, and the different types of tracking employed in a tactical setting. Mastering these skills provides a significant advantage in locating, engaging, and disrupting enemy operations.

## Section I – General

7-1. Visual tracking is the skill of being able to follow a person or group of people from the sign they have left. It is a precise skill, and all trackers require considerable practice if they are to achieve and maintain a high standard. There is an obvious requirement to be able to interpret signs left by an enemy as this yields vital information. Units should endeavor to have enough qualified trackers when deployed. This skill is not confined to the jungle environment as it can be adapted for use in any theater.

7-2. Tracking has been employed in numerous military campaigns. It was used to great effect in the Malayan Emergency, the Borneo Confrontation, Vietnam, Southern Africa, East Timor, and Bosnia. Wars in Iraq and Afghanistan have shown the utility of tracking. Countless reports of successful operations in these areas have been associated or accredited either directly or indirectly with the intelligence gathered by the trackers or ground sign-aware Soldiers.

7-3. In an environment where information on the enemy is limited and where most of the movement is concealed, the primary means of intelligence gathering is patrolling. It should be the commander’s intent to find, fix, strike, and exploit the enemy wherever he may be. The importance of tracking therefore cannot be underestimated as each and every sighting or contact with the enemy must be followed up to fulfill the commander’s intent. The main purpose of military tracking therefore is to gain and pass onto commanders as much information as possible about the enemy by employing tracking techniques to mount subsequent military operations.

7-4. The options for establishing a tracker platoon are as follows:

- In a jungle environment task organize elements of the antitank platoon as the tracker platoon. This option has obvious advantages as the armored threat is greatly reduced therefore manpower could be provided without losing a capability elsewhere.
- Train the reconnaissance platoon as a tracker platoon. This would require the reconnaissance platoon to fulfil two roles although it has several advantages as follows:
  - The Soldiers within the reconnaissance platoon generally have the high level of individual soldiering skills required by a tracker.
  - The platoon is established and has all the equipment required by a TT.
  - On discovering an enemy objective, the team would be experts in the conduct of close target reconnaissance and would not require additional training.
- Convert a section per company into a TT, and either keep them attached to that company or group them together as a platoon under the battalion HQ.

## Section II – Types of Tracking

7-5. There are two types of tracking that have important aspects in providing a complete picture of the targets being tracked.

## Visual Tracking

7-6. Visual tracking is the skill of being able to track a person or animal by the signs and marks they have created. Examples are:

- Changes in the color and the unnatural formation of vegetation due to disturbance.
- Bruises, breaks, and cuts in vegetation.
- Water in areas that are normally dry.
- Lack of water or dew on vegetation.
- Mud or soil on grass or bushes.
- Footprints in muddy ground.
- Sap from a bruised root or trunk of a tree.
- Disturbance to animal, bird, or insect life.

7-7. Since the visual tracker (VT) relies primarily on sight, the VT is unable to track at night except in very unusual circumstances, for example, brilliant moonlight, artificial light in open country, or with the use of night observation devices and if the tactical situation allows.

## Scent Tracking

7-8. Scent tracking is normally performed by dogs; however, scent can also assist a well-trained tracker in his pursuit of the target whether human or animal, provided the scent is strong and fresh. In addition, the VT uses sense of smell to warn of any foreign scent in the area, for example:

- Cooking smells.
- Smoke.
- Latrines.
- Newly dug earth.
- Perfume smells from toiletries.

## Qualities of a Tracker

7-9. Generally, trackers are the most efficient in their country of origin; they move them from their natural environment and their efficiency deteriorates until such time as they become accustomed to their new surroundings. However, there are certain fundamental factors that apply to tracking irrespective of the country, which are apparent to the trained tracker, remaining hidden to the untrained. Nobody is born with the natural instincts of a tracker, however, contrary to the common belief a tracker is not a special type of person. A tracker is a Soldier who has attained these skills through dedication, practice, and hard work. It is possible for anyone to become proficient in the skill of tracking provided they are determined and interested.

7-10. The tracker is a highly trained Soldier who can locate, identify and pursue sign and as a result form intelligent interpretations and assumptions about the target. Qualities of a tracker include the following:

- Honesty.
- Patience.
- Perseverance.
- An inquisitive mind.
- Acute observation.
- Determination (mental and physical).
- Above average endurance.
- Above average standard of fieldcraft.
- Knowledge of the enemy and his tactics.
- Knowledge about the local flora and fauna.

7-11. With patience and perseverance, it is possible for any Soldier with the right attributes to be trained as a tracker. It is essential that a tracker has a sound understanding of the types of tracking, and the basic terminology used before the Soldier learns the elements of tracking (EOTs).

## Section III – Elements of Tracking

7-12. EOTs is a term used to describe the four main aspects of tracking, particularly when training trackers. The tracker should fully understand the EOTs, as these are the core skills and fundamentals of tracking. The four main EOTs are:

7-13. EOT 1—Signs–which aims to define the characteristics, categories and classifications of sign.

7-14. EOT 2—Factors Which Affect Tracking–which aims to outline how sign is adversely affected by the elements.

7-15. EOT 3—Judging the Age of Sign–which illustrates procedures and considerations when determining the age of sign.

7-16. EOT 4—Information Gained from Sign Tracking–which establishes exactly what information can be obtained through the application of tracking techniques.

## Element of Tracking 1 — Sign

7-17. Sign can be defined as any evidence of change from the natural state that is inflicted upon the environment by the passage of human, animal, or machinery. Sign is visible to all; however, to most people, the uninitiated, they do not always recognize the significance of what they are looking at.

### Characteristics

7-18. The ability to recognize sign is fundamental to being able to track, and the tracker who fully understands what sign the tracker is looking for can tune in and track in all environments and in all weather conditions (see figures 7-1 to 7-6 on pages 60 to 65). All sign therefore may be identified by one or more of the following characteristics:

- Regularity is an effect caused by straight lines, arches or other geometric shapes being formed and leaving marks not normally found in nature.
- Flattening is the general levelling or depression caused by pressure on an area and is identified through a comparison with the immediate surroundings, for example:
  - Boot prints on grass.
  - Where someone has sat down or placed heavy equipment and so forth.
- Transfer is a deposit carried forward over an area after the target has moved from one environment to another, for example, mud, sand, grass, water, and so forth.
- Color change is the difference in color or texture from the area that surrounds it, for example, overturned leaves.
- Discardable are any materials ‘cast off’ (intentional or otherwise) by the target, for example, rations, packaging, equipment.
- Disturbance is any other change or rearrangement of the natural state of an area caused by the passage of the target, for example, insect/animal life, dead leaves, bruised roots.

### Categories

7-19. Once the tracker has developed the ability to recognize the characteristics of sign, the tracker has acquired the primary skill required of a tracker. While tracking all sign is divided into two categories with the dividing line taken at ankle height. The two categories are top sign and ground sign.

7-20. Top sign is above the ankle to the height and width of the person and equipment the person may be carrying. Examples of top sign are:

- Broken twigs or leaves.
- Scratches on tress and rocks.
- Hand holds on trees.
- Changes in color and unnatural position of vegetation.
- Cutting.
- Rope marks on a tree from a hammock.

7-21. Ground sign is a sign left mainly by the feet or equipment placed on the ground. It refers to all sign below ankle height. Examples of ground sign are:

- Foot or boot marks.
- Broken twigs or leaves on the ground.
- Bruised or bleeding roots.
- Disturbances of insect life on the ground.
- Disturbances of grass or ground vegetation.
- Mud, and so forth, deposited from boots.
- Disturbed leaves, stones, and twigs on the ground.
- Discardables.
- Disturbed water.

### Classification of Sign

7-22. In any environment the tracker can identify sign because animals or people may have been in the area prior to or since the target. It is essential that the tracker can identify what sign belongs to the target and to discount foul sign. When pursuing sign, the tracker designates all sign into one of two classifications as follows:

- Conclusive sign indicates to a tracker the passage of the target through the area, for example, a footprint, discardable or cutting; in other words, sign that is made by the target.
- Inconclusive sign may or may not have been caused by the target (not definite).

### Dynamics of Footprints

7-23. The ability to interpret footprints and action indicators can play a vital role in tracking. Piecing together the information helps formulate a picture that, together with intelligent assumption can provide accurate intelligence. In certain terrains the distinct footprint is a rare find, knowledge of the footprint and its indicators allow the Soldier to gain increased knowledge about the enemy and use it to their advantage.

7-24. Basic elements to every footstep: primary impact point and terminal point (TP).

7-25. Primary impact point: The first part of the foot that strikes the ground whether the individual is walking forward or backwards. The heel dig may be prominent and sharp when walking forwards, when walking backwards the toe digs in first (see figure 7-7).

*[Figure 7-7. Primary impact point — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-26. Foot roll: As the foot rolls forward, the heel markings are more prominent than the remainder of the sole due to the pressure laid down onto the heel (see figure 7-8).

*[Figure 7-8. Foot roll — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-27. Heel strike: The heel of the second foot strikes the ground creating a primary impact point as the first foot completes the roll.

7-28. Pre-TP: The weight is shifted forwards on the toe of the foot.

7-29. TP: The last point of the foot as it leaves the ground. As weight and power transfer to the other foot, there is a certain amount of toe drag as the foot moves. Over the right terrain, these characteristics show a rising of the soil under the foot, distorting part of the imprint of the sole (see figure 7-9).

*[Figure 7-9. Terminal point — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-30. It is possible to gauge the activity of the quarry by studying a series of footprints. Every time a new activity takes place, the five-action indicators change (see figure 7-10 on page 68). The five-action indicators are as follows: stride, pitch angle, straddle, pressure, and dwell time.

*[Figure 7-10. Dynamics of footprint — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-31. Stride: The distance from heel to heel. A person’s stride can vary as much as 4 to 6 inches as terrain and other circumstances change.

7-32. Pitch angle: This is the angle of the foot that pitches out from the line of travel, for example, whether their toes point inwards or outwards. The angles are referred to as a positive (point outwards), neutral (straight) or negative (point inwards) pitch angle. All individuals differ on the pitch angle; there is some evidence to support the claim that females have a certain amount of a negative pitch angle although this would depend on the female. Every person is unique in the way they angle their feet as they walk.

7-33. Straddle: The inner distance between the left and right foot. Straddle varies depending on the person’s activity; running creates a narrower straddle as does walking slowly with a heavy load (see figure 7-11).

*[Figure 7-11. Straddle — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-34. Pressure: The weight of the body through the foot making a depression onto the ground by the pressure of weight. Heavier people and those carrying heavy loads have more pressure creating deeper footprints in the terrain. Likewise remaining still in the same place for longer periods of time creates more pressure.

7-35. Dwell time: This is the amount of time the foot is on the ground at the same location. The pressure and dwell time tend to go hand in hand.

7-36. Using the dynamics of a footprint, action indicators, and recognizing the differences that occur when activities change, a competent Soldier could quickly and fairly accurately identify the activities of the quarry. Namely: speed of movement, accelerating into a sprint, fast running, and size and weight.

7-37. Speed of movement: Information gained could show that the quarry is dawdling, strolling, walking slow, fast, striding out, jogging, running, or sprinting.

7-38. Accelerating into a sprint: The action indicators would change dramatically as the quarry develops the pace. The stride length increases: the dwell time and pressure are less onto the ground and the pitch angle would change from a positive to a neutral angle as the individual increases in speed. Disturbance onto the soil is visible as the ball of the foot and toe dig is evident, but only as they have moved into the sprint (see figure 7-12 on page 70).

*[Figure 7-12. Accelerating into a sprint — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-39. Fast running: The stride length is generally at its maximum and the pitch angle is near neutral. Differing styles mean that some individuals run on the balls of their feet and others place their full foot down (see figure 7-13).

*[Figure 7-13. Fast running — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-40. Size and weight: Key information found on the ground would indicate what size and weight the quarry is based on the dwell time and pressure. If the person is heavy or carrying weight, then there would be depth to the footprint and more prominence as the individual travelled forward. Should a person be carrying weight, then the straddle could well be narrower for the body to adapt to the added weight. The edge of the footprint is sharper in difference too if the person stood on the same spot as the edge would not build up any soil. Different terrain presents different sign. Roughly speaking, a measurement of the foot in inches and then divided by two would be an assumption as to the individual’s height for example. Foot length is 12.5 inches/2 equals approximately 6 feet, 2 inches tall.

### Track Trap

7-41. An area that is conducive to leaving and retaining good sign or spoor. How a tracker determines conclusive and inconclusive sign varies between trackers and is dependent on the tracker’s experience. A novice may require several characteristics of sign to be convinced that the same person/target is being pursued. With experience and practice, a tracker requires fewer or less characteristics to pursue the target and treats what the tracker once considered inconclusive sign confidently as conclusive sign.

### Summary

7-42. A good understanding of sign, and how to identify it, assists the tracker during the initial learning stages of the course. EOT 1 is the most important part of visual tracking, and it must be remembered that some people may not have the aptitude. With experience and practice locating and identifying sign becomes easier.

## Element of Tracking 2 — Factors Which Affect Tracking

7-43. There are four main factors which can affect tracking, and they are all very closely related. This can be advantageous and disadvantageous for the tracker. It is imperative that the tracker has a thorough understanding of each of the four factors and anticipates how each one can affect the others. The four factors which affect tracking are: sign and spoor, terrain, climatic conditions, and time.

### Sign and Spoor

7-44. Tracking the target’s sign is the most important thing to consider but, to remain on the correct sign, a tracker must consider foul tracking by other human sign, or by animal spoor.

### Animal Spoor

7-45. A tracker must consider animal life in the area and know how to differentiate between animal spoor and human sign. For example, most hoofed animals make a distinct chop-mark as they walk. The shape of the hoof acts like a knife-edge and the way animals distribute their body weight onto the hoof causes it to cut into the ground. By contrast a human tends to create heel and toe movements when walking. As humans place their feet down the heel is the first part to touch the ground. As they are moving, their weight is being transferred from the heel onto the ball then onto the toe, which means the weight is being displaced evenly over the foot. For example, if a wild pig stood on a stick, it would cause one clean break due to the cutting motion by which the pressure is applied, however a human would invariably cause two, one on either side of the foot due to the way the weight is distributed (see figure 7-14).

*[Figure 7-14. Animal spoor — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-46. If there is other human sign in the area, the tracker must determine through the sign pattern that the tracker has identified which quarry to pursue (see figure 7-15).

### Terrain

**7-47.** The terrain in which the target is operating has an impact upon the amount and type of sign produced. The characteristics are equally applicable; however, the tracker must have a comprehensive knowledge of what type of sign is likely to be prominent in different terrain and tune in accordingly. Tracking can be conducted in any environment. The following can be used as a guide: grassland, rocky country, primary jungle (rain forest), secondary jungle (scrub), coastal and estuarine jungle (rivers and streams, marshy and swampy ground), and sand.

### Grassland

7-48. If the grass is high, for example, above 3 feet, tracks are relatively easy to follow since the grass is knocked down and stays down for some time, depending on the weather. If the grass is short, it springs back into its original position in a relatively short space of time (see figures 7-16 to 7-19 on pages 74 to 76). The following points assist when tracking in grassland:

- Pointer: grass is normally trodden down and pointing in the direction the target is travelling, presenting a contrast in color to the normal undergrowth when pressed down.
- Color change: if the grass has been wet with dew the night before, the dew is rubbed off.
- Transfer: mud or soil from footwear may appear on some of the grass.
- If it is dry grass broken and crushed stems are found. Footprints are normally found in dry grass areas.
- If new vegetation is showing through, it indicates the track is an old one.
- In very short grass a boot damages the grass near the ground and invariably a footprint or impression is found.

### Rocky Country

7-49. Tracking through a rocky country is not as difficult as is imagined because rocks are both easily disturbed and are generally easily marked. The following are some points to consider in this type of country:

- Unless moving over large boulders, stones or rocks either move aside or roll over.
  - This disturbs the soil, leaving a distinct variation in color and an impression.
  - If wet, the underside of the stone and rock is darker in color, and if dry, a much lighter color.
- If moving over large stones the base of the boots tends to scratch the surface of the rocks.
- On sandstone, boot marks tend to show dark in color, and on lava, the marks are white.
- If the stone is brittle, it normally chips and crumbles when walked on and a light patch appears. The pieces of stone that have chipped off should also be seen nearby.
- Stones on the side of hills move slightly or roll away when walked on, regardless of whether the target is moving up or down hill.
- Stones on a loose or soft surface are normally pressed into the ground when walked upon, leaving either a ridge around the edge of the stone where it has forced the dirt out, or a hole where the stone has been pushed below the surface of the ground.

### Primary Jungle (Rain Forest)

7-50. Within the primary jungle, trackers find many mediums which readily show sign. These include the undergrowth, live and dead leaves and trees, streams with muddy or sandy banks, moss on the forest floor and rocks (see figures 7-20 to 7-22 on pages 78 to 79). Tracking in the rain forest is assisted by observation of the following details:

- Wet leaves on the forest floor when disturbed show up as being much darker than those that are undisturbed.
- Dry leaves when undisturbed show a distinct bleached upper surface whereas the underside is dark brown. Therefore, disturbed dry leaves leave a significant dark color change.
- Dead leaves often become very brittle and crack or break under the pressure of a person walking on them. The same can be said for small dry twigs.
- Where the undergrowth is thick, especially on the edges of the forest, the target may have to push through the vegetation. Bushes and branches with green leaves that have been pushed aside and twisted expose the underside of the leaves which are lighter in shade than the top of the leaf.
- To the trained eye this is prominent. However, when looking for this type of sign the tracker must look through the vegetation and not at it.
- Broken twigs assist the tracker in assessing how long it has been since the track was laid. Freshly broken twigs, green or dead, tend to portray a cream color at the break and fine hairlike strands of wood. The color at the break will get darker and the fine strands dissipate with time, but if the tracker breaks the twig again, the tracker gets an indication by comparison of how long it is since the original break occurred. Only by experiment and experience will the tracker be able to determine the age of the break. Freshly broken green twigs usually retain the smell of sap for up to 3 or 4 hours.
- Boot impressions are left on fallen rotten trees if walked on.
- When logs lay across a path, there are generally marks on the log or either side of it.
- Where roots are stepped on, they often show bruising marks.
- Broken cobwebs across the path may indicate the passage of animals or humans.

### Secondary Jungle (Scrub)

7-51. This is the type of country where the primary growth has been cleared, and the secondary growth has started. Usually, it is very thick and difficult to penetrate. To do so the individual may be forced to make a way through by either cutting or crawling along at ground level. When tracking in it, the main points to watch for are the following:

- Broken branches and twigs.
- Leaves knocked off branches.
- Color-changed leaves or small branches facing the direction in which the target has travelled.
- Footprints on the ground which show up clearly as grass does not grow underneath.
- Tunnels made very low to the ground.
- Broken cobwebs.
- Pieces of clothing or equipment caught on sharp edges of vines, bushes, and so forth.

### Costal and Estuarine Jungle

7-52. The term coastal and estuarine jungle is taken to include rivers, streams, and marshy and swampy ground. The most likely forms of sign to be located are as follows:

- Footprints on the banks.
- Footprints in shallow water or in mud.
- Discolored water from stirred up mud.
- Rocks splashed with water in a slow moving/small stream.
- Water on the ground at the exit point.
- Boots may have been taken off to wade the stream.
  - Look for spots on banks where this was done and where they were put on again.
  - Normally there are signs indicating where the person sat down to take off and put on boots.
- In mangrove swamps mud will be stirred up. Also branches of mangrove will be bent where people have held onto them to prevent themselves from tripping over roots.
- Areas of color change on river/stream beds indicating where the target has passed.
- Top sign in the form of color change where the target has pushed through tall reeds, and so forth.

### Sand

7-53. Sand is relatively easy to track on. The biggest problem with the tracker is that rain and wind can obliterate marks and impressions within a couple of minutes (see figures 7-23 to 7-25 on pages 80 to 81). The main points to be considered are as follows:

- If the surface is reasonably hard, the footprint is very clear.
- If the surface is soft the footprint will be quite deep and in the early morning and late afternoon the walls of the impression may cast a shadow.

### Climatic Conditions

7-54. In tropical areas the weather can change rapidly between sun, rain, and strong winds. Prolonged periods of either of these conditions can exist, such as in the monsoon and dry seasons. However, each of these climatic conditions, either taken singularly or combined, have an adverse effect on sign and can make the task of the tracker more difficult. The following climatic conditions should be considered:

### Direct Sunlight

7-55. The average temperature in tropical areas is between 30º to 40ºC. In open areas such as grassland, or in exposed areas in the jungle, such as an LP or large bend in the river, direct sunlight will greatly affect sign. The heat accelerates color change to a far greater extent than in covered areas, for example an overturned leaf which is damp will quickly dry, or the darker side of an overturned dry leaf will become quickly bleached. However, in certain circumstances direct sunlight can be an aid, for example in disturbed areas it can produce a contained shadow.

### Strong Wind

7-56. Strong winds are a frequent occurrence, especially prior to heavy rain, and although not always felt by Soldiers operating within thick jungle, strong winds cause a great deal of disturbance to the vegetation. In exposed areas it encourages disturbed vegetation to return to normal, for example, blow it or the vegetation around it into different positions. Strong wind may even conceal some ground sign by blowing vegetation on top of it.

### Heavy Rain

7-57. The average annual rainfall in most of the tropical areas is around 500 centimeters (180 inches) per year. Rain occurs frequently in primary jungle hence the name rainforest. However, most of the rain falls during monsoon seasons. Thick clouds also reduce light therefore affecting visibility reducing the tracker’s ability to see sign considerably. Heavy rain washes out some sign very quickly, particularly ground sign, and is the most challenging climatic condition for a VT.

### Time

7-58. To be able to assess the time between when the sign was first made and when it was located is the hardest part of a tracker’s task. Full details on how to judge the age of sign are given at EOT 3, however, experience and practice help to overcome this difficulty. The longer the time span since the track was laid and the time of discovery, the more chance there is of the track becoming fouled or flawed by climatic conditions.

### Other Considerations

7-59. In addition to the factors in paragraphs 7-55 to 7-58, there are several additional considerations that affect a tracker’s ability. The two main considerations are:

### Animal Life

7-60. Within a jungle environment the amount and type of wildlife both assist and hinder tracking operations. In many countries, birds, insects, and mammals will warn other animals of impending danger from intruders. Monkeys are particularly proficient at shouting a warning when they see humans in the jungle. Insects on the other hand tend to go silent as a means of warning. A firsthand knowledge of the habits and nature of wildlife assists a tracker in the application of his task. Large groups of animals between the tracker and the target can cause foul track so badly that pursuit becomes almost impossible. In this instance a tracker should consider applying the following drill:

- Look for a likely exit point.
- Isolate the track.
- Carry out a likely area search.

### Tracking in Built-up Areas

7-61. Built-up areas, including villages, roads, and so forth, cause additional problems for trackers. A track leading towards a village may indicate a release point with other forces, familiarity with the locals, or that the enemy target area is close. Tracking in these areas will invariably involve significant foul track and will slow down most trackers. However, the tracker may speed up his pursuit by carrying out a process of isolation procedures as follows:

- Locate the entry and exit point.
- Conduct a likely area search.
- Look for the presence of the sign pattern being pursued.

### Summary

7-62. Each of the four main factors will affect the sign created by the enemy. It must be remembered that the type of sign will vary in different types of terrain. The tracker must take time to tune in and anticipate climatic conditions and time. The most important thing to remember is what can adversely affect tracking.

## Element of Tracking 3 — Judging the Age of Sign

7-63. A tracker must be able to accurately judge the age of the sign if the tracker is to fulfill the role. The ability to judge the age of sign accurately only comes with experience and continual practice, however it is a vital skill which assists the tracker in the following ways:

- It enables the tracker to determine the approximate time frame since the target passed through the area.
- When tracking it is essential that the VT can determine if the target is in proximity to prevent unnecessary casualties to the TT; judging the age of sign will assist the VT in deciding whether—
  - The VT is gaining on the target.
  - The VT is continually falling back.
- Deducing which sign is the same age as the target’s sign assists the tracker in overcoming the problems of foul track.
- It assists commanders in deciding whether or not the team should continue on track.

### Considerations

7-64. As stated in EOT 2, a VT must understand local flora and fauna and the relationship between these, as well as a knowledge of the weather and terrain in the AO. All these factors determine the ageing process of sign found. When judging the age of sign the following factors must be considered:

### The Type of Sign

7-65. Sign will age to differing degrees depending on whether the sign is hard or soft as follows (see figures 7-26 to 7-31 on pages 84 to 87):

- Hard sign: examples of hard sign are marks in sunbaked soil, marks on stones, or marks in resilient mosses or tussock grass.
  - Discardable such as plastic or metals are also considered hard sign.
  - Hard sign takes longer to either deteriorate or return to normal therefore the tracker is likely to identify hard sign for a longer period than soft sign.

- Soft sign: examples of soft sign are marks in soft soil, mud, or sand, and marks inflicted upon green leafy plants.

  - Soft sign deteriorates or returns to normal more quickly than hard sign so the tracker knows that if the tracker is identifying soft sign, then the tracker is closer to the enemy.

### Exposure

7-66. The degree of exposure to the elements has differing effects on the sign. In areas exposed to direct sunlight and heavy winds sign change rapidly (return to normal/age) which must be considered by the tracker. In exposed areas trackers may find that they are tracking on minimal sign and realize that when judging the age of sign any sign found is likely to be fresher than it appears.

### Weather

7-67. The condition of the sign is directly connected with the weather conditions it has been subjected to. The tracker knows if the track was laid before or after heavy rain or showers.

### Methods Employed

7-68. Having considered the type of sign, its degree of exposure and the weather it has been subject to, the tracker could use one of the following methods to assist in judging the age of sign:

### Comparison of Color

7-69. The VT should compare the color of the sign in relation to the color of the surrounding area for color change. Good areas to look for color change are:

- Cracks in bent grass or leaves: an indication of the age of a track may be gained by the state of dryness of such cracks:
  - When fresh they are green, but after a few days they turn a brown color.
  - The older the sign is, the darker and dryer the crack is likely to be (see figure 7-32).

*[Figure 7-32. Cracks in bent grass or leaves — illustration not reproduced.]*

- Breaking and comparing sticks and twigs: A fresh break is a lighter color, and a study of ends normally show fibers protruding:
  - These fibers disappear over a period of time and their color darkens.
  - The fewer fibers are seen and the darker the color of the break the older the sign (see figure 7-33).

*[Figure 7-33. Comparing by breaking a twig — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Comparison of Impression

7-70. The state of dryness of a track in mud or soft ground must be noted. If the track is very fresh, water does not run back into the print or depression made by the target’s foot. Later the water runs back and later the mud that has been pushed up around the print and kicked forward begins to dry. By comparing the print beside that of the target’s and then considering the color change and the degree of regularity an assessment of the signs age can be made (see figure 7-34 on page 90).

### Bracketing

7-71. This method is based on knowing the weather conditions and the amount of wildlife movement typical in the area during the approximate period since the track was laid as follows:

- Weather: by considering the recent weather conditions it is possible to bracket the period when the sign was left for example:
  - Time of rain: a period of heavy rain will leave pockmarks on the sign and will cause a loss of definition. Knowing the time of the last rainfall will provide a clue as to the age of the sign, for example if the last rain was 12 hours a go the sign would be over 12 hours old. Likewise, sign with little damage and plenty of definition will indicate that it was made after the last rainfall, for example, less than 12 hours old (see figure 7-35).
  - Overnight dew: where sign has traces of dew over it indicates that the sign was made the previous day.
  - Strong winds: high wind may result in leaves and foliage obscuring the sign. If this is the case and the tracker knows the time of the last heavy winds, this can be a further aid to judging the age sign.

*[Figure 7-35. Before and after rain print — illustration not reproduced.]*

- Game tracks superimposed: most wild animals lay up during the day and move at night:
  - If human prints on a main game trail have animal tracks superimposed, and these tracks show that the animals have moved in both directions, then human prints are probably at least one night old or older.
  - Where human prints are superimposed on the animal sign then the track is likely to have been made that day.

### Local Knowledge of Flora/Fauna

7-72. A detailed knowledge of local flora and fauna can assist the tracker in judging the age of sign.

7-73. The state and position of trodden vegetation must be noted. Various grasses have different degrees of resilience and prior knowledge of the flora’s resilience to being crushed, pushed aside, broken, cut, and so forth, may assist in determining age. Only by studying flora present in AOs over a period of time can the tracker become adept with this method.

7-74. By studying and noting the habits of wildlife, with regards to feeding, watering, movement, and so forth, the tracker can use this information to assist in assessing age. Most wild animals feed and move during hours of darkness, but birds that feed on the forest floor, like blackbirds, feed during the day, so any sign disturbed by feeding or moving animals can assist in assessing age. Disturbed or crushed worm casts, spider webs, and broken ant, or termite trails should all be taken into consideration. Awareness of the natural surroundings aid in judging age.

### Summary

7-75. Over a period of time all sign becomes subjected to natural processes which eventually return it to a state where judging its age becomes more of a guess. However, by considering the type of sign, the degree of exposure and the weather, and then by applying the methods to assist in judging age, it is possible for a VT to become adept at making accurate assessments of the sign’s age. This allows the VT to become an accurate intelligence-gathering asset.
