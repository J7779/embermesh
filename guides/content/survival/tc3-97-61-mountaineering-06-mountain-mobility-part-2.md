# Mountain Walking, Scrambling, Rock Climbing and Movement on Snow and Ice (Part 2 of 2)

*TC 3-97.61, Chapter 6, continued.*

## Movement over Snow

6-103. Low- to moderate-angle snow slopes found on or adjacent to valleys are primary avenues of movement for military units operating in winter conditions. Steeper slopes in gullies and passes are often the easiest routes through even steeper rocky features around them. Though challenging to move over, these often provide the best access to points of domination in alpine terrain.

6-104. Techniques for moving over snowy surfaces change IAW slope and surface conditions. Some soft snow increases physical strain but can increase the security of foot placements. During movement, breaks should be taken more frequently and for shorter durations in cold weather to assist with moisture management. This helps prevent overheating, which can cause problems once movements stop. Snow deeper than calf-height makes movement difficult without skis or snowshoes. Hard-packed snow slopes requiring special techniques and equipment or falls that could result in serious injury or death are likely.

6-105. A thorough analysis of avalanche threat should be conducted prior to moving over slopes that could be exposed to avalanche risk. Terrain at any risk for avalanche should be avoided. All Soldiers moving in potential avalanche terrain must be equipped with avalanche protective equipment and trained in its use, as well as avalanche response procedures. (See chapter 5 for more information on avalanche considerations.)

### Boot Packing

6-106. The boot-packing method requires the least amount of training and special equipment. The same techniques can be used in mountain walking; however, there is often less traction. During the boot-packing method, Soldiers move slower and minor slips and falls should be expected. In deeper soft snow, Soldiers should step in the tracks left by the Soldier in front of them. This packs them into hard steps and makes movement less difficult. Rotate lead Soldiers to prevent fatigue.

6-107. Crampons are strongly recommended for moving on hard-packed slopes. However, if crampons are not available, there are a few techniques to use to improve the security of foot placements. Step kicking involves kicking the toe or edge of the boot into the surface with each step creating a small depression, which improves traction. Mountain axes can be used as a third point of contact, allowing the body to gain more traction. The axe is typically held by its head in the uphill hand, with the pick to the rear (cane position). Steps can be cut into the slope using the axe’s adze to improve foot placement security. (See figure 6-34, page 120.) This technique is effective for short distances but is very time-consuming.

### Glissading

6-108. Glissading is a technique used to descend low to moderate angle slopes by sliding in the seated position and using a mountain axe as a brake. (See figure 6-35.) Glissading allows Soldiers to descend appropriate slopes very quickly and efficiently, thereby saving significant time and energy. Specific conditions are required for glissading to be appropriate. The slope should not have any obstacles (such as trees, rocks, drop offs, crevasses) in the slide path, and it should have sufficient soft snow on the surface to allow Soldiers to control the speed of the slide. Glissading should never be done on hard-packed snow or ice surfaces. Crampons should be removed prior to glissading to ensure injuries to lower extremities are not caused by a crampon becoming caught on something during the slide.

6-109. Glissading is conducted by sitting in the snow and positioning the midpoint of a mountain axe shaft against one hip with the spike in the snow. The hand on the same side as the axe holds its shaft to the hip at the midpoint. The other hand holds the top of the axe where pick, shaft and adze meet. The mountain axe pick should always face forward (away from the Soldier) to prevent injury. Soldiers then initiate the slide on their buttocks using the axe spike as a brake. To slow down, Soldiers rotate the spike downward, pressing it deeper into the snowpack and increasing friction. To speed up, Soldiers rotate the spike away from the snowpack, decreasing friction. Adequate time should be allowed between Soldiers sliding in the same glissade path to ensure a collision does not occur should a Soldier stop shortly mid-slide.

*[Figure 6-35. Glissading — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Flatfooting

6-110. Flatfooting is the term for numerous techniques that increase security and efficiency of movement over snow and ice slopes. These techniques involve the use of both crampons and a mountain axe. The key principle of flatfooting is to position the body so that all crampon points are embedded in the snow surface. This provides maximum security for foot placements. These techniques are particularly critical to safe movement over steep, hard-packed snow. Flatfooting with heavy loads requires greater proficiency. Flatfooting techniques are much more difficult to perform in flexible boots or with loose sloppy crampons. (See figure 6-36, page 122.)

6-111. Crampons substantially increase the security of foot placements when used properly. However, walking in them forces the Soldier’s gait to change and can take some practice getting used to. They must be sized properly and snugged tight to the boot, so Soldiers’ boots do not slide around in the crampon introducing instability to their foot placements.

### Ascending

6-112. Lower-angle slopes can be ascended using a diagonal traverse or switchback technique. More security can be gained from pointing the toe downhill some when placing the foot. Mountain axes or an ice tool can also be used in cane position the same way previously mentioned. (See figure 6-37.)

6-113. As terrain gets steeper, maximizing crampon point contact with the surface becomes more difficult. Footwork techniques used in rock climbing, such as edging, are very insecure when wearing crampons. They should not be used. Ankles must be rolled somewhat downhill to ensure crampon points are embedded in the surface. Turning the downhill foot so that the toe points downhill increases security by making it easier to engage the crampon points on the outside edge of the foot.

*[Figure 6-37. Ice axe uses in low-angle terrain — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-114. To obtain secure foot placements, lean the body away from the slope to increase pressure on crampon points. This becomes more important as surface conditions get firmer. Generally, it is easier to stay in balance and achieve solid foot placements by taking smaller steps. Moving diagonally up slope makes this easier to execute.

6-115. Flatfooting, using a mountain axe in a cross-body position. To perform this technique, the Soldier stands sideways to the slope. The mountain axe head is held in the downhill hand and the shaft held low in the uphill hand. The uphill foot is placed perpendicular to the slope and the downhill foot is placed similarly, but with the toe pointed downhill as much as comfort demands. The axe spike is then forced into the snowpack uphill from the feet far enough so that most of the Soldier’s weight is transitioned onto it. This provides balance and security during foot movement. The downhill foot is moved across and placed above the uphill foot. If the crossover step is too awkward, it can be moved to a position just below the uphill foot, but this method is less efficient. The Soldier’s weight is then transitioned onto the newly placed foot so that the uphill foot may be moved upslope. The process is then repeated, starting with replacing the axe uphill and the Soldier moves in a generally diagonal path up the slope. (See figure 6-38.)

*[Figure 6-38. Flatfooting with axe in cross-body position — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-116. Flatfooting using a mountain axe in anchor position. The footwork is the same for this technique as when using the axe in a cross-body position. However, the axe is held by the shaft like a hammer and swung so that the pick is embedded into the uphill slope. The axe can then be “climbed” by walking the hands up the shaft while the feet work their way up the slope. (See figure 6-39.)

*[Figure 6-39. Flatfooting with axe in anchor position — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-117. Front pointing involves kicking into the slope with the toe of the boot so that all front crampon points become embedded in the surface. The Soldier leans forward slightly locking the ankle so that the sole of the foot is held perpendicular to the slope. If the heel is too low or too high, or only the large front points are embedded, then the foot placement will be less secure and may fail. The Soldier stands up on the placement using hands or mountain axe for balance and repeats kicking in with the other foot higher on the slope. This process is repeated as the Soldier generally moves straight up the slope. The front-pointing technique is very effective and allows for faster movement in steep terrain but is more physically taxing. The Soldier’s calf muscles become fatigued quickly using this technique. The front-pointing technique is most effective for ascending short, steep sections of terrain. (See figure 6-40.)

*[Figure 6-40. Front pointing — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-118. Front pointing with a mountain axe in dagger positions involves Soldiers holding the axe by the head with the adze and thumb facing them. The pick is then forced into the slope in a hammer-fist motion (high dagger). Once placed, Soldiers walk their feet up the slope, using front pointing as high as they can go. A low dagger technique can be used to allow climbing higher than a high dagger allows by transitioning the hand to the top of the axe head and pushing down on it for balance. The process is then repeated, moving straight up the slope. (See figure 6-41.)

*[Figure 6-41. Front pointing with axe in dagger positions — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-119. Front pointing with mountain axe in anchor position involves holding the axe by the shaft and swinging it like a hammer so that the pick is embedded into the slope higher above the Soldier. The axe is then used as holds for both hands as Soldiers climb the slope with their feet using front pointing. (See figure 6-42.)

*[Figure 6-42. Front pointing with axe in anchor positions — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-120. Front pointing using mountain axe as in ice climbing involves holding the axe by the shaft and swinging it like a hammer so that the pick is embedded into the slope as with anchor position. However, in this technique the Soldier only uses the axe for holds with one hand. The other hand is placed to push against the slope for balance. (See figure 6-43.)

*[Figure 6-43. Front pointing using axes in ice climbing — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Descending

6-121. Slips and slides should be considered very likely on moderate to steep terrain, especially if surface conditions are hard. Rope systems may be used to mitigate the risk of injury, but they do not make movement faster or easier. Efficient movement requires good flatfooting skills.

6-122. Moderately steep slopes can be descended by simply using a diagonal traverse or switchback pattern down the slope. This may require rolling the ankle more than usual to force all crampon points into the snow. The downhill foot can be turned so that the toe points downhill to reduce strain and add security during movement. The mountain axe is used in any of the positions described above to aid with balance and prepare the Soldier for self-arrest. Edging technique is ineffective; Solders should not attempt this technique. (See figure 6-44, page 128.)

6-123. Steeper slopes require descent facing downhill and relying heavily on a mountain axe for balance and security. Soldiers must bend their knees to lower their center of gravity and position their weight over their crampons. Depending on the slope angle and surface hardness, the mountain axe should be used in one of the positions described below:

- Using the mountain axe in cross body position, while driving the spike into the slope behind the feet is a very secure method of moving down steep slopes when executed properly. During movement, most of the weight is supported by the mountain axe and the feet are moved forward in short deliberate steps. In hard surface conditions, crampons need to be stomped into the ground to ensure points are set. Weight is then transitioned over the feet, the axe moved down and reset. (See figure 6-44, page 128.)
- In addition to the positions already discussed, the mountain axe can be used in support position while descending. This is when it is carried in one hand, head oriented uphill and pick facing down, while the Soldier moves down the slope facing downhill. The axe is held in this position so that the Soldier can quickly plant the pick into the snowpack for security, as necessary. If this is done, the axe provides a handhold to assist in making one or two moves that are more difficult. This can be repeated for longer sections that are more difficult than necessary. (See figure 6-44, page 128.)
- If more security is needed, then the Soldier can transition to using the axe in the anchor position, allowing both hands to grip the axe and a more secure pick placement. (See figure 6-44, page 128.)

6-124. Using the mountain axe in cross body position, while driving the spike into the slope behind the feet is a very secure method of moving down steep slopes when executed properly. During movement, most of the weight is supported by the mountain axe, and the feet are moved forward in short deliberate steps. In hard surface conditions, crampons need to be stomped into the ground to ensure points are set. Weight is then transitioned over the feet; and the axe moved down and reset.

6-125. In addition to the positions already discussed, Soldiers can use the mountain axe in its support position while descending. Soldiers carry the axe in one hand, head oriented uphill and pick facing down, while moving down the slope, facing downhill. The axe is held in this position so that the Soldier can quickly plant the pick into the snowpack for security, as necessary. Carrying the axe this way provides a handhold to assist in making one or two moves that are more difficult. This can be repeated for longer sections that are more difficult, as necessary. (See figure 6-44, page 128.)

6-126. If more security is needed, the Soldier can transition to using the axe in the anchor position, allowing both hands to grip the axe for a more secure pick placement.

### Self-Arrest

6-127. Self-arrest is a technique intended to stop a short fall before it turns into a long, sliding, tumbling fall. Soldiers that will be moving on moderate to steep snow slopes unroped must be issued a mountain axe at a minimum and trained to proficiency in self-arrest techniques. There is little margin for error in executing self-arrest on steep slopes with firm surface conditions. Self-arresting should not be considered an adequate protective measure when slipping and falling risk is determined to be likely. Good movement skills are still the primary risk mitigation for moving unroped.

6-128. Soldiers should carry the axe in the cane position with the pick to the rear in the uphill hand. The instant Soldiers fall, they must commit to self-arresting immediately. The technique is executed by bringing the mountain axe into position at the center of chest, with the hand on its head and pick facing away. The lower hand moves to a position low on the axe shaft. The Soldier then uses body weight and violence of action to drive the pick into the snowpack. This must be done in seconds and if the axe is not centered under the body, it is difficult to apply adequate force to achieve this. The less aggressive and curved design of the ice axe pick is meant to prevent it from biting into the surface too well, where momentum would cause it to be ripped out of the falling Soldiers’ hands. Rather, it is designed to stop the Soldier gradually. For this reason, picks that are more aggressive like those found on tools meant for ice climbing are less effective for this. If crampons are not worn, the Soldier can also dig toes into the snowpack to assist stopping the slide. Careful attention must be paid not to allow the spike of the axe to dig into the snow while sliding. If this happens, the Soldier could be flipped over backwards and thrown into a tumble. Recovering from a tumbling is unlikely. (See figure 6-45.)

6-129. When self-arresting with crampons on, Soldiers must bend their knees during the slide, so the crampon points do not catch and cause a tumble. Legs must remain bent until sliding has stopped. Front points can then be planted to reduce the likelihood of a slide resuming.

6-130. To self-arrest while sliding on the back, the Soldier must bring the axe into position at the center of the chest, then roll in the direction of the axe head until on the stomach, and then drive the pick into the snow.

6-131. To self-arrest headfirst face down, the Soldier must reach downhill to the same side as the axe head, plant the pick, pivot the body around the pick, and swing the legs downhill.

6-132. Self-arrest headfirst, on the back, is the most difficult. Here, the Soldier must plant the pick, twist and roll toward the pick, swing the legs downhill, and roll the chest toward the pick.

### Snowshoes and Skis

6-133. Snow deeper than calf height typically requires more floatation than crampons provide for efficient movement to be possible. Skiing is the most efficient technique for moving over deep snow but requires substantial training to become proficient. Snowshoeing is less efficient but is relatively easy for Soldiers to learn.

6-134. Moving in snowshoes provides good floatation and traction in deep snow. Poles should be used to maintain balance while moving. More practice is necessary if the terrain is rough, rocky, or strewn with obstacles. Soldiers should practice with operational loads to proficiency.

6-135. Skiing involves using climbing skins on flat terrain and while ascending. Heels are released during movement. Traversing or switchback techniques should be used when ascending slopes. Poles should be used to help with balance, especially when carrying a pack. Slopes can be descended slowly with skins on, or skins can be removed for rapid decent. Well-trained skiers can move very quickly in steep terrain. The historical effectiveness of ski troops is well documented.

## Ice Climbing

6-136. Ice climbing is demanding and requires specialized equipment and techniques. Waterfall ice develops in the bottom and along the steep sides of drainages, such as gullies and couloirs. (See figure 6-46.) Alpine ice can develop in the same areas or in glacial features that are more open. (See figure 6-47.) These terrain features may provide the easiest routes for ascending and descending steep mountains.

*[Figure 6-46. Waterfall ice — illustration not reproduced.]*

*[Figure 6-47. Alpine ice — illustration not reproduced.]*

6-137. All the hazards associated with rock climbing exist for ice climbing, as well as additional ones, including avalanches and icefalls. Icefall is always a real threat to ice climbers and personnel located below them. This is particularly true during warmer temperatures as hanging ice features can break off and fall naturally. Ice climbers are at higher risk of injury during a fall due to the sharp tools and crampons used in climbing. Even short falls can result in a tool accidentally puncturing the climber’s body, or a crampon catching on a feature during the fall and causing lower extremity injuries.

6-138. Climbing ice usually requires the use of rope systems for protection even when climber’s skills are high due to the consequences of falling.

### Ice Climbing During Military Operations

6-139. Sustained climbing in 5th-class terrain during conventional military operations is impractical, especially when covered in ice. However, routes through easier terrain are often blocked by short sections of low or steep angle waterfall ice. These routes may be the only or most tactically sound approaches to points of domination for properly trained units. Small teams of advanced mountaineers can be used to establish fixed-rope routes through this terrain for lesser-trained Soldiers to use for protection during movement.

6-140. Typically, Soldiers climbing ice are unable to provide themselves with sufficient, local security during movement. Bounding overwatch in vertical terrain by small-specialized teams or VDA-crossing procedures must be performed by a larger unit to provide adequate security for climbing Soldiers. (See ATP 3-21.50 for more on VDA crossing.) Small reconnaissance and surveillance teams may have to rely on careful technical route selection that provides concealment throughout the approach, the climb, and the top out. Fortunately, the terrain in which ice develops often provides such opportunities for teams with the technical and mobility skills necessary to negotiate it.

### Ice Climbing Route Selection

6-141. Before starting to climb, Soldiers should study the face of the ice flow for features that indicate that climbing the route is possible. With experience, Soldiers can determine how difficult an ice flow will be for them to climb and how reliable protection placements will be. Climbers should form a plan for climbing and protection using rope systems (when necessary) before leaving the ground. (See chapter 10 for how to protect climbers from fall hazards.)

6-142. During military operations, the least difficult routes possible should be selected for travel. (See chapter 7 for more information on planning technical movement routes.)

### Basic Ice Climbing Techniques

6-143. Compared to rock climbing, ice climbing movement techniques are relatively simple. The most difficult part is learning how to place tools and crampons into the ice so that they hold the climber’s weight, and then moving in balance so as not to dislodge them or waste energy. The keys to efficient ice climbing are a reliable and secure tool, crampon placements, and staying in balance during movement.

6-144. Complicated movement techniques are rarely necessary in ice climbing.

### Using Ice Tools

6-145. The purpose of the tools in ice climbing is to provide climbers with handholds to hang from while moving their feet and for maintaining balance while moving the body. Whenever possible, each tool placement should be secure enough to hold the climber’s entire body weight.

6-146. Modern ice tools should be held loosely by the grip, with the heel of the hand wedged into the hook or stop at the bottom of the shaft. This feature allows the climber to hang without squeezing. When done properly, the climber’s weight can be held with just moderate pressure from two or three fingers around the grip. (See figure 6-48.)

6-147. Some force must be used when swinging ice tools, but how they are swung is more important than how hard it is. Placing tools starts with positioning the climber’s hips close to the ice creating space to swing the tool. This becomes more important as the ice gets steeper. The tool is swung in an overhead arc with a flick of the wrist as the pick is about to strike the ice.

6-148. Ideal tool placements are as high as the climber can comfortably reach and as close to the centerline of the body as possible. Low features, or small depressions, are the best targets for tool placements. If a placement is not secure enough after one swing, climbers should swing again aiming for the same spot until they are confident that it will hold their entire weight.

6-149. Ice tools can be removed from the ice by gently tapping upward on the adze or hammer to loosen the pick in the ice. If the tool is too low in relation to the upper body, it can be gripped by the head and wiggled up and down to loosen.

*[Figure 6-48. Ice tool grip — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Using Crampons

6-150. Crampons must be sharp, correctly sized, and tight. Vertical ice or technical crampons are preferred, but not always necessary.

6-151. Crampon placements on low-angle ice are used in the same manner as flatfooting with occasional front pointing. The hard surface of the ice requires more effort to ensure crampon points are set securely in the ice before committing weight to them. Edging techniques are insecure on ice.

6-152. When using crampon placements on moderate and steeper ice flows, front pointing is used exclusively. Climbers must move their lower body away from the ice during crampon placement. Good front point placements are those in which all front points are in solid contact with the ice and the foot is perpendicular to the surface. (See figure 6-40, page 124.) Low spots in the ice, the top of small features sticking out of the ice, and the tops any step-like features are the easiest spots to place crampons. Climbers must ensure that the sole of their boot remains perpendicular to the surface of the ice (for example, heel is not too high or too low), or front points may lever out of the ice.

### Body Positioning and Moving Efficiently

6-153. The basic body position for ice climbing is a triangle, with crampons placed about shoulder width apart, and tools placed in a staggered vertical pattern close to the centerline of the body. Body weight should be centered under the higher ice tool. This puts the climber in balance. The climber must resist the urge to place tools parallel with each other or wide in relation to the body. When this is done, the position causes the climber to become out of balance during tool removal, causing the climber’s body to open or swing toward the remaining tool placement. (See figure 6-49.) This could cause a fall.

6-154. The basic movement sequence starts from the position described in this paragraph. (See figure 6-50.) Climbers hang from a high tool placement and walk their feet up the ice as high as they can go. They return to a stable stance and push their body upward with their legs. Once erect, the low tool is removed. Climbers then place it as high as possible as close to the centerline of the body, and the sequence is repeated.

6-155. With practice, the above sequence becomes more fluid, and steps are combined for efficiency. However, focusing on this sequence helps new climbers to learn what being in balance feels like. Once they develop basic proficiency, they can adjust the technique for their body type and can better negotiate more complex ice features.

6-156. Occasionally, moving diagonally left or right of the high tool is necessary or more efficient than straight up above the climber. In this case, the high ice tool is placed as high as necessary in the direction of travel. Climbers then adjust their feet laterally back into line with the high tool to maintain balance, then climb their feet up as previously described. With practice, the lateral foot movement can be skipped, and climbers can just climb toward their high tool and back into position.

*[Figure 6-49. Basic body position — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Special Moves

6-157. Stemming is the same as in rock climbing and provides the same benefits. The opportunity to do this is created by a groove feature running vertically up and down the ice. Climbers kick their crampons diagonally into the sides of the feature for very secure foot placements. Stemming makes climbing steep ice much easier.

6-158. Climbing over ice bulges is challenging because maintaining a high tool for balance is not always possible. The high tool must be placed low in relation to the body on a horizontal feature. Feet must then walked up the lower face as close to the lip of the bulge as necessary for the climber to step onto the top of it. Crampon placements can be difficult because the bulge may block the climbers’ line of sight with their feet. This series of movements is strenuous for the arms because it forces the climber to lean back away from the ice more than normal so as not to topple forward. (See figure 6-50.)

6-159. Traversing is challenging because it requires the climber to move deliberately out of balance and then back into balance with each move. The lead hand places a tool far to the side in the direction of travel. The climber then makes a short move in that direction. The rear tool is then moved into a new position closer to the centerline of the body. The climber then moves toward the lead tool again until it is close to the centerline of the body. The lead tool is then moved to a position further in the direction of travel and the sequence repeats. (See figure 6-50.) Another more advanced technique involves leap frogging the tools across the ice face to provide successive high tool placements. This requires changing hands on the high tool with each move and passing the rear tool to the lead hand before the next tool placement is made. With either technique, there is a strong likelihood that a tool could be pulled sideways and out of the ice. The climber must always pull straight down when traversing.

6-160. Down climbing ice is tricky but can be done on lower angle ice by Soldiers with strong skills. Down climbing steep ice is very difficult since the ice flow usually blocks the climbers’ view of their feet and transitioning over bulgy features during descent may prevent climbers from even positioning their body to make effective crampon placements.

*[Figure 6-50. Climbing over bulges — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Mixed Climbing Considerations

6-161. Mixed climbing involves climbing terrain that is made up of both rock and ice features. Ice tools are used as described earlier in ice features but are also used to hook rock features or wedge into cracks. This kind of 5th-class climbing requires very high rock and ice climbing skills.

6-162. Climbing difficult mixed routes is impractical for military operations. However, easy to moderate mixed climbing (3rd- and 4th-class) is often necessary in alpine terrain. At the very least, transitioning back and forth between climbing low angle ice and snow and scrambling on rock in a cold environment is often necessary.

### Training for Ice Climbing

6-163. Beginner ice climbing training is typically conducted at established climbing areas using top rope belay techniques (see chapter 10, section III). When supervised by qualified mountaineers, this kind of ice climbing is typically moderate risk and high payoff for developing mobility skill for small numbers of participants, if sufficient time is allotted. Top rope ice climbing sites are typically small or spread out. Training large groups effectively is difficult without special manufactured ice-climbing facilities. This is primarily due to the cold-weather environment in which ice develops. Ice climbing training should be planned along with concurrent training for smaller groups to rotate through. Route difficulty should be challenging for climbers, but easy enough that they can be successful. This motivates climbers to improve their skills. A motivational rather than critical atmosphere is most effective when building climbing skills.

6-164. Climbing training for more advanced military mountaineers can be conducted the same way, just on more challenging routes, but should also be conducted in multi-pitch settings that more accurately reproduces conditions of climbing during military operations. This setting requires the development of lead climbing and route-finding skills critical to the advanced military mountaineer.

6-165. Leaders should try to learn the etiquette associated with climbing before using civilian climbing areas.

## Section III – Load Management for Mobility

6-166. Overburdened Soldiers cannot move safely or efficiently in steep unstable terrain. The amount of weight Soldiers carry and how they carry it directly affects their ability to move safely and efficiently. The heavier the load carried; the greater the Soldier’s capability is reduced. This results in slower movement and the potential for injury in rough or steep terrain. Unbalanced or loosely packed loads have the same effect.

## Load Management

6-167. **Load management is the act or strategy of planning and packing loads to reduce negative impacts on Soldier physical performance**. Excessive loads reduce movement speed, increase the likelihood of orthopedic and lower back injury, and significantly increase the risk of falling in high-consequence terrain.

## Planning

6-168. When planning packing lists, leaders must consider the level of mobility necessary during movement first. Once identified, loads should be reduced IAW it. Commanders should consider modifying requirements for the protective posture for Soldiers operating in difficult terrain. When Soldiers must move dismounted in mountainous terrain, plate carriers, lower profile armor plates, and lower profile tactical load-bearing harnesses can allow them to move further, faster, and more efficiently, resulting in increased maneuver capability. Trends of past enemy engagements in a unit’s area of operation should be considered to determine if ammunition and other munitions requirements could be reduced. Equipment used infrequently must be considered for reduction or removal entirely from standard packing lists. “Ounces equal pounds,” and units that try to carry equipment for every contingency are less mobile and less effective in rugged terrain. (See chapter 4 for general load management guidance.)

6-169. Sleds should be used when heavy loads must be carried over snow-covered terrain. Their use reduces Soldier fatigue and allows for rotating load-bearing responsibilities throughout a movement. Additionally, Soldiers can then drop most of their load when contact with the enemy is made while retaining essential survival items in their pack.

6-170. In steeper less stable terrain, packs should be loaded with heavier objects lower than normal to assist with balance by lowering the Soldier’s center of gravity. Heavy or loose items should not be attached to the outside of packs, as they could shift and cause a fall.

## Scrambling and Climbing Load Management

6-171. Load planning for Soldiers who need to climb rock or ice is a difficult task. The difficulty of the climbing they must conduct, protective systems needed to prevent fall injury, and fitness must all be considered before packing lists are developed. Leaders must be open to developing different packing lists for different teams IAW the mobility level their mission requires. It is likely that only items necessary to the mission may be carried out to make easy climbing feasible. Lead climbers, who are at risk of long falls, may only be able to carry what is necessary to fulfill their role during the climb (for example, finding routes in technical terrain and establishing protective systems for follow-on troops). Any additional equipment they may need might need to be cached, hauled through the terrain, or carried by follow-on personnel.

6-172. Climbers may require smaller packs that allow for greater range of motion when climbing. Occasionally it is necessary for them to attach their pack to their harness, hanging below, or between the legs.

6-173. Weapons must be slung on the back or to the side of the pack during sustained climbing. Lead climbers may need to leave weapons behind or carry smaller weapons, such as pistols, during particularly difficult sections of climbing to reduce fall risk. Follow-on Soldiers who are protected by rope systems would then carry their weapon through the section.
