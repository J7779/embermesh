# Boats, Waterways and River Crossings in the Jungle

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 10. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

In the CCTE/jungle environments, inland waterway travel provides an efficient means of travel for both people, trade, and military operations to areas that are otherwise not trafficable. Most villages and population centers will be situated with access to these river ways. Using waterways for troop and logistic movement, as well as denying use of these waterways to the enemy with severely reduce the operational efficiency of the enemy.

## Section I – Use of Boats

10-1. Waterways in the jungle allow for fast movement. Utilization of boat/watercraft can help with the movement but require steps in planning for the use of boats.

## Use of Waterways

10-2. Waterways should not be viewed as only an obstacle, but also a simple and effective means of movement and transportation. All Infantry units should be able to utilize waterways, as well as a special team dedicated to water movement dependent on the operating environment. A waterway can provide the following:

- Large numbers can move over great distances relatively efficiently as compared to other methods.
- Logistic and resupply efforts can be moved to and from desired locations.
- When operating near rivers, CASEVAC plan should always include a boat option. Casualties can be extracted when air is not available and there are no other trafficable routes to the location.
- Large craft can be used to move equipment long distances that are too heavy for aircraft or ground transport.

## Order of Events

10-3. The general planning factors and sequence of events relevant to riverine operations include the following:

- (1) Complete an estimate.
- (2) Studying the river.
- (3) Selection and preparation of boats.
- (4) Orders.
- (5) Rehearsals. All drills should be rehearsed thoroughly so that all Soldiers and coxswains have a complete understanding of any actions-on to be used during the patrol.
- (6) The move.
- (7) Action on objective.
- (8) Extraction (by boat if applicable).

10-4. Commanders should complete a full estimate from which they will determine the number of boats and most effective method of movement.

10-5. A river must be studied in detail. This study will enable the commander to determine whether the planned move is feasible or not for some sources of information which may be available.

- Charts and maps.
- Aeriel reconnaissance or photographs.
- Previous patrol reports.
- Local knowledge.
- Reconnaissance by watercraft.
- Tide tables and almanacs.
- Coastguard/local harbor master.
- Weather reports.

10-6. There are also information requirements which include:

- Tidal rivers will affect the operation as some obstacles may only be navigable during certain tidal conditions
- The strength and direction of current can affect boat speeds and fuel consumption.
- Width.
- Depth.
- Texture of a river bottom. This is to determine proper anchorage as well as bank conditions for embarkation and disembarkation.
- Depending on the geographic region, the season can dramatically change the trafficability of a river or waterway. If pro-longed operations are occurring, this estimate should be made to support the overall effort (see figure 10-1).

- Rapids. These should always be reconnoitered by a dismounted patrol.
- Other factors include weather, obstacles, debris, and civilian population travel.

## Selection and Preparation of Boats

10-7. The boats that will be used should be adequate for the mission and METT-TC (I) analysis. Improvements to the boats must be made if needed and should be planned into the timeline. Additionally, consider other preparation such as adding padding to paddles if using to approach an objective in a metal boat to reduce noise.

10-8. The craft available to a commander falls into three categories from which one should consider using one type or a combination of boats as follows:

### Military Craft

10-9. (Army) The rigid inflatable boat is a rigid hull craft with a 100-square feet (9.2-square meters) of deck space and a center console design. It can carry 3,815 pounds (1,730 kilograms) of payload with a full tank of fuel. Twin outboard engines are hard mounted to the craft with sufficient power to meet payload and performance requirements. The rigid inflatable boat is towed and fielded on its own Department of Transportation compliant trailer, with an interchangeable pintle ring and ball type hitch with a surge type braking system to ensure inter-operability between military and civilian vehicles. This trailer is compatible with the Light Medium Tactical Vehicle and/or Medium Tactical Vehicle depending on mission specific payload requirements (see figure 10-2).

*[Figure 10-2. Rigid inflatable boat — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-10. (Army) Inflatable Combat Raiding Crafts (I-CRC) are inflatable boats that operate with a paddle for stealth or powered for speed. Each boat is built for rapid deployment, high performance, and reliability. Each boat is designed for a variety of military missions and maritime patrol. The missions include combat reconnaissance, search and seizure, boarding operation, search and rescue, and insertion and extraction (see figure 10-3 on page 142).

*[Figure 10-3. Inflatable Combat Raiding Crafts (I-CRC) — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-11. (Navy/Special Operations Command) Special Operations Craft Riverine (SOC-R) is an armored vessel with ballistic protection (up to 7.62mm x 39mm ball) for the engines, helmsman and gunners. Its hull is made of aluminum with fiber reinforced polymer accessories. The SOC-R is designed to be air transportable, with one SOC-R, its tractor towing unit, associated with small boat team personnel and Navy Sea, Air, and Land (also called SEAL) passengers and all their mission gear fitting into a single C-130 aircraft. SOC-Rs can also be transported as under slung loads by medium-lift and heavy-lift helicopters (see figure 10-4).

*[Figure 10-4. SOC-R — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Civilian Craft

10-12. Civilian craft of any size may be commandeered. These will often be equipped with sophisticated equipment such as radar and navigational aids that would otherwise not be available. Civilian craft can be used to accomplish any of the roles fulfilled by military craft; however, it will often be far less robust and require more time for preparation.

### Indigenous Craft

10-13. Small local craft can be used for tasks such as the covert insertion of reconnaissance patrols, or for patrolling narrow rivers which would otherwise prove inaccessible (see figure 10-5).

### Orders

10-14. A specific boat movement annex should be added into the orders for riverine operations. This annex should include:

- 1. The preparation required for the boats.
- 2. Coordinating instructions between boat commanders and coxswains from a different unit.
- 3. Speed, pacing, and formations to be used.
- 4. Method of movement to be employed.
- 5. Detailed route description while on the water.
- 6. Signaling while in the boats.
- 7. Actions on contact, breakdown, and towing while in the boats.
- 8. River and tidal details.
- 9. Emergency drills.
- 10. Logistical requirements.
- 11. Bump plan.

## Movement

10-15. The drills for embarking and disembarking should be designed for simplicity, safety and speed. They will vary depending on the type of boat being used, military, civilian or indigenous, but must always be rehearsed.

### Embarkation

10-16. The coxswains should position the boats nose into the bank facing the current flow. This should hold them in position while embarkation occurs. The grouping that is embarking should maintain 360-degree security. One member of each section should collect the life jackets from the boats and distribute them to each person. Working in pairs they should then don the life jackets. Soldiers should then embark onto the boats ensuring that all equipment that is not secured to their person is attached to a center-line rope in the boat. All round defensive positions should gradually collapse as members of the formation embark. It may be necessary for the last member of each section to hold the boat and give a push-off from the bank.

### Disembarkation

10-17. If not in contact, the coxswains should position the boats nose into the bank facing the current flow. This should hold them in position while embarkation occurs. If in contact the chances are that the boat will be positioned/rammed directly into the bank. One member of each boat holds it steady while the remainder disembark to positions of all round defense. Life jackets are then removed in pairs, collected by one member of the group and returned to the boats. The formation is then ready to move off (see figures 10-6 to 10-9 on pages 144 to 146).

10-18. Formations should be chosen that are best suited based on METT-TC (I) analysis. Common formations are the file, staggered column, V, echelon left, echelon right, and line.

## Actions on

10-19. The method of contact drill that will be used during operations should be analyzed using the METT-TC (I) framework to determine the most efficient method to support the overall mission. Paragraphs 10-20 to 10-22 are the three main types of boat contact drills.

### Withdrawal

10-20. The withdrawal is only suited to two occasions. A distant contact front and in a contact where the river ahead has been blocked by the enemy or the natural flow of the ground. During any form of close contact, it will most likely cause too much time to be spent in the killing area as boats turn around. It should also be noted that there will still be a requirement to return to the point of contact and clear the firing positions at a later point. If this is not done, then the friendly forces accept that the enemy has control from that point of the river forwards and friendly forces will therefore have reduced freedom of maneuver and ability to influence that part of their area of responsibility.

### Drive Through

10-21. This is the simplest and fastest form of response. It will rely on heavy use of suppressing fire and fast boats. Boats should seek to push through the killing area as quickly as possible and once through should pull closer into the ‘enemy bank;’ the thickness of the vegetation will place them quickly out of the enemy’s arcs. To allow for this burst of acceleration it is advised that the standard patrol speed is 2/3 throttle or less.

### Counterattack

10-22. The sequence of events is as follows:

- a. Contact is initiated. All personnel on that side of the boat return rapid suppressing fire.
- b. The boat in contact maintains forward momentum, to increase the distance from the contact point, and moves diagonally to the opposite bank to the enemy. The Soldiers disembark and return fire across the river, becoming the support by fire element.
- c. Boats not in contact converge on a point approximately 100 meters away from the enemy and disembark. The exact beaching point is dictated by the boat that is closest to the enemy but out of contact. This boat will be able to see the area of the firing point where the others cannot and therefore is vital in getting Soldiers to shore at a suitable location for a follow-on assault; the remaining boats follow his lead. This location is the objective rally point for the subsequent counterattack. Life jackets are ditched, an assault formation adopted, and final preparations made as per for a hasty attack.
- d. Soldiers clear the enemy position on foot. Control measures for this attack will include:
  - (1) A full report from the fire support group giving all available information on the enemy. This should include the gird of the enemy, grid of the fire support group, distance of the assault group disembarkation point to the enemy and a bearing upon which the fire support group is returning fire.
  - (2) Orders, including the following information:

(a) The assault bearing.

(b) Timing.

(c) Method of marking the forward line of own troops of the attack. This can either be by boat, person, or signal in line with the front of the attack and will ensure that the fire support on the far bank know exactly where assaulting Soldiers are.

(d) The signal for the fire support group to switch or cease fire.

(e) Any boat that is on the other side of the contact from most of the patrol (who are assaulting on foot) can deploy Soldiers as cut offs.

## Other Considerations

10-23. Smoke. Some varieties of smoke can be deployed direct into water and still work however this is not always the case, especially if the grenade does not float or it is a phosphorus-based smoke. Experimentation should be conducted to see what does and does not work with the types of smoke grenades that are available.

10-24. Personal protective equipment. Military life jackets are generally designed to be work correctly in conjunction with the correct wearing of issue body armor. A check, however, must be conducted to confirm this dependent on the type issued. Due to the high levels of exposure when on a riverine patrol and the potential for contact, personal protective equipment must be easy to hand for quick use. However, the way rucks are packed and stowed down the centerline of small craft often makes it impractical to retrieve body armor quickly and the wearing of life jackets also prevents it being donned. It is therefore best to wear body armor from the start of the patrol. A steady breeze produced by the boat’s movement and the fact that no strenuous physical activity is being partaken by the Soldiers on board makes this sustainable, even in tropical heat. Life jackets can be worn over the top when on patrol yet still easily removed if disembarking or conducting a counterattack. Helmets should be clipped to webbing and donned in a counterattack situation once the boats have beached, this allows jungle hats to be worn the rest of the time to protect Soldiers against the sun and heat exhaustion.

10-25. Extraction. Extraction methods (if applicable) should be accounted for when planning. This will include location of link-up following operations, order of march, and bump plan.

## Section II – River Crossings

10-26. In a movement through the jungle a unit will likely have to utilize a method to cross a body of water. The following are different techniques depending on mission requirements.

## Waterway Crossing

10-27. Waterways serve as the veins of the jungle and provide life, sustainment, and transportation to indigenous populations and active military forces within the AO. These include rivers, creeks, canals, and other bodies of water which serve as a route.

## Hasty Crossing

10-28. These emphasize speed, surprise, and minimum loss of momentum. They serve as a method to quickly continue movement of a patrol.

## Deliberate Crossing

10-29. This type of crossing emphasizes detailed planning and centralized control. They are dependent on the failure or infeasibility of a hasty crossing.

## Tactical Methods

10-30. Walk through tactical method includes:

- Requires walking through water.
- The waterway must be shallow enough to facilitate walking or have a current that is not strong enough to sweep away personnel (but may be strong enough to knock them off balance).
- Walking pace should be avoided.
- Bodily flotation aids may be used if necessary.
- Poles may be used for balance and for probing the waterbed for obstacles.
- Rucks should be carried over one shoulder so that they may be easily put down if required.
- Personnel should traverse the waterway using sideway steps or in a crab-like manner while facing upstream.

10-31. Ruck raft:

- Made using carabiners or poles to attach rucks together.
- Ruck rafts should be made with four rucks. A fifth ruck may be attached to aid in transportation of a casualty (fifth ruck secured at one end of the raft to support the casualty’s head).
- When using a ruck raft, personnel should enter the water and swim across while using the raft as a flotation device.
- This method is generally only used at the squad level.

10-32. Poncho ruck raft:

- Used as a flotation device while keeping the element in two-person teams and maintaining security.
- Will keep sensitive items dry if properly utilized.

10-33. One-rope bridge:

- Can be used to cross a waterway by tactically extending a squad rope from the near side to the far side.
- Allow the element to use the rope as a handrail or to cross above the water to remain dry.
- Hand-to-hand or hand-over-hand method should be used when crossing a one-rope bridge.

## Alternative Methods

10-34. Improvised bridges, such as preexisting fallen trees, may provide a bridge across reasonably narrow waterways. The tactical situation will dictate whether it is feasible to use improvised bridges.

### Personal Preparation

10-35. Individuals should unblouse trousers from boots and turn their pockets inside out to allow water to drain quickly when exiting the water.

10-36. Daily “de-leeching” should also be conducted when operating in jungle environments; this serves to deliberately drain water from uniforms and as an opportunity to remove land leeches.

10-37. Non- or weak swimmers must use the maximum number of flotation aids possible and cross with strong swimmers.

10-38. Each person must use a flotation aid that is secured to their body for all crossings.

- Ideally, this should be an aid that is deliberately built to serve as a floatation device, such as the issued scout swimmer’s vest.
- Improvised flotation aids should only be used as a last resort. These are any items which will float and can be used to assist swimmers or to float equipment across a water obstacle.

10-39. The following are a few items that can be used as flotation aids:

- Water bottles and canteens. Six empty water bottles with tops well secured, attached to a belt and worn upside down, will keep a non-swimmer afloat.

**Note.** After the crossing, empty water bottles should be refilled when tactically appropriate.

- Clothing. Trousers tied at the ankles, soaked in water and filled with air, can be used to assist weak swimmers. The waist can be tied or held together underwater by the swimmer.
- Natural resources. Materials such as bamboo, logs, coconuts, and the like can all be used to improvise flotation aids.

### Equipment Flotation

10-40. Flotation must also be placed inside all equipment—

- Webbing/TAP should be worn and water bottles emptied to enable it to float.
- Rucks should be made to float by fitting air-filled dry bags inside.
- Foam pads may also be placed inside TAPs or webbing panels to increase buoyancy.

10-41. All equipment must be thoroughly waterproofed. This is ideally achieved by using waterproof ruck liners or the waterproof wet weather bag.
