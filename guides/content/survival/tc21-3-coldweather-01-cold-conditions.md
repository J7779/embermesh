# Types of Cold and Their Effects

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 1. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Different Types of Cold Conditions

1-1. There are different types of cold found throughout the world and each presents its own challenges. For Soldiers to be effective in a cold-weather environment, it is essential they first understand the effects of cold on the body and equipment. (See ATP 3-90.97 for more information.)

1-2. Weather is considered to be cold when the temperature registers 39 degrees (°) Fahrenheit (F) or 4° Celsius (C) above (+) zero and drops to negative (–) 40°F or lower. The Army groups cold temperatures into five categories:

- Temperature zone I (wet cold): +39°F to + 20°F (4°C to –7°C).
- Temperature zone II (dry cold): +19°F to –4°F (–7°C to –20°C).
- Temperature zone III (intense cold): –5°F to –25°F (–20°C to –32°C).
- Temperature zone IV (extreme cold): –25°F to –40°F (–32°C to –40°C).
- Temperature zone V (hazardous cold): –40°F (–40°C) and below.

**Note**. An explanation on how to convert Fahrenheit and Celsius degrees, as well as common United States customary and metric conversion units can be found in appendix A.

### Wet and Dry Environments

1-3. Wet-cold conditions often occur when snow and rain mix with cold weather. This type of environment is often more dangerous to troops and equipment than colder, dryer environments due to slushy, muddy ground, and wet clothing and equipment. Water conducts heat twenty-five times faster than air, causing core body temperatures to quickly drop when Soldiers become wet and caught in blowing wind.

1-4. Soldiers rapidly become casualties due to weather if not properly equipped and trained for this environment. Wet cold can quickly lead to hypothermia, frostbite, and trench foot. Wet-cold conditions exist in many other environments during seasonal transition periods. Under wet-cold conditions, the ground alternates between freezing and thawing due to fluctuating temperatures above and below the freezing point. This makes planning problematic. For example, areas that are trafficable when frozen could become severely restricted if the ground thaws. Waterproof footwear is essential.

1-5. Dry-cold conditions are easier to live in than wet-cold conditions. Like wet cold, proper equipment, training, and leadership are critical for successful dry-cold operations. Wind chill is a complicating factor in this type of cold. Of the four cold-weather categories, the dry-cold environment best enables troops to survive due to low humidity and permanently frozen ground. As a result, people and equipment are not subject to the effects of the thawing and freezing cycle, and precipitation is generally in the form of dry snow.

### Intense, Extreme, and Hazardous Conditions

1-6. Intense cold exists from –5°F to –25°F (–20°C to –32°C) and can affect the mind as much as the body. Intense cold has a rapidly numbing effect. Simple tasks take longer and require more effort than when done in warmer temperatures. The quality of work degrades as attention to detail diminishes in this type of environment. Clothing becomes bulkier to compensate for the cold, causing Soldiers to lose dexterity. Commanders need to consider these factors when planning operations and assigning tasks.

1-7. Extreme conditions occur when the temperature falls below –25°F (–32°C), and the challenge of survival becomes paramount. During extremely cold conditions, individuals can easily prioritize physical comfort above all else. Soldiers may withdraw into themselves and adopt a cocoon-like existence. Leaders should expect and plan for weapon, vehicle, and munition failures when temperatures drop this low. Leadership, training, and specialized equipment are critical for successful operations in this harsh environment.

1-8. During hazardous conditions, commanders and planners assume a great deal of risk if they choose to engage in operations when the temperature falls below –40°F (–40°C). Extensive training is required before undertaking an operation in such extreme cold.

### Wind Chill

1-9. Wind chill is a measure of the combined effects of wind and temperature. Temperature alone does not give a true indication of the impact of cold weather. To effectively gauge the difference between temperature and the impact of cold, a wind-chill scale is used.

1-10. A simple and practical guide outlining the conditions when cold weather becomes dangerous and exposed flesh is likely to freeze can be found in chapter 4 of this publication. Familiarity with the included wind-chill chart helps Soldiers recognize these dangers.

## Effects of Cold Weather on Military Operations

1-11. With its numbing effect on skin, cold hinders task performance. This fact is considered when performing routine tasks such as vehicle maintenance (see TM 4-33.31), and when making and striking bivouac areas.

1-12. When conditions become extreme and the temperature drops, survival becomes critical. Under these circumstances, Soldiers may tend to withdraw emotionally, not wanting to leave the warmth and protection of their shelter or tent. The remedy is physical activity. Remaining alert and active helps alleviate this problem. Execution of all military duties, and the fast and proper performance of group tasks is vital.

1-13. In addition to emotional withdrawal, many Soldiers, when wearing headgear and several layers of clothing, tend to assume a cocoon-like existence. When clothed like this, many Soldiers’ hearing and field of vision are restricted; they tend not to notice their surroundings. Thinking and reasoning can become sluggish.

1-14. This process is characterized by individuals seeking the comfort of sleeping bags, and by the group remaining in tents or other shelters. Guard and security measures may be neglected, jeopardizing the safety of the unit. Talking or even acknowledging others can become nonexistent.

1-15. Group hibernation can also occur. It is a process characterized when an entire unit, usually a squad, seeks the comfort of sleeping bags and shelter. Mundane tasks such as maintaining equipment and even preparing rations are neglected in favor of staying warm. Security postures may also be neglected, jeopardizing the safety of the unit.

### Overcoming Cold

1-16. It is essential the body stays protected. It is kept clean, dry, and warm, with all normal body processes maintained. Rest and nourishment are vital. The right approach to cold-weather living promotes health and provides strength and fortitude through challenging times. There are four basic rules for success:

- Stay in shape.
- Drink plenty of water.
- Eat to stay fit.
- Maintain a good attitude.

1-17. Cold-weather clothing and equipment is heavy and adds weight to the normal equipment load. This hinders movement in snow and uses a lot of physical energy. The importance of being in excellent physical condition in this environment cannot be overemphasized. Developing a tough, challenging, training program is essential.

1-18. Water may be hard to procure, causing Soldiers to drink less than needed. This leads to dehydration. Drink at least 3.5 quarts (3.3 liters) of water per day to avoid dehydration and fatigue. Do not eat snow as a water substitute because it lowers core body temperature. It also contains a lot of air that can cause a very uncomfortable stomachache.

1-19. Regular, nutritious hot food is needed for top performance. Eating, even when not hungry, and calorie intake should be a near-continuous activity. Field rations are designed to be portable, pocket-sized, and should be used by every Soldier.

1-20. Maintaining a good attitude when environmental conditions are harsh can be difficult. Aggressive leadership actions that overcome the challenges of the environment are essential for mission accomplishment.

### Positive Leadership

1-21. While living and working in a frigid environment presents many new challenges, personal attitude about cold weather tends to reflect from the leader’s point of view. A bad attitude and gloomy outlook can spread like wildfire. After 3 to 4 weeks of cold-weather training, most Soldiers can cope with the environment. Some Soldiers may not be as skilled as others, but most succeed. As a leader, set an example for other Soldiers.

1-22. Winter conditions can be frightening, especially when trying to meet new challenges. Any mistake could create a casualty. There are two adversaries to overcome in cold-weather operations: the enemy soldier and the freezing environment. The first step in defeating either problem is having the right attitude. Cold can be psychologically debilitating for a poorly prepared unit if leaders are unaware of the symptoms. Suggestions on how to defeat the cold include—

- Soldiers and leaders getting more involved with others if depression or moodiness sets in so that the weaker Soldier is not left out.
- Reviewing orders and drills if it becomes hard to remember things that were previously taught.
- Reminding Soldiers that their job is to close with and destroy the enemy.
- Keeping weapons and equipment in fighting order.
- Reminders that operations can easily become a camping trip with serious consequences if a common ambush cannot be avoided in a cold environment.
- Emphasizing following an order may take longer due to the weather but is no excuse not to do it.
- Having knowledge, proper equipment, and rigorous training can help anyone overcome the cold and be successful in combat.
