# Moving and Navigating in the Jungle

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 3. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Successful jungle operations hinge on the ability to move effectively and maintain situational awareness. This chapter details the critical considerations for planning and executing movement in this complex terrain. This chapter covers appropriate formations, tactical procedures, and essential navigation techniques for jungle environments. Mastering these skills is paramount for avoiding detection and achieving objectives.

## Section I – Planning Jungle Movement

3-1. The movement and maneuver warfighting function is the related tasks and systems that move and employ forces to achieve a position of relative advantage over the enemy and other threats. Consequently, the meticulous planning of routes, timing, and mobility is what translates tactical objectives into this physical advantage on the battlefield. In the jungle, planning must de-emphasize speed and instead focus on stealth and detailed micro-navigation, often plotting routes along terrain features like streams or ridgelines rather than established trails.

## Factors Affecting Selection of a Jungle Formation

3-2. There are many factors unique to jungle environments which require special considerations while operating as a squad. To address these challenges, formations and order of movement must be slightly altered to fit operations in jungle environments. These factors include:

- Limited visibility.
- Limited mobility.
- Slow movement.
- Limited intelligence.
- Logistical (resupply) difficulties.
- Limited communication capabilities.
- Limited fire support.

## Route Selection

3-3. The jungle environment has a huge impact on the ability of formations to move, because of this there is an importance to do effective route planning prior to any movement. Map reconnaissance is going to have the largest impact on route planning. Higher HQ may give tentative routes or guidance for movement; however, it is imperative that the unit on ground still conducts effective planning taking into consideration the following areas:

- Use observation and fields of fire, avenues of approach, key terrain, obstacles, and cover and concealment [military aspects of terrain] (also called OAKOC).
- Avoid likely enemy contact.
- Speed of movement.
- Tentative halts and sites.
- Countertracking.
- Water sources.
- Time and distance:
  - Solider load.
  - Terrain.
  - Physical conditioning.
  - Illumination.
  - Weather.

3-4. Waterways are the highway of the jungle allowing for the quickest movement as long it can support troop movement. Waterways in the jungle allow less restrictive movement on foot or with the use of watercraft (boat) depending on the viability of the waterway. Jungle amphibious and riverine considerations include the following advantages: vegetation, security, and reconnaissance. The heavy vegetation along the banks of inland waterways offers excellent concealment and enhances the effectiveness of ambushes against watercraft. Unit leaders plan counterambush measures in conjunction with all water movements. Steep, slippery riverbanks coupled with dense vegetation often make committing the waterborne force in a coordinated assault landing extremely difficult. Security measures during the movement phase along a jungle waterway include proper watercraft formations and air observation, when possible. Fire support, to include surface-to-surface, air-to-surface, and naval surface fire must be preplanned for all water movements.

3-5. Leaders plan for waterway reconnaissance. Like route reconnaissance, Soldiers conduct waterway reconnaissance to determine the depth, trafficability, and the impact of the tide on waterways. This information is critical in determining the feasibility of using rivers and other inland bodies of water for movement. Waterway reconnaissance identifies the type and size of watercraft that can operate on the reconnoitered waterway or if on-foot movement is viable. While aerial reconnaissance yields a considerable amount of information, leaders supplement it with reconnaissance by boat, especially in areas where vegetation has overgrown tributaries.

3-6. The unit leader is responsible for the route the patrol takes, but the lead scout selects the immediate path. The lead scout needs to pick the route with care, bearing in mind the other section members’ extra weight carriage. The lead scout should avoid steep banks, deadfall and dense vegetation where possible. After selecting a bound, the lead scout should try to memorize it and move forward without looking at the ground. When this is unavoidable, the second Soldier in the order of march should ensure the lead scout’s vulnerable arc is covered.

## Jungle Mobility

3-7. Mobility in the jungle is difficult because of challenging terrain with large changes in elevation. Mountain terrain is characterized by high peaks, fast moving rivers, cliffs, steep slopes, deep valleys, and a variety of hazardous obstacles. These features pose unique challenges to military operations. Mountains are defined as land masses rising more than 500 meters around the surrounding plain. They may appear as a single peak, long ridgelines, or over an entire range. Cliffs and steep terrain are particular hazards which require specialized training. This mountain terrain is commonly found in the jungle. Chapter 9 discusses specific techniques to navigate this terrain however it is important to consider when planning movement in the jungle.

## Section II – Formations and Order of Movement

3-8. The jungle environment has specific challenges that affect both the unit formations and individual movement that need to be considered when executing movements. Due to the extremely limited visibility and restricted fields of fire, formations must be kept compact, often single-file, to maintain command and control and prevent soldiers from becoming separated. This necessitates a well-rehearsed order of movement, where the lead and rear elements are equipped for immediate action, as contact with the enemy is often sudden and at close quarters. The slow, deliberate pace required for stealth and navigation further dictates that the entire unit must move as one cohesive body, with individual discipline being paramount to the formation’s integrity.

## Challenges in Jungle Movement

3-9. Dense foliage and restricted observation put units moving in the jungle at risk of ambush. To mitigate this risk, units use point, flank, and rear security teams to identify and provide early warning of an ambush. These teams must position far enough away from the main body so that if they make contact, the enemy will not engage the whole force. However, they should not be so far away that they cannot provide mutual support. These security teams must stay alert to the signs of the enemy and should carry as light a load as possible so they can maneuver quickly and easily. The main body and the security teams maintain contact to prevent fratricide.

## Considerations

3-10. The two most used formations in jungle environments are the file formation and staggered column formation. The distance from the lead scout team to the rest of the squad is 10 meters or METT-TC (I) (mission, enemy, terrain and weather, troops and support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations) dependent. Distance between personnel for the rest of the squad is 3 to 5 meters or METT-TC (I) dependent. The distance between each element should be 5 to 10 meters or METT-TC (I) dependent.

## Jungle File

3-11. The most advantageous movement formation for units in the jungle is the file. This formation allows the unit to stay small and allows for quicker movement in the dense foliage and helps the unit leader maintain the best command and control of the formation (see figure 3-1 on page 30).

3-12. Advantages include the following:

- Hand signals are easily passed and acknowledged.
- Movements are simple and Soldiers follow the same path of travel.
- On contact, the enemy are not easily able to estimate the strength of the patrol.
- Movement is quiet and the formation may still be used at night.
- Maximum security is oriented towards the flanks; this is important for reacting to ambushes in the jungle, which are common and deadly.
- Single file forms the basis for immediate action drills (IADs).
- Decreases the change of visual detection.
- Disturbs the minimum amount of vegetation.
- Reduces the noise.
- Single file enables the leaders to maintain control.
- An extended line is normally used in the assault or when conducting a sweep through an ambush or contact area.

3-13. Disadvantages include the following:

- Cannot produce immediate and effective frontal fire.
- Vulnerable to defilade fires.
- The track sign left behind by the formation is obvious for many days.
- The patrol has no depth and may be easily ambushed.
- Little lateral coverage of the ground.

## Section III – Jungle Land Navigation

3-14. Navigation in the jungle environment has specific consideration. At night, this challenge is magnified exponentially, as the profound darkness renders landmarks invisible and can disorient even experienced Soldiers, forcing teams to use methods like illuminated compasses and pace counting with extreme care to avoid giving away their position.

## Night Movement

3-15. The principles for movement at night in the jungle are the same as those for movement by day. The main problem is with visibility and the effects that limited visibility has on command and control. With favorable moon and canopy conditions, movement can be conducted at a slow and deliberate rate, allowing surprise to be achieved and giving the use the tactical advantage. In adverse light and weather conditions, movement is inevitably slower and difficult to control; unless a strong tactical requirement is evident, it will probably not be worthwhile.

3-16. Improvements to night vision, navigation, and communications technologies, coupled with well-trained Soldiers, enable jungle operations during limited visibility. Historically, forces conducted jungle operations mostly during daylight because of technological limitations, navigational challenges, and fatigue.

## Night Vision Devices

3-17. If used correctly, night vision equipment can help significantly with the ease and security of a night move. The following paragraphs 3-18 to 3-20 explain how to select and prepare NVDs for operations.

3-18. Depending on the type of NVD, they require ambient light to work effectively; thick jungle canopy or an over-cast moon causes them to perform poorly. More advanced NVDs can provide an infrared flashlight to improve image quality when no ambient light is present. However, the enemy can spot this infrared light easily if they also have night vision goggles. Detailed planning for the use of these aids and their ancillaries needs to be conducted if friendly forces are to get the most utility from them. Most goggles and monoculars come with a helmet mount and a head strap for use without a helmet. It is, however, quite standard for Soldiers experienced in temperate environments to only be issued the helmet mount. This is clearly a limiting factor as no Soldier wishes to conduct extended periods of movement in the jungle, even at night, with their helmet on. The additional sweat produced and the humidity that the helmet traps around the head causes concerns over increasing the risk of heat casualties and causes the device to mist up. Using the device without a mount or strap by placing it in a convenient pocket and taking it out for use only when required is also an option. In this situation it should be noted that the humidity that the body creates likely causes the device to steam up in a pocket, thereby rendering it useless when taken out for use. Although the head strap is additional weight, it may be the best solution to allowing goggles and monoculars to be worn for a prolonged period during a night move.

3-19. The humidity at night in the jungle is very noticeable, especially at dawn and dusk. This humidity becomes the natural enemy of the NVD, misting it and therefore rendering it unusable. There are several de-misting fluids and cloths that can be used to help reduce this issue, but extra care must be taken not to breathe directly onto the device or place it close to the body for extended periods; both of which cause misting. Some NVDs come with a sacrificial lens over the top of the actual eye piece. This stops damaging the expensive and it is difficult to replace the lens itself. The sacrificial lens, however, acts as a trap for moisture, making it difficult for a device to be de-misted. These sacrificial lenses can often be removed to help improve the de-misting. However, this needs to be a taken at risk by the chain of command as the chances of NVDs becoming unserviceable with scratched lenses increase significantly and therefore reduces the unit’s overall night vision capability.

3-20. Thermal imaging equipment produces a picture by displaying the temperature difference between the elements of what is being observed. The system does this using a black and white scale, where one color represents hot items, and the other color represents cold items. Thermal imaging, however, cannot see through the undergrowth and therefore still requires a clear field of view to see an enemy. Tall grass, a thick patch of trees, and many low-level leaves and branches will therefore obstruct the line of sight and prevent Soldiers seeing the enemy. Thermal imaging is also limited in its definition of the surroundings, with all leaves gaining and losing temperate at roughly the same rate and all appear as a single block of color when viewed on thermal imaging.
