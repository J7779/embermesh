# Anchor Selection and Construction

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 9. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Understanding anchors is an important fundamental that must be understood early in the path to becoming a military mountaineer. An anchor is the point where manufactured equipment for mountaineering meets the surface of the earth. Great varieties of natural features are found in different operational environments that a Soldier could use to construct an anchor. Anchors are found in all rope systems and are built in many ways. This chapter gives Soldiers the tools to assess terrain for the suitability of anchor construction for a wide variety of rope systems. This chapter also exposes Soldiers to the principles of anchor construction that ensure they are building solid anchors in a wide variety of terrain for many different purposes. Anchor selection in mountainous terrain requires a certain degree of knowledge and substantial experience. Most mountaineering accidents occur at the anchor. Inexperienced Soldiers should stick to selecting anchor features that are obviously stronger than necessary and choose simple rigging techniques with which they are familiar.

## Section I – General Principles: Single and Multipoint

9-1. An anchor for military mountaineering is a fixed object to which a rope system is secured and is of sufficient size, weight, and security to suspend the weight of or arrest the fall of an attached load. It is a point in the operational environment, which a rope installation is attached, providing more than enough resistance to a directional pull. Rope systems can be used for moving casualties and equipment. Some, like rappels or fixed ropes, are used for aiding movement. Technical rope systems provide the tools for addressing many of the challenges posed by mountainous terrain. Anchor selection and evaluation together form the foundation of all systems.

9-2. Considerations for selecting a natural feature to construct an anchor are its location and ability to resist any forces that are placed upon it. The anchor should be above any intended rope pathways. This reduces the pendulum effect on any loads attached to the anchor. Another important facet of anchor location is its proximity to the edge. In some situations, having the anchor a short distance away from the edge creates a platform to work on. In other situations, such as setting a top rope, it is best to have the anchor near the edge. This aspect of anchor building can be challenging as the strength of natural features, such as rocks, trees, roots, and boulders; can be difficult to judge. Soldiers must err on the side of caution and leave no doubt that an anchor is strong enough for its intended purpose. If there is any doubt about a single-point anchor’s strength or security, then additional anchor components should be constructed and incorporated until sufficient strength and security are achieved to ensure that anchor failure cannot occur. The strength of an anchor should never be in question.

9-3. Anchors in mountaineering should be loaded in a steady and slow manner. Avoid bouncing as much as possible as it could weaken the integrity of the natural object being used. Sometimes slack can generate in various types of rope systems and cause an anchor to be shock loaded. It is best to avoid any circumstances that may lead to this as shock loading results in very high forces on the anchor.

## Single-Point Anchors

9-4. Single-point anchors are the simplest in mountaineering and serve as the building blocks for more complicated anchors. If a single feature, object, or piece of equipment is to be used as an anchor, there must be no doubt that it is both strong enough and secure enough to hold if shock loading were to occur. The same goes for all rigging used in building the anchor. Correctly tied master points, locking carabiners, and double nonlocking carabiners opposite and opposed must be used for all points of connection. (See figure 9-1, page 196.)

*[Figure 9-1. Opposite and opposed carabiners — illustration not reproduced.]*

9-5. If using opposite and opposed carabiners in place of a locking carabiner, ensure they are properly configured. (See figure 9-1.)

9-6. Single-point anchors are created by simply tying cordage or webbing around something of sufficient strength to form an adequate anchor.

9-7. There are three basic methods of attaching webbing slings to single-point anchors: single loop (drape) with the webbing joined around the anchor, basket hitch (wrap), and the girth hitch. (See figure 9-2.)

*[Figure 9-2. Single-point webbing anchors: single loop, double bight, and girth hitch — illustration not reproduced.]*

9-8. There are two basic methods to create single-point anchors using joined cordelette. They are the basket hitch and master point. (See figure 9-3, page 198, and figure 9-4, page 199.)

9-9. A cordelette can also be doubled before tying a basket hitch. This keeps the attachment point closer to the anchor. Round turns can also be added around the anchor to hold the anchor in place. Certain specific situations require this (for example, a high redirect). (See figure 9-3, page 198.)

9-10. A knot can be incorporated in the basket hitch to create a master point. A master point is the loop created from the knot that is incorporated into the anchor. It serves as an attachment point for carabiners for building rope systems or belays. This can be created by creating a fixed loop with a figure-eight or overhand knot.

9-11. The master-point technique should be used if there is any chance the material could be cut on sharp rock edges when the cord is wrapped around a rock. The knot isolates the individual strands creating redundancy so if an individual strand were to break, the entire anchor would not fail.

9-12. A master point is also useful for creating a clean and easily identifiable working point for use with multiple carabiners.

## Multipoint Anchors

9-13. Multipoint anchors are more than one fixed object joined with technical rigging to form an anchor system capable of suspending the weight or arresting the fall of an attached load. They become necessary as suitable single-point anchors become scarce. This not only provides greater anchor strength but also adds redundancy or backup because of the multiple components. Multipoint anchors are used frequently in mountaineering. It requires a strong understanding of anchoring principles, creativity, and experience applying them in the field to ensure adequacy.

### Angle Considerations

9-14. When using only two anchor points, it is extremely important to recognize the angle that is created. Imagine holding two ends of a rope with a 5-gallon water can in the middle. It would be very difficult to hold the can up with the rope straight at 180°. It would be much easier to support the weight of the water can if the two rope ends were almost touching in the middle. As the angle between anchor components increases, so does the force that is felt by each anchor. (See figure 9-5.)

*[Figure 9-5. Ideal angle required between anchor points to reduce force — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Self-Equalizing Anchors

9-15. A self-equalizing anchor is a multipoint anchor comprised of adjustable strands from each anchor component to a shared attachment point that allows for maximum load sharing even when direction of pull changes during use. (See figure 9-6, page 202.) Self-equalizing anchors are rarely necessary in mountaineering as the direction of pull rarely changes, and there is the possibility of extension and shock loading if a component fails.

*[Figure 9-6. Self-equalizing anchor — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Pre-Equalized Anchors

9-16. A pre-equalized anchor is the preferred type of multipoint anchor used in mountaineering. The anchor distributes the weight of the load among each individual anchor component using fixed and independent strands. (See figure 9-7.) Multipoint anchors orient all strands together pointing in the direction that the anchor will be loaded. A pre-equalized anchor prevents extending and shock loading the anchor if an individual component fails. An anchor is pre-equalized by tying a master point using an appropriate loop knot in the webbing or sling.

9-17. The mnemonic EARNEST-H can be used as a guide to build optimal and efficient multipoint anchors. Multipoint anchors should be—

- E—equalized: Each component of the anchor shares the load evenly. Nonlocking carabiners are typically acceptable links between soft materials here.
- A—angle: The angles between the strands of individual components should not exceed 90°, if possible. The goal is to minimize multiplying forces as shown in figure 9-7.
- R—redundant: The entire anchor system is not reliant on a single anchor component. Rigging of the cord is such that if a single strand breaks or is cut, the entire anchor would not fail. Redundancy is achieved by tying a master point.
- NE—no extension: If one leg of the anchor does fail, the anchor does not extend, causing shock load to the rest.
- S—solid: This is the big picture look at the anchor system. Ensure that there are no problems with how the individual segments work together, that the larger feature comprising the anchor is strong and secure, and that the whole thing is appropriate for the intended anchoring application. Anchor components should not all be placed in the same feature.
- T—timely: This is meant to remind mountaineers to remember efficiency when building anchors. The fastest and simplest anchor system to build that meets the above criteria is the best.
- H—high: This is not required, but if it can be achieved while meeting all the above criteria, then it is strongly recommended. Anchors with master points between waist and shoulder height make operating any attached system significantly easier and more efficient.

## Section II – Trees

9-18. Tree anchors should be considered for use first. They are usually strong and often simple to construct with minimal use of equipment. Trees are already in place and simply require a method of attaching the rope. However, they should be carefully studied and evaluated for stability and strength before use. Sometimes the rope is tied directly to the anchor, but under most circumstances, a cord or sling is attached to the tree and then the climbing rope is attached to the sling with a carabiner.

## Considerations

9-19. The following are considerations for using trees as anchors for mountaineering rope systems:

- In rocky terrain, trees usually have a shallow root system. This can be evaluated by pushing on the tree to see how well it is rooted. If there is concern that a tree may be pulled over when loaded, another anchor should be selected. If this anchor is the only option, the rope should be anchored as low as possible to reduce leverage.
- When evaluating a tree for suitable strength, strive for a diameter at the base of the tree of at least 8 inches.
- In the summer months, it may be easy to determine if a tree is alive. The best indicator can be leaves. However, even trees that have fallen over may still grow leaves if they are still partially rooted. Ensure that a concentrated effort is made to evaluate the tree.
- In the winter, certain trees do not have leaves. This may make it difficult to establish if they are alive. Look at the tree trunk for signs of extensive rot, woodpecker holes, or fungus growing on the tree trunk. Trees that have no leaves in the winter can still be checked to see if they are alive. The tree will have buds at the ends of small twig branches that will turn into next year’s leaves. Major deformities, cracks, or damage are also an indicator that the tree may not be a viable anchor.
- Use padding on soft, sap-producing trees to keep sap off ropes and slings.

## Rope Anchors

9-20. The climbing or installation rope can be tied directly to the tree anchor using several different techniques. (See figure 9-8.) This requires less equipment but also sacrifices some rope length to tie the anchor. The rope can be tied to the anchor using an appropriate anchor knot, such as a bowline or triple bowline. Round turns can be used to help keep the rope in position on the anchor and reduce the likelihood of it slipping on the trunk.

*[Figure 9-8. Anchoring a rope to a single-point tree anchor — illustration not reproduced.]*

## High-Strength Tie Off (tensionless HITCH)

9-21. A high-strength tie off is a single-point anchoring method that retains maximum installation rope strength and releasability, using multiple-round turns around the anchor object to create friction, and securing the end back to itself ensuring no force is applied to any component knots or carabiners. It is used to anchor the rope on high-load installations used for bridging and traversing. (See figure 9-9, page 206.) The wraps of the rope around the anchor absorb the tension of the installation and keep the tension off the knot and carabiner. The anchor is usually tied with a minimum of three wraps, more, if necessary, to absorb the tension. A smooth anchor may require several wraps, whereas a rough barked tree might only require a few. The rope is wrapped from top to bottom without crossing strands so that it can be untied under tension, if desired. A fixed loop is placed into the end of the rope and attached loosely back onto the rope with a locking carabiner.

*[Figure 9-9. High-strength anchor — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Multipoint Tree Anchors

9-22. When there are multiple tree anchors available that do not meet the criteria for a single-point anchor, then multiple trees should be used. Using multiple anchors incorporates redundancy in case one anchor fails. The same principles for multipoint anchors apply for trees and all other anchors. If using only two trees for anchors, then the angle created between the two trees should be less than 90° as described in paragraph 9-14, page 200.

## Bushes and Shrubs

9-23. Bush and shrub anchors are not recommended for use unless there are no other options, and a technical task cannot be avoided. An advanced-level mountaineer should be present to evaluate the rigging and strength of the anchor. They are constructed by equalizing many small bushes and shrubs together into a master point using cordelettes, slings, and anchor ropes. As with trees, the anchoring rope is placed as low as possible to reduce leverage on the anchor. All vegetation should be healthy and well rooted to the ground. It cannot be overstated that the strength and security of bush and shrub anchors cannot be easily assessed for strength.

## Section III – Rock Anchors

## Boulders and Rock Protrusions

9-24. Boulders and rock protrusions make ideal anchors. (See figure 9-10.) Rock features should not be used as anchors if they can be moved by pushing or kicking it or sound hollow or detached when tapped. The surface a boulder rests upon should be inspected to ensure the boulder does not slide when force is applied to it. Rock protrusions should be a solid mass of rock. Crumbly or loose rock formations are not typically sufficient anchors. Talus and scree fields are an indicator that the rock in the area is not solid. All areas around the rock formation that could cut the rope or sling should be padded. It is also wise to consider the consequences if the rock were to dislodge.

*[Figure 9-10. Boulders and protrusions used as anchors — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Chockstones

9-25. A chockstone is a rock that is wedged in a crack that narrows downward. (See figure 9-11, page 208.) Chockstones should be checked for strength, security, crumbling and should always be tested before use. All chockstones must be solid and strong enough to support the load. They must have maximum surface contact and taper with the surrounding rock to remain in position. Chockstones are often directional; they are secure when pulled in one direction but may pop out if pulled in another direction.

*[Figure 9-11. Chockstone used in a construction — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Tunnels, Arches, and Pinch Points

9-26. Tunnels and arches are holes formed in solid rock. If viable, they provide one of the more secure natural anchor points as they can be pulled in any direction (See figure 9-12.) A natural anchor is a natural fixed object (such as a tree, rock formation, or ice formation) to which a rope system can be secured and is of sufficient size, weight, and security to suspend the weight of or arrest the fall of an attached load. A sling is threaded through the opening hole and secured with a joining knot or girth hitch. The load-bearing hole must be strong and free of sharp edges (pad, if necessary). A pinch-point anchor is used the same way as arches or tunnels; it is just formed by a pinch point between two large boulders, allowing a sling or cordelette to be tied around it. Boulders comprising a pinch point need to be large enough and secure enough to ensure they do not move when a force is applied. Sometimes a little digging in snow or earth provides a pinch-point anchor where no other options are available.

*[Figure 9-12. Natural tunnel or hole in the rock — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Artificial Rock Protection

9-27. Artificial rock protection is anything used to protect a climber or anchor a rope system and is not part of the natural environment. The evolution of artificial protection started with handmade pieces of metal called pitons. Now there are several companies that manufacture various lightweight artificial protection. There is fixed and removable protection. Fixed protections are bolts and pitons; they are left in the rock for future use and can be difficult to remove. Removable protection refers to spring-loaded camming devices (known as cams), chocks, and other specialty protection that is easily recoverable after use.

9-28. Artificial protection is used to construct anchors in various ways. Anchors may be comprised of a combination of artificial protection and other objects, such as a tree or a boulder. The combinations are endless. However, when building anchors made with only artificial protection, multiple pieces are required. No single piece of artificial protection is considered adequate to comprise an anchor. A minimum of two components are needed to provide redundancy in the case of bad placement, poor rock quality, or any other factor causing a piece to fail.

## Pitons

9-29. Pitons have been in use for over 100 years. Although still available, pitons are not used as often as other types of artificial anchors due primarily to their impact on the environment. Most climbers prefer to use other artificial anchors rather than pitons because they do not scar the rock and are easier to remove. Pitons still have a place in military mountaineering since they are a relatively light, cheap, and expendable yet extremely strong and secure anchor option when placed properly. They are particularly useful during rocky alpine descents, when an ice axe with a hammer is already carried and natural rappel anchors are scarce.

9-30. One piton is not considered strong or secure enough to serve as a single belay or system anchor. When establishing an anchor using multiple pitons, they should be placed in different cracks or features. A single piton can also be combined with other viable natural anchoring methods, if available.

**Note**. Properly placing pitons requires significant practice to master, which should be conducted in training scenarios involving backup systems to protect the trainee should a placement fail.

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-31. Some advantages with using pitons are—

- Depending on type and placement, pitons can support multiple directions of pull.
- They are less complex than other types of artificial anchors.
- Pitons work well in thin cracks where other types of artificial anchors do not.

9-32. Some disadvantages with using pitons are—

- During military operations, the distinct sound created when hammering pitons is a tactical disadvantage.
- Due to the expansion force of emplacing a piton, the rock could spread apart or break, causing an unsafe condition.
- Pitons are more difficult to remove than other types of artificial anchors.
- Pitons leave noticeable scars on the rock. They are prohibited in most established climbing areas.
- Pitons are easily dropped if not tied off when being used.

### Piton Placement

9-33. Properly positioning or placing pitons is critical. (See figure 9-13 for examples of piton placement.) Usually, a properly sized piton for a rock crack will fit one-half to two-thirds into the crack before being driven with the piton hammer. This helps ensure the depth of the crack is adequate for the size piton selected. As pitons are driven into the rock, the pitch or sound that is made will change with each hammer blow, becoming higher pitched as the piton is driven in.

9-34. Test the rock for soundness by tapping with the hammer. Driving pitons in soft or rotten rock is not recommended. When this type of rock must be used, clear the loose rock, dirt, and debris from the crack before driving the piton completely in.

9-35. While it is being driven, attach the piton to a sling with a carabiner (an old carabiner should be used, if available) so that if the piton is knocked out of the crack, it is not lost. The greater the resistance overcome while driving the piton, the stronger and more secure the anchor is. The quality of the rock around the crack a piton is placed into also greatly affects the strength and security of the anchor. The piton should not spread the rock, thereby loosening the placement.

9-36. Sometimes a piton cannot be driven completely into a crack because the piton is too long. Although this is not optimal, occasionally there are no other options. Instead of attaching to the piton hole, webbing should be attached to the shaft with a girth hitch as close to the rock face as possible to reduce leverage on the piton. (See figure 9-14, page 212.)

*[Figure 9-13. Examples of piton — illustration not reproduced.]*

*[Figure 9-14. Hero-loop — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Removing Pitons

9-37. Attach a carabiner and sling to the piton before removal to reduce the likelihood of dropping and losing it. Tap the piton firmly along the axis of the crack in which it is located. Alternate tapping from both sides while applying steady pressure. Pulling out on the attached carabiner eventually removes the piton. (See figure 9-15.)

*[Figure 9-15. Piton removal — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Reusing Pitons

9-38. Soft iron pitons that have been used, removed, and straightened may be reused, but they must be checked for strength. In training areas, pitons already in place should not be trusted since the weather loosens them in time. Also, they may have been driven poorly the first time. Before use, test them as described above and drive them again until certain of their soundness.

**Note**. Fixed artificial anchors, such as pitons and bolts, are not widely accepted for use in all areas because of the scars they leave on the rock and the environment. They are often left in place and are unnatural fixtures in the natural environment. For training, consider local laws and courtesies for each area of operation.

## Bolts

9-39. **A bolt is a type of rock protection or anchoring component comprised of an expansion bolt or glue-in shaft attached to a hanger to which a carabiner can be attached**. Bolts are often used in fixed-rope installations and in aid climbing where cracks are not available. Bolts provide one of the most secure means of establishing protection.

9-40. Some bolts have rings attached for easy retrievable rappelling, clip-in points for top rope climbing, chains to aid in establishing tope rope climbing anchors, or other special-purpose attachment points.

9-41. There are two primary types of bolts: expansion bolts and glue-in bolts. Both involve drilling a hole into a rock face, cleaning out dust and debris, and inserting a bolt. The difference is in how the bolt is secured in the hole:

- Expansion bolts use a sleeve that expands as it is tightened. The sleeve expands against the walls of the hole to prevent it from coming out. (See figure 9-16.)
- Glue-in bolts are held in place by a high-strength adhesive. Glue-in bolts are used in softer, porous rock, such as sandstone. Delicate rock, like sandstone, may not be strong enough to support expansion bolts. Its porous structure allows the glue to expand deep into the rock around the original hole. (See figure 9-17, page 216.)

*[Figure 9-16. Expansion bolt — illustration not reproduced.]*

*[Figure 9-17. Glue-in bolt — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-42. Some advantages of using bolts in anchor construction are—

- Extremely strong and secure when installed properly.
- Very easy to use for climbing protection when already installed.
- Very easy to use in multipoint-anchor construction when already installed.

9-43. Some disadvantages of using bolts in anchor construction are—

- Extremely time consuming to install relative to other artificial anchoring devices.
- Require the mountaineer to use both hands and hang during installation.
- Require special, relatively heavy tools (for example, drills, hammers, wrenches) to install.
- Noisy to install.

### Installation and Use

9-44. There are enough differences in bolts available on the market that mountaineers should refer to manufacturers’ instructions for installing bolts. Installing bolts permanently alters the rock. The climbing community has strong ethics regarding the placement of bolts. Ethics and regulations vary regionally, but in all areas, it is unacceptable to add bolts to existing climbs. Any bolting should be done with the consensus of the climbing community.

9-45. When using existing bolts as climbing protection or as part of an anchor system, mountaineers should inspect them for anything indicating compromise. The first thing that should be inspected is the rock on which it will be placed. Bolts are only as strong and secure as the quality of the rock. The hanger should not be rusty or loose. Loose hangers can indicate the bolt being insecure, or the hole has not been drilled deep enough to secure the hanger to the rock’s face. If the nut holding a hanger can be tightened to a point, then it is viable. If it keeps spinning, the bolt should not be used or at the very least backed up with additional protection.

9-46. Many chock picks (sometimes referred to as nut-tools) have a hex cut out in them that can be used like a wrench to tighten loose bolts. A chock pick is used in lead climbing to aid a follow-on climber in removing protection. (See figure 9-18.)

*[Figure 9-18. Chock pick — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Chocks

9-47. The principles for using devices commonly called chocks, nuts, or stoppers are the same as with chockstones above. They are designed to work in smaller cracks, easier to place, and more secure. (See figure 9-19, page 218.) The direction of pull must be considered carefully when using chocks.

*[Figure 9-19. Chocks — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-48. Using chocks in anchor construction has the following advantages:

- Tactically quiet installation and recovery.
- Usually easy to retrieve and, unless severely damaged, are reusable.
- Light to carry.
- Minimal rock scarring as opposed to pitons.
- Sometimes can be placed where pitons cannot (expanding rock flakes where pitons would further weaken the rock).

9-49. Using chock in anchor construction has the following disadvantages:

- May not fit in thin cracks, which may accept pitons.
- Often provide only one direction of pull.
- Practice and experience are necessary to become proficient in proper placement and removal.

### Placement

9-50. The principles of placing chocks are to find a crack with a constriction at some point, place a chock of appropriate size above and behind the constriction, and set the chock by gently jerking down. Maximum surface contact with a tight fit is critical. Chocks are usually good for a single direction of pull.

9-51. Avoid cracks that have crumbly (soft) or deteriorating rock, if possible. Some cracks may have loose rock, grass, and dirt, which should be removed before placing the chock. Look for a constriction point in the crack, and then select a chock to fit it.

9-52. When selecting a chock, choose one that has as much surface area as possible in contact with the rock. A chock resting on one small crystal or point of rock is likely to be unsafe. A chock that sticks partly out of the crack is avoided.

9-53. All chocks used for lead climber protection should include a soft material extension, such as a runner, to prevent the rope from wiggling the chock out of placement.

9-54. Chocks must be lightly tugged on to set them so that they do not fall out of placement. Weighting the placement helps to keep the protection in position. A carabiner often provides enough weight.

## Camming Devices

9-55. Camming devices are designed for building anchors in parallel-sided cracks where chocks are ineffective. There are several different types, but they all work by changing from a downward force to an outward force when loaded. This force creates enough friction that the device cannot move, even when no constriction in the crack is present. Camming devices increase force on surrounding rock as downward force is applied. Due to this, care should be taken to inspect the security of the rock on which they are placed.

9-56. Camming devices become even more secure when placed above a constriction. If the lobes slip during loading, they cannot exit the crack.

9-57. Cams are the most used camming devices in mountaineering. They have advantages and disadvantages and require extensive practice and experience to place and use properly.

### Spring-Loaded Camming Devices

9-58. Cams are the most versatile and widely used artificial rock anchoring devices available to mountaineers. Although there are several types of cams, the modern design consists of two sets of lobes that oppose each other and rotate from one or two axles, connected to a trigger mechanism on a single, flexible stem that runs perpendicular to the axle. Attached to the axle is a presewn connection sling. They are designed to work in parallel-sided cracks where a chock would just slide down.

9-59. Cams come in a wide variety of sizes to fit cracks from 0.5 inches to over 21 inches. Each unit has an expansion range that enables it to function properly within a certain width of cracks. It may be necessary to carry many sizes depending upon the route, mission, and intended use. Most come with color-coded slings to help the user select the correct size for use.

9-60. Three quality camming devices equalized together are required to establish an adequate anchor. An exception is two large camming devices perfectly placed in solid rock with constrictions below them. A single camming device is usually placed for intermediate lead-climbing protection. Suboptimal placements or rock features require additional pieces to achieve an acceptable anchor.

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-61. The advantages of using cams in anchor construction are listed below:

- They are much faster to place and remove, for creating anchors or as points of protection while lead climbing.
- They require only one hand to place, so the mountaineer can hold on to the rock with the other.
- They are easy to insert and remove when placed properly.
- They can be more forgiving than other anchor devices if the direction of pull changes.
- They provide many possibilities for rigging multipoint anchors.
- They do not damage the surrounding rock.

9-62. The disadvantages of using cams in anchor construction are listed below:

- They can wiggle or walk out of proper placement, resulting in a compromised anchor, or in a stuck camming device that is difficult or impossible to remove.
- They are much more expensive compared to other types of anchors.
- They can be heavy and bulky to carry.
- They have the potential to generate enough outward mechanical force to damage the surrounding rock and compromise the anchor when loaded.

### Placement

9-63. To place cams, find a uniform, parallel spot in the crack where the rock is clean and solid. Retract the lobes by pulling back on the trigger. Gently insert the camming device and release the trigger so the lobes expand to fit the crack. The spring mechanism works to hold the camming device in place. Ideally, the lobes should be open between 10 percent and 80 percent of full expansion. (See figure 9-20 and figure 9-21, page 222.)

9-64. Avoid overcamming, which can make the unit difficult or impossible to remove, or undercamming, which results in too little surface area contact and reduced stability. The unit should also be placed deep enough to not break the edge of the crack but not so deep that the trigger mechanism cannot be reached.

9-65. Orient the stem to point in the direction from which the camming device is loaded.

9-66. Inspect the final placement to ensure the angle of the lobes in relation to each other is even and within the acceptable range.

9-67. An extension using a runner may be added between the camming device and climbing rope to reduce likelihood it walks out of position during lead climbing.

9-68. Single cam placements can be used for fall protection while lead climbing. Multiple cam placements can be equalized together with cordage or webbing to create load-bearing anchors.

9-69. To remove cams, simply pull the trigger mechanism and gently remove the device.

## Section IV – Ice Anchors

9-70. Fall protection and anchors can be constructed in alpine or waterfall ice using specialized ice screws. Occasionally, natural ice features can be found allowing anchor construction without them.

## Types of Ice Anchors

9-71. Ice anchors consist of ice pillars, ice screws, and V-threads. Except for the case of large ice pillars, ice anchors should incorporate two or more anchor points to be strong and secure enough.

## Ice Pillars, Ice Screws, and V-Threads

9-72. Ice pillars often form in steep alpine terrain. It is sometimes possible to pass a length of cordage or webbing around the whole mass of ice and create a single-point anchor in the same fashion as a rock protrusion or tree. (See figure 9-22.) Ice pillars should be totally frozen to the ground, comprised of dense, crack-free ice, and greater than 1 foot in diameter to use as a single-point anchor. Smaller pillars may be used as part of multi-point anchors.

9-73. Occasionally, ice pillars can be created in ice features, such as sufficiently thick ice curtains where there is a hollow space behind, and a hole can be broken through a thin section with a mountain ax. Then an anchor can be established as above. Care must be taken not to damage the selected ice mass when creating a hole.

*[Figure 9-22. Ice pillar — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Ice Screws

9-74. The ice screw is the most common type of ice protection. An ice screw is a threaded tubular screw with teeth on the end. Some screws have longer hangers or handles, which allow them to be easily twisted into position by hand. Ice screws come in various lengths for use in various ice thicknesses. Newer ice screws come manufactured with a twist knob on the hanger for easier placement.

9-75. The security of an ice screw is determined by the quality of the ice in which it is placed. The thicker and denser the ice is, the stronger it is. Thinner ice, aerated ice, or ice with air pockets is weak; mountaineers should avoid this sort of ice. Deep, thick ice is generally bluish in color, while ice that has air pockets is often white in color. It takes significant experience to judge the strength of ice, but even a novice can learn much about ice quality by the resistance felt when placing the ice screw. Air pockets are felt as a reduction in resistance. The more resistance, the denser the ice. The longer the core grows as it is screwed in, typically the better the ice. (See figure 9-23.) Evaluate the integrity of the ice structure. If possible, avoid fracture lines, the apex of ice bulges, and ice blocks that are not attached to a main flow.

*[Figure 9-23. Long ice core protruding from screw indicates good ice quality — illustration not reproduced.]*

9-76. One ice screw in quality ice is sufficient to protect a lead climber. Two or more ice screws must be placed in quality ice to create a secure anchor. (See figure 9-24.) The same principles apply for creating multipoint anchors for ice screws. If the ice quality is suboptimal, then additional screws should be incorporated.

*[Figure 9-24. Multipoint ice anchor construction — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-77. The advantages of using ice screws in anchor construction are that they—

- Can be placed almost anywhere in an ice flow.
- Can be placed with one hand.
- Fastest protection to place in ice.

9-78. The disadvantages of using ice screws in anchor construction are that they—

- Are bulky and difficult to carry in large numbers.
- Require difficult and tedious maintenance to keep sharp because of the double angle and curvature of the teeth.
- If not removed and cleared correctly, ice can freeze in the core of the screw, rendering it useless.

### Placement

9-79. Ensure all rotten ice is cleared away from the surface.

9-80. Hold the hanger end of the screw, and press teeth against the surface at an approximately 90° angle to the ice with a twisting motion. An acceptable range is +/–20°. (See figure 9-25, page 226.) Typically, several twists are necessary to get it started. The screw is started when it does not fall out of the ice. This is more easily done at about waist level for leverage, especially if the footing is poor.

9-81. Once started, fold out the screw’s knob or handle, and twist in a circular motion clockwise until the hanger is tight to the ice. Fold knob or handle down and out of the way. Avoid overturning the screw once it has become tight. If the screw is tight and the hanger is not pointed downward, back the screw out enough to achieve a downward hanger. The screw should be tight and secure; the threads in contact with solid ice are what absorb any force placed on the screw.

9-82. Clip a carabiner to the hanger, then incorporate into a multipoint anchor or clip rope when lead climbing.

9-83. Remove the ice by folding out the knob or handle and spinning counterclockwise. Clear any ice out of the tube so that it does not freeze.

9-84. Melting of the ice around a screw must be considered. The effective time or strength of an ice screw placement are limited. The screw will heat from solar radiation, or the ice may crack or soften. Solar radiation can be nearly eliminated by covering the screw with ice chips once it has been emplaced. If repeated use is necessary for one installation, such as top roping, the screws should be inspected frequently and relocated when necessary. When an ice screw is removed, the ice that has accumulated in the core of the screw must be cleared immediately.

*[Figure 9-25. Ice screw placement — illustration not reproduced.]*

### V-Threads

9-85. An ice anchor can be improvised using one long screw. This is particularly useful when the climber is short on ice screws or does not want to leave ice screws behind while rappelling. V-threads are common in fixed rope construction in snow and ice. Vertical or horizontal V-thread anchors can be created depending on the nature of the ice flow. Multiple V-thread anchors may be created and joined to create a secure ice anchor when ice quality is suboptimal. (See figure 9-26.)

*[Figure 9-26. V-thread anchors — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Advantages and Disadvantages

9-86. The advantages of using V-threads in anchor construction are that—

- They can be placed almost anywhere in an ice flow.
- They require very little to build many of them.
- Their gear placed in the ice is expendable.

9-87. The disadvantages of using V-threads in anchor construction are—

- Compared to other ice anchors, it takes a long time to construct, and two are necessary for a strong and secure anchor.
- They require a fair amount of experience to use appropriately.

### Placement

9-88. Place a long (19+ cm) ice screw into quality ice at a 60° angle to the ice face, and then remove it, creating a borehole the length of the screw.

9-89. Use the length of this ice screw to measure how far away from the first hole (either vertically or horizontally) a second hole should be bored.

9-90. Bore another 60° angle hole in the opposite direction as the first so that it intersects the first at the back of the hole, creating a roughly triangular column of ice.

9-91. Pass a piece of cordage or webbing through the tunnel created and tie it off with an appropriate knot to complete the anchor. Usually a V-thread tool is used to fish the cordage or webbing out of the hole.

9-92. V-thread tools can be made from a wire coat hanger by cutting the length of wire, bending one end into a small hook and the other into a handle (See figure 9-27, page 228.)

## Section V – Earth Anchors

9-93. Using improvised earth anchors becomes necessary when naturally existing anchors, such as trees, are unavailable. These anchors are created using materials that are readily available. The strength of these anchors depends on the material used, the depth, and the density of the earth in which they are placed. The art of constructing and placing good earth anchors requires a great deal of practice and experience. It is especially important to load earth anchors in a slow and steady manner. It is also important to ensure the direction of the pull is low and does not pull up on any component of the earth anchor.

## Deadman Anchor

9-94. A deadman is an effective means of building an anchor in earth or snow when there are no other natural anchors available. A deadman is constructed by digging a trench perpendicular to the direction of pull and placing a lengthy solid object with a sling or cordelette attached into it. A thinner slot is cut into the ground in the direction of pull and bisecting the trench to form a T-shape. The attached cordelette or webbing is then routed through this slot in the direction of pull.

9-95. The object is then buried. A rope or rope system can then be attached to it. The strength of a deadman anchor is determined by the density of soil it is placed into. The security of it is determined by the depth of the trench. Objects such as lengthy rocks, sticks, poles, and mountain axes can be buried.

## Picket Holdfast

9-96. A picket holdfast can be constructed in the absence of suitable existing anchors. It requires three suitable pickets that can be made from sturdy sticks at least 3 inches in diameter and 3-feet long. The pickets can also be improvised from sturdy metal stakes or poles, if available. It is best to sharpen one end of each wooden stick or picket, so they are easily driven into the ground. (See figure 9-28.)

9-97. Next, drive the pickets in line with the load a safe distance from the edge. Drive the pickets approximately 2-feet apart from each other at a 45° angle leaning away from the edge. The pickets should be driven into the ground at least half their total length.

9-98. Attach the rear picket at its base with a cord. Then attach the other end at the top of the middle picket. Using a clove hitch here will make it easy to adjust so there is equal tension on the pickets.

9-99. Repeat this process from the middle picket to the front picket, then attach a cord to the front picket at its base and create an attachment point for the anchor.

## Section VI – Snow Anchors

9-100. Snow anchor effectiveness is determined by the density of the snow into which they are built. Two or more snow anchor points can be equalized into a multipoint anchor if strength and security are questionable.

## Pickets and Ice Axes

9-101. Pickets and ice axes may be used as snow anchors. The picket should be driven into the snow at 10° to 15° off perpendicular from the lower surface. If the picket cannot be driven in all the way to the top hole, the carabiner should be placed in the hole closest to the snow surface to reduce leverage. The picket may also be tied off with a short loop of webbing or rope girth hitched low on the picket. (See figure 9-29, page 230.)

*[Figure 9-29. Snow picket placement — illustration not reproduced.]*

9-102. When the snowpack is loose or it is suspected that a single picket cannot act as a sufficient anchor, then use multiple pickets if available. (See figure 9-30.) If the snow picket goes into the snow with ease, consider placing it in the deadman orientation to maximize strength and surface contact. If the snow picket requires pounding to place in vertically, the placement is most likely solid. The same general principles apply to constructing this multipoint anchor as any other. Ensure that each picket shares the load equally. They can be placed in succession similarly to the picket holdfast described in section V in this chapter or in a reverse V-shape shown in figure 9-31, page 232. If the snow is extremely loose, it may be better to build a deadman anchor that is dug in quite deeply where there is firmer snow.

*[Figure 9-30. Multipoint snow anchor — illustration not reproduced.]*

9-103. In softer snow conditions, both snow pickets and mountain axes can be used effectively in constructing a deadman anchor in the same fashion that they are constructed in earth (described in paragraphs 9-93 through 9-99, page 228). Longer objects, such as skis, can be used for increased strength. (See figure 9-31, page 232.) Snow density determines strength, and depth of the deadman determines security. Deadman anchors are the most reliable compared to all other techniques described. (See figure 9-31, page 232.) This is the most simple and reliable improvised snow anchor that can be used in a variety of snow conditions.

## Bollard Anchor

9-104. This is an artificial anchor shaped generally like a horseshoe. (See figure 9-32.) It is formed from either ice or snow and constructed by either cutting with an ice axe or stamping with the boots. When constructed of snow, the width should not be less than 10 feet. In ice, this width may be narrowed to 2 feet, depending on the strength of the ice. The length of the bollard should be at least twice the width. The trench around the horseshoe should be stamped as deeply as possible in the snow and should be cut not less than 6 inches into the ice after all rotten ice is removed. The backside of the anchor must always be undercut to prevent the rope from sliding off and over the anchor.

9-105. This type of anchor may be used for fixed ropes or rappels. It must be inspected frequently to ensure that the rope is not cutting through the snow or ice more than one-third of the length of the anchor. If it is, a new anchor must be constructed in a different location.

9-106. A horseshoe anchor constructed in snow is always precarious; its strength depends upon the prevailing texture of the snow. For dry or wind-packed snow, the reliability of the anchor should always be suspected. The backside of the bollard can be reinforced with ice axes, pickets, or other equipment for added strength. A snow-T trench is an example of a horseshoe anchor. (See figure 9-31.)
