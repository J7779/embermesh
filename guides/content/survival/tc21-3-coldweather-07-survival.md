# Cold-Weather Survival: Shelters, Food and Knots

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 7. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## General Emergency Solutions

7-1. Missions typically occur in Arctic or subarctic regions where all personal equipment and Soldiers of the unit remain together. However, it is possible to find oneself alone in a remote area with little or no personal gear.

7-2. Part of mission orders is the route taken to the objective. Memorize details, if possible. Mission orders should contain isolated Soldier guidance. This is detailed instructions for what to do if separated from the unit. A Soldier can be separated from the main troop body, especially when fighting in action. This applies mainly to night battle or bad weather but can also happen if a Soldier is careless or march discipline is poor.

7-3. If all is quiet and there are no battle sounds or aircraft for guidance to friendly lines, go back to the last known position. If that is not possible, carefully consider the journey already made and the distance and direction from the starting point.

7-4. If with others, information should be gathered from the group. Send search parties out to find the next known steering mark. Carefully mark trails so the search party can return to guide the main group forward, or to rejoin the group should their search be unproductive. Meanwhile, the rest of the group should seek shelter. If the search party fails to find any known location, perform the steps below but remember to think clearly and keep calm:

- Stay together:
  - Meet the emergency as a group.
  - Do not wander off as individuals, and never allow Soldiers to straggle.
- Take shelter:
  - If a tent is available, set it up and get in.
  - If there is no tent, build an improvised shelter.
  - The type of shelter is not important if there is protection from the weather.
- Keep warm:
  - If lost in a forest, light a fire (depending upon the enemy situation).
  - If lost in an area where there are no trees, do not use the gasoline stove to heat the shelter.
  - The stove is used only to prepare hot food and drinks.
  - Conserve the fuel supply and get into the sleeping bag to stay warm.
- Check the food supply:
  - All food and water in the group is collected and rationed systematically.
  - Supplement the food supply by shooting large animals and trapping or snaring small ones.
- Check communications:
  - If there is a radio set, use it to contact the parent unit for help.
  - If in a forest, prepare fires to attract search planes.
  - Smoke is better by day and flame is better at night.
  - Mirrors and ground signals are helpful in attracting search parties.
  - Isolated Soldier guidance has ground and air signals for search planes.
- Rest:
  - Less energy is expended.
  - Food is eaten when resting.
- Create an arctic necklace:
  - This is a 30-inch (76 cm) piece of parachute cord tied in a loop with a lighter and lip balm taped to it.
  - A small pocketknife is secured at the knot.
  - This is worn around the neck.
- Carry candles:
  - Candles are easy to light, provide heat and light, and help start fires.
  - If there are no matches, fire can be produced by striking the flint on the waterproof match box with the blade of a knife and collecting sparks in dry tinder.
  - Dry moss and bark make good tinder for starting fires.
- Build fires in layers, with sticks laid out like the spokes of a wheel.
- Create signals with fires since ground and air signals can be made with small fires to form the identification characters.
- Carry tactical, chemical, and strobe lights:
  - A small tactical flashlight or chemical light can be seen from the air or on the ground as far as line-of-sight permits.
  - Strobe lights (infrared or plain) can be seen for miles by aircraft.
  - Every squad should carry a mix of these lights, as well as a VS-17 panel to signal friendly forces.
  - Remember the enemy situation before deploying any signal.
- Survival depends upon clear thinking and resourcefulness:
  - Keep calm.
  - Think.
  - Try to help each other.
  - Stay together.
  - Keep warm.

**Note**. Emergency visual communication depicting ground-to-air terrain markings, hand-and-arm movements, and ground-to-air signals can be found in appendix C.

## Improvised Shelters

7-5. Sometimes tents or other regular shelters are not available. In summer, if the weather is mild, it may mean protection only from insects. In the winter, however, it is not possible to stay in the open for long periods of time unless Soldiers are moving. In case of emergency, Soldiers need to know how to protect themselves from the effects of weather. Use natural shelters such as caves or rock ledges if available. If they are not, build a temporary improvised shelter.

7-6. Building a shelter depends on the equipment and materials available. Some sort of shelter can be built during any season of the year. In open terrain, a shelter can be built with ponchos, canvas, snow blocks, or other materials. Snow caves, snow trenches, snow houses, or snow holes can be built in the winter. When in the woods, a lean-to is usually preferred to other types of shelter. Nature provides the means to prepare shelters. However, comfort depends on initiative and improvisational skills.

### Poncho Shelter

7-7. The simplest type of shelter is made by pulling a poncho over a sleeping bag. For additional comfort, various types of shelters and lean-tos can be made by attaching a poncho to trees, tree branches, or poles. To build a one-person shelter from one poncho, spread the poncho on the ground, hood side up. The hood opening is tightly closed by adjusting and tying the drawstrings. The poncho is raised in the middle of its short dimension to form a ridge with the stakes out at the corners and sides. Snow, sod, or branches are used to seal the side and one end of the shelter for heat retention and additional protection from the wind. (See figure 7-1.)

*[Figure 7-1. One-person shelter — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-8. A two-person shelter is made from two ponchos. Spread the ponchos on the ground with the hood side up and the long sides together so that the snap fastener studs of one poncho can be snapped into the other poncho. Raise the ponchos where they are joined to form a ridge. The shelter is then staked out at the sides and corners. A third poncho can be snapped into the other ponchos to form a ground cloth. (See figure 7-2.)

### Lean-To Shelter

7-9. The lean-to shelter made of trees and tree limbs is built in forested areas. A poncho, a piece of canvas or a parachute, plus boughs, can be used for covering. Depending on the number of people needing shelter, two types of lean-tos (single and double) are built. Boughs should be laid in the shelter like overlapping shingles. (See figure 7-3.)

*[Figure 7-3. Single lean-to — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-10. To save time and energy, use two trees the right distance apart and sturdy enough to support the crosspiece. If suitable trees are not available, use two forked poles or build two A-frames to hold the crosspiece. A large log is laid at the back for added height. Stringers approximately 11 feet long and 4 inches in diameter (about 3 meters long and 10 cm in diameter) are then placed approximately 1.5 feet (45 cm) apart from the crosspiece over the top of the log at the rear. Available coverings, ponchos, boughs, and other building material are then placed on top of the stringers.

7-11. A double lean-to is made by building two single lean-tos facing each other with the fire in between. Any kind of an open fire can be used to heat the lean-to. The best kind for a large lean-to is the log fire. Precautions are taken to prevent the fire from burning too hot or burning down the shelter. A fire reflector can be built of green logs or poles to reflect the heat into the shelter. A fire reflector also serves as a windbreak to keep the fire burning steadily.

### Tree Shelters and Wigwams

7-12. In wooded areas, the tree-pit shelter furnishes temporary protection. Select a large tree with thick lower branches surrounded by deep snow. Enlarge the natural pit around the tree. The walls and floor are lined with branches and boughs. Try not to disturb the snow that may be on the branches. (See figure 7-4.)

7-13. An emergency shelter for one man can be built by cutting down a coniferous tree at a point approximately 3 feet (about 1 meter) from the ground. After the tree falls, the branches on the underside are trimmed away and the cut material used for insulation on the ground.

*[Figure 7-4. Tree pit shelter — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-14. A wigwam or tepee can be built in wooded areas by tying several poles near the top and spreading them at the bottom to form a large circle. Cover this framework with ponchos, a parachute, or any other suitable material.

### Snow Walls, Trenches, and Caves

7-15. In open terrain with snow and ice, a snow wall can be built for protection from strong winds. Blocks of compact snow and ice are used to build a windbreak. Be careful not to get covered up by drifting snow, which may accumulate on the lee side of the wall. (See figure 7-5.)

*[Figure 7-5. Snow wall — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-16. A snow trench provides shelter quickly. It is built by burrowing into a snowdrift or digging a trench in the snow and making a roof of ponchos. Ice and snow blocks supported by skis, ski poles, or snowshoes can also be constructed as a roof. Boughs can be used for covering the roof and lining the bed. Leaning snow blocks against each other is another method. (See figure 7-6.)

*[Figure 7-6. Snow trench — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-17. A snow cave can be used as an improvised shelter in open areas where deep and compacted snow is available. Normally, a suitable site is located on the lee side of a steep ridge or riverbank where drifted snow collects and becomes wind packed. The tunnel entrance gives access to the lowest level of the chamber. This is the bottom of the pit where equipment is stored.

7-18. Make the snow cave high enough to provide a comfortable sitting space. The sleeping area is on a higher level than the highest point of the tunnel entrance, where there is warmer air. The roof is arched for strength and so that drops of water forming on the inside do not fall on the floor. This water follows along the curvature of the roof and sides, causing the walls to glaze over when frozen. The roof is about a foot (30 cm) thick. The size of the snow cave depends upon the number of men occupying it. A large cave is usually warmer and more practical to build and maintain than several small caves. The shape of the snow cave can vary to suit conditions. When the main cave is built, short side tunnels are dug to make sleeping rooms for one or two Soldiers, storage space, a latrine, or kitchen space.

7-19. When building the snow cave, locate a snowdrift at least 10 feet (approximately 3 meters) deep. Newly fallen, powdery, or loose snow should not be used. The depth of a snowdrift can be tested with a sharpened branch or young tree about 13 feet (approximately 4 meters) long, or with a ski or shorter ski pole. The entrance is chosen carefully so that wind does not blow into the cave or block the entrance with drifting snow. The entrance should be built so that it is about 45 degrees from the downwind side. A small tunnel is burrowed directly into the side of the snowdrift for 3 feet (approximately 1 meter). A chamber is excavated from this tunnel. Excavation is done to the right and left, so that the length of the chamber is at right angles to the tunnel entrance. (See figure 7-7 on page 96.)

7-20. Personnel doing the digging become wet from perspiration and the snow inside the cave. They should wear a minimum amount of clothing to ensure they have a change of dry clothes when finished. To ensure that the cave is warm, the entrance should be blocked with a rucksack, poncho, or snow block when not in use. All available materials such as ponchos, cardboard from ration boxes, brush, and boughs should be used for ground insulation. Walking on the roof can cause it to collapse. At least two ventilator holes are used, one in the roof and one in the door. A ski pole can be stuck through the roof ventilator to clear it from the inside. The ventilation holes are inspected every 2 or 3 hours to ensure they have not become clogged with snow or ice. Extra care is taken to keep the air in the cave fresh when cooking or heating. The entrance should be marked by placing a pair of skis, or other equipment, upright on each side of the entryway.

7-21. Building a snow cave requires practice. Large shelters are usually more efficient for squad-size and larger units. The most critical decision is when and where to stop and build the shelter.

### Quinzhee Shelters and Igloos

7-22. In alpine regions and other barren locations, shelter can be constructed by piling up a large mound of snow and then digging into it. Even light powder snow solidifies enough once it is disturbed and shoveled into a mound. This is similar in appearance to a snow cave and referred to as a molded-dome shelter or quinzhee shelter.

7-23. Although the quinzhee shelter may look like a snow cave, this molded dome should not have a sleeping bench. Soldiers dig the entire living area down to ground level (or as close to the ground as possible) and seal the entrance as they would for a thermal shelter. For best results, follow these guidelines:

- When constructing a quinzhee shelter for two Soldiers, clear an area down to the ground:
  - The area should be at least 2 feet (approximately 61 cm) wider and 2 feet longer than a pair of sleeping mats placed side by side.
  - Place a ski pole upright, in the center.
  - Place a second pole along the ground towards one of the long ends.
- Begin to pile snow back into the area.
- After piling up 2 to 3 feet (approximately 61 to 91.5 cm) of snow, walk on the area to pack the snow down.
- Continue to pile snow, periodically stopping to pack the snow down into a dome at least 5 feet (approximately 1.5 meters) tall.
- Let the snow set for about 3 to 4 hours:
  - After this time, check that the snow has sufficiently hardened.
  - If it has not, wait until it does, possibly repacking the area.
- If the snow has hardened, begin to tunnel in from one of the long ends of the dome along the ski pole, at or just above ground level.
  - Remove snow until reaching the center pole.
  - Begin to widen the sleeping area until two Soldiers can fit inside.
  - The ceiling should be arched and smooth to prevent dripping.
  - Ceilings melt with time, increasing the internal dimensions of the shelter.
  - Make the walls at least 8 to 10 inches (approximately 20 to 25.5 cm) thick.

7-24. Survival manuals tout the igloo as a shelter in snow-covered areas. These shelters require a lot of skill, patience, and practice to get right and can result in lots of frustration and wasted effort if not done properly. If not well-practiced in igloo making, eliminate the aggravation and build an easy shelter using less energy, time, and equipment resources.

## Food

7-25. The chances of finding a variety of food in cold-weather areas depends on the time of year and location. Shorelines are usually scraped clean of all animals and plants by winter ice. Food can still be found north of the timberline, even when mice, fish, and grubs are not available.

7-26. If a large animal is killed or a large number of small game is found, store or preserve some of the meat for future use. Freezing is the best way to preserve fresh meat or fish. It may be necessary to protect supplies from small animals. Hang these supplies about 6.5 feet (approximately 2 meters) from the ground.

### Fish and Shellfish

7-27. There are few poisonous fish in cold-weather areas, but some fish, like the sculpin, lay poisonous eggs. Black mussels should be avoided in any season because it is impossible to distinguish toxic from nontoxic varieties in the field. Some black mussels can be as deadly as strychnine.

7-28. In cold regions, shark meat is poisonous. Salmon moving upstream to spawn in coastal streams and rivers may be plentiful, but their flesh deteriorates as they travel away from the ocean, making them unfit to eat (except in an emergency). A salmon that has been in fresh water long enough to turn a brilliant red is decaying.

7-29. Coastal waters are rich in all types of seafood. Grayling, trout, white fish, burbot, and pike are common to the lakes, ponds, and Arctic coastal plains of North America and Asia. River snails or freshwater periwinkles are also plentiful in the rivers, streams, and lakes.

7-30. Fish can be speared, shot, netted, hooked, caught by hand, or stunned with a rock or club. Fish hooks can be improvised from pins, pieces of wire, or any other metal object that can be bent into a hook. Pieces of meat, insects, or minnows can be used for bait. Some fish nibble at any small object that hits the water. Cod swim up to investigate strips of cloth or bits of metal or bone, making them easy to catch. A net can be made from stout twine or the inner strands of parachute suspension lines. These strands can also serve as fishing lines.

### Land Animals

7-31. Deer, caribou, reindeer, muskoxen, moose, elk, mountain sheep, goats, bears, and other animals are found in most cold-weather areas. Tundra animals include rabbits, mice, lemmings, ground squirrels, wolves, and foxes. Where there are trees, the porcupine is often encountered. Porcupine can easily be clubbed or shaken out of trees. Pick up a porcupine only after it is dead.

7-32. Hunting is better during the early morning or late evening when the animals are moving to and from feeding, bedding grounds, and water. Large game such as moose and caribou should be shot just to the back of the front shoulder. If the range is short and the Soldier is sure of the shot, shoot them in the neck. (See figure 7-8.)

*[Figure 7-8. Hunting land animals — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-33. Large animals are fairly easy to stalk and kill, and their skins are useful for survival. Bears should be shot just forward of the front shoulder to stop them, or if the Soldier is sure of the shot, in the neck. To successfully hunt these land animals, Soldiers should know something of their characteristics:

- Caribou or reindeer are usually very curious:
  - It is possible to attract them near enough for a shot by waving a cloth.
  - Move slowly toward them on hands and knees.
- The technique of imitating a four-legged animal may also cause a wolf to come closer to a hunter.
- Moose can be found in heavy brush or around the shoreline of lakes.
- Mountain goats and sheep are cautious and hard to approach:
  - They can be surprised by moving quietly downwind while they are feeding.
  - Stay on higher ground than they are on, if possible.
- Musk oxen leave cattle-like tracks and droppings and when alarmed they—
  - Group together in a circle with their heads facing out.
  - Remain in that position unless approached, then a bull (or bulls) may charge.
- Bears are always dangerous:
  - A wounded bear is most dangerous and should not be followed into cover.
  - The polar bear is a tireless, clever hunter with good sight and an extraordinary sense of smell.
  - Be careful not to become the hunted instead of the hunter.
- Rabbits often run in circles and return to the same place when they are frightened:
  - A running rabbit can sometimes be stopped by whistling.
  - Snares made of wire and placed on small game trails, arranged so that the animal places its head through the loop, are an excellent means of catching rabbits.
  - Snares are efficient and several snares can be placed with little energy cost to the Soldier.
  - Check snares every few hours so that another animal does not take the catch.
  - A snared animal that is still alive becomes quite aggressive when approached.
  - A pole with a noose fixed to the end can be looped over its head and tightened to choke it. (See figure 7-9.)

*[Figure 7-9. Snaring a rabbit — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Sea Mammals

7-34. During winter and spring, sea mammals—seals, walruses, and polar bears—are found on frozen pack ice and floes in open water.

7-35. Seals are hard to approach but can be stalked. Keep downwind and avoid sudden moves. A white camouflage suit helps. Advance only when the animal is sleeping. Do not eat the liver of the bearded seal. Its high vitamin A content can cause sickness. Walruses are found on pack ice and are approached by boat. Due to its large size and boldness, the walrus is one of the most dangerous animals in the Arctic.

7-36. Polar bears are found in almost all Arctic regions but rarely appear on land. Avoid them if possible. If it is necessary to kill one for food, do not eat the liver. This liver is extremely high in vitamin A, which is poisonous to humans. Never eat any bear meat unless it is thoroughly cooked; it is always diseased.

### Birds and Snakes

7-37. The Arctic is the breeding ground for many birds. Ducks and geese build their nests near ponds on the coastal plains during the summer, providing an important source of food. Grouse and ptarmigan live in mountainous terrain and brush-covered areas in Arctic and subarctic areas. Sea birds can be found on cliffs or small islands on the coast. Sea birds, ravens, and owls are useful for food.

7-38. In winter, owls, ravens, grouse, and ptarmigans are the only birds available. Ptarmigans and grouse are very tame and easily approached. Although they are hard to locate because of their protective coloring, they are a good source of food because they can be killed with a stone or a club.

7-39. The extreme northern reaches of the world have very few snakes. The only venomous snake is the European adder found in Europe and Russia. (See figure 7-10.) It is found up to the latitudinal tree line (approximately –32°North latitude). Alaska and Greenland are free of venomous snakes, and Canada only has them in the southern third of the country.

*[Figure 7-10. European adder — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Plants

7-40. Most plants that grow in cold regions can be eaten. The water hemlock and the baneberry are the only poisonous plants. Water hemlock is one of the world’s most poisonous plants and can be recognized by where it grows (always in wet ground) and by the following characteristics: a hollow, partitioned bulb at the base of a hollow stem, spindle-shaped roots, and a foul odor that is very noticeable in the root and bulb. The water hemlock is abundant in marshes near southern beaches and around marshy lakes in interior river valleys. It is never found on hillsides or dry ground. (See figure 7-11.)

*[Figure 7-11. Water hemlock — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-41. Baneberries are normally found on hillsides or in forested bog areas. The white or red berries with a black dot at the bottom opposite the stem are clustered on a central stalk. (See figure 7-12.) The flesh is white. They are sometimes confused with cranberries, but cranberries are individual berries on several stalks. Cranberry flesh is red with clear juice.

*[Figure 7-12. White and red baneberry — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-42. Some mushrooms are poisonous and should not be eaten. Cold-region areas have many kinds of berries that are edible and can linger under snow for most of the winter. Rosehips and high bush cranberries can remain above the snow for most of the winter.

## Knots

7-43. Knot is a generic term that also includes bends and hitches. A rope is only a limp coil of hemp or nylon. It is useful only when the Soldier can attach it to itself (knot), another rope (bend), or a rope or other object (hitch). (See TC 3-97.61 for more information.) There are six basic knots that are most useful for tying rope together, onto equipment, or personnel:

- Half hitch.
- Overhand knot.
- Square knot.
- Clove hitch.
- Double sheet bend.
- Bowline.

7-44. The half hitch is used to secure the end of a rope to some other object or to secure other knots. (See figure 7-13.)

*[Figure 7-13. Half hitch — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-45. The overhand knot is used to make a knotted rope for a hand line, or to temporarily whip the end of a rope to keep it from unraveling. (See figure 7-14.)

*[Figure 7-14. Overhand knot — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-46. The square knot is used to tie the ends of two ropes of equal width together. The knot is secured by tying a half hitch on each side of the knot. If properly tied, the square knot does not slip or jam. Soldiers should be careful to follow figure 7-15 or they may end up with a granny knot, which can slip and jam.

*[Figure 7-15. Square knot — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-47. The clove hitch is the most useful of the hitches. It can be used to tie the end of a rope to a post or eye, or it can be used to secure the middle of a rope without using the ends. (See figure 7-16.)

*[Figure 7-16. Clove hitch — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-48. The double sheet bend is used for tying two different width ropes together. (See figure 7-17.)

*[Figure 7-17. Double sheet bend — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-49. The bowline is used to form a loop that does not slip on the end of the rope. (See figure 7-18.)

*[Figure 7-18. Bowline — illustration not reproduced.]*

7-50. Remember the qualities that make a good knot; they are easy to tie and do not jam or become hard to untie. Also, a good knot will not slip when weight is put on it.
