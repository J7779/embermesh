# Belaying and Rope Systems for Climbing (Part 2 of 2)

*TC 3-97.61, Chapter 10, continued.*

## Lead Climber

10-88. Lead climbers have a lot to consider while climbing. **A lead climber is the first climber to negotiate a route, for whom a top belay is not possible, and who assumes the most risk of injury in a fall**. They should spend sufficient time reviewing the various techniques available for protecting themselves and their followers before selecting one, as well as preparing their equipment for efficient use before beginning a climb.

### Racking

10-89. The protection the lead climber brings with them is referred to as the rack. The term racking is simply the process a climber goes through to prepare the equipment for climbing. Racking should be organized so the right gear can be found and accessed quickly. Equipment should be spread out between a gear sling over the shoulder and the gear loops on the harness. The specific location and order of placement of the protection falls to personal preference, but there should be a logical sequence to the process.

### Climbing

10-90. The procedures for conducting a lead climb with a group of two are relatively simple. The most experienced individual is the lead climber or leader. Leaders are responsible for selecting the route. They must ensure that the route is well within their climbing ability and that of the second climber (“second”). The lead climber carries most of the climbing equipment to place protection along the route and set up the next belay. The leader must also ensure that the second has the necessary equipment, such as a chock pick, to remove any protection that the leader may place.

10-91. The leader is responsible for emplacing protection frequently enough and in such a manner that if either the leader or the second should fall, their fall is neither long enough nor hard enough to result in injury. The leader must also ensure that the rope is routed so that it can run freely through the protection placements, thus minimizing friction, or rope drag.

10-92. The other member of the climbing team, the second, is responsible for belaying the leader, removing the belay anchor, and retrieving the protection placed by the leader between belay positions (also known as cleaning the pitch).

10-93. The leader continues the route until either a designated belay location is reached, or the near end of the rope is reached. At this position, the leader sets an anchor, connects to the anchor, and says OFF BELAY. The belayer remains attached to the anchor until the command BELAY ON is given.

10-94. Leading a difficult pitch is the most hazardous task in roped climbing. Lead climbers may be exposed to potentially long, hard falls. They must exercise keen judgment in selecting routes, placing protection, and routing climbing rope through the protection. Every effort should be made to keep the climbing line as direct as possible to the next belay to allow the rope to run smoothly through the protection with minimal friction. Protection should be placed whenever the leader feels it necessary to prevent hazardous situations and before moving past a difficult section.

### Placing Protection

10-95. Generally, protection is placed from one stable position to the next. If the climbing is difficult, protection should be placed more frequently. If the climbing becomes easier, protection can be placed farther apart, saving hardware for difficult sections. On some routes, an extended diagonal or horizontal movement, known as a traverse, is required. As leaders begin this type of move, they must consider the second’s safety as well as their own. The potential fall of the second will result in a pendulum swing if protection is not adequate to prevent this. The danger comes from any objects in the swinging path of the second.

### Reducing Rope Drag; Using Runners

10-96. No matter how direct the route, the climber often encounters problems with rope drag through the protection positions. Rope drag is caused when there are exaggerated changes in direction of the rope between carabiners attached to protection that creates excessive friction. Minimal rope drag is an inconvenience; severe rope drag may pull the climber off balance, inducing a fall. When belaying the second, rope drag can be mistaken for the climber, causing the belayer to not take in the necessary slack. This can potentially result in a serious fall. To prevent this, the placements should be extended by using runners, when necessary, so the rope creates a smooth, almost straight line as it passes through the carabiners. (See figure 10-16.) Do this by attaching an appropriate-length runner to the protection to extend the rope connection in the necessary direction. The runner is attached to the protection while the rope is clipped into a carabiner at the opposite end of the runner. Extending placements with runners allows the climber to vary the route slightly while the rope continues to run in a relatively straight line.

*[Figure 10-16. Use of runners to extend and reduce rope drag — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-97. Not only is rope drag a hindrance, but it can also cause undue movement of protection as the rope tightens between any out of line placements. Rope drag through chock placements can be dangerous. As the climber moves above the placements, an outward or upward pull from rope drag may cause correctly set chocks to pop out, even when used correctly. Passive protections, like chocks, are especially prone to wiggling loose as the rope pulls on the cable attachment. All wired chocks used for leader protection should be extended to reduce the chance of the rope pulling them out. (See figure 10-17, page 262.)

### Clipping Technique

10-98. Clip the climbing rope into a point of protection by reaching down and grabbing the rope between the thumb and index finger. Use the middle finger to stabilize the carabiner at the protection or runner, and use the thumb and index finger to clip the rope in. Alternately, the rope can be held between the middle and index finger and clipped in as shown in figure 10-18. Ensure the gate of the carabiner faces out (not towards the rock), and the rope is clipped in the direction of travel to prevent the rope from potentially becoming unclipped in a fall. Recreational climbers typically do all their clipping while hanging onto rock holds as much as they can; however, as a Soldier not worried about preserving the free climbing style, simply grab the point of protection and hang onto it while clipping the rope and runner into it. This may provide better temporary hold or provide a much-needed rest.

### Belaying a Second Climber

10-99. Typically, one climber takes the lead while belayed from below, moves up the route to the next ledge or desired spot, and then sets up a new anchor and belay. This distance between these belays is known as a pitch. The length of a pitch is limited to the length of available rope and convenient, suitable to belay positions.

10-100. In most cases, the leader attaches to the anchor with either separate sling material or by using the climbing rope with a clove hitch and locking carabiner. Using a clove hitch in the climbing rope itself has the distinct advantage of allowing more room and material to fine tune the distance between the anchor and the cliff edge to enable better communication with the climber below. The leader then uses the command of OFF BELAY. Once the belayer has removed the belay, enabling the rope to run freely, the belayer would use the command BELAY OFF. The leader then pulls in all the remaining rope until it is tight with the second. Once an appropriate belay is set up, the leader uses the command BELAY ON letting the second know that it is safe to climb.

10-101. Many techniques can be used to belay the second, depending upon the terrain, difficulty of the climbing, availability of anchors, and likelihood or consequences of a fall. The second is essentially on a top rope, with a snug rope being maintained throughout the climb.

### Indirect Belays

10-102. An indirect belay occurs when a leader is anchored and belays the second off the harness with either a Munter hitch or tube-style belay device. While this does work, it has several drawbacks. It can oftentimes be quite awkward in the event of a fall that pulls the belayer down towards the ground or if there is a climber who is struggling and hanging excessively on the rope. The belayer is forced to hold the rope in the brake position with much or all the climber’s weight pulling in a downward direction on the harness. Additionally, should belayers need to tie off the climber and escape the belay for any reason, several steps would be required to free themselves from the system. For these reasons, this technique is only preferred when using an anchor that is suspected to be weak. With a weak or bad anchor, belayers’ bodies, in this case, act as the primary anchor with the other simply backing them up. Once again, a good, braced position is necessary. The preferred technique otherwise is to always use direct belays.

### Direct Belays

10-103. Belaying directly off the anchor allows any forces to be transmitted solely to the anchor so that the belayer is not pulled in any direction and cannot become trapped in the system should problems arise. Direct belays are also generally more comfortable and give the belayer freedom to move while maintaining control of the brake rope. This can be done either using a Munter hitch or using a plaquette-style belay device in guide mode. In either case, the first step is for the climber to attach to the anchor and shout, OFF BELAY. Pull the remainder of the rope up until the second shouts, THAT’S ME. Then use one of the following belay methods: the Munter hitch or the autolocking tubular device in guide mode.

### Belaying with the Munter Hitch

10-104. Using a Munter hitch as a belay is quick, easy to tie, and can be used to easily lower the second back down, if necessary. It is an alternative in case a Soldier does not have a belay device. Attach a locking carabiner to the anchor master point, pull the slack out of the rope to the climber below, and tie a Munter hitch with the climbing rope. (See figure 10-19.)

10-105. Place the guide hand on the load strand and the brake hand on the brake strand a few inches away from the Munter hitch, ensuring both thumbs are pointing towards the anchor. To take in rope, hold firmly and slide both hands towards each other until they meet. With the guide hand, grab both strands of rope just beneath the brake hand, then slide the brake hand back up to within a few inches of the Munter hitch. Slide the guide hand back down and repeat. Care should be taken to ensure that the brake hand does not get so close to the Munter hitch that a thumb or finger could be sucked in and pinched during a fall.

*[Figure 10-19. Belaying using a Munter hitch — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Autolocking Tubular-Style Device

10-106. The tube-style belay or rappel devices issued in the Army mountain kits can be used in guide or autolocking mode. To use this device in autolocking mode, a locking carabiner is clipped through the metal loop and attached to an anchor. A bight of rope is then fed into the device, with the climber’s strand on top, and a second “braking” locking carabiner is clipped through the device’s carry wire and the bight of rope. One locking carabiner is used to attach the device itself to the anchor, and a second is used as a braking carabiner after the rope is fed through the device. The shape and orientation of the device allows rope to pass smoothly through it in one direction, but lock off in the opposite direction, being pinched around the stock of the braking carabiner. (See figure 10-20, page 266.)

10-107. To use the device, attach it to the anchor with a locking carabiner in the appropriate spot. Then feed a bight of rope into the device, paying close attention to the diagram on its side. Clip an additional locking carabiner around both the rope and the keeper, ensuring again that the brake strand is between the Soldier’s body and the load rope. Pull down on the load rope to make sure it is rigged correctly and locks off in the correct direction before shouting ON BELAY. To take in rope with a device in guide mode, use the same technique described above for the Munter hitch.

10-108. One advantage of these devices over the Munter hitch is that they do not require holding power on the part of the belayer because they lock off automatically, oftentimes allowing the belayer to multitask at the anchor. They can also be used when using half or twin ropes or when belaying two seconds simultaneously on two separate ropes. They are particularly useful in these situations or if the climbing is difficult and the second is expected to be hanging a lot. A disadvantage is that since it only allows rope to pass through in one direction, giving “slack” or lowering a climber back down requires additional training.

**Note**. Never use a device in standard tube-style mode to belay directly off the anchor.

### Rope Management While Belaying

10-109. Pay careful attention to ensure that rope taken in when belaying the second is stacked or coiled neatly to make the belay transition easy and to ensure that it does not fall down the cliff and become tangled or caught.

### On Ledges

10-110. On small or large ledges, it is usually possible to stack the rope in a neat pile as it is pulled through the belay. This allows for an easy transition and for smooth rope feeding.

### At Hanging Stances

10-111. At hanging belay stances, it may be necessary to stack the rope back and forth over the tie-in strand as the Soldier pulls rope through the belay. If this is done, the rope should be looped back and forth in large bights (not so long as to becoming caught on things below). If the second becomes the new leader for the next pitch, these bights should start big and gradually become smaller. This allows the rope to feed out without becoming tangled during the next lead. If the original leader leads the next pitch, the rope may have to be restacked over the second’s tie-in strand when they reach the anchor. Another option is to stack the rope back and forth over a leg or foot. However, feet tend to get shifty when belaying during a long lead, which can lead to dropping the rope where it could possibly become stuck or jammed below.

### Rope Tug Commands

10-112. Sometimes yelling cannot be heard when the climber and belayer are far apart. This is especially true in windy conditions or if the climber is around a corner, above an overhang, or at the back of a ledge. It may be necessary to use a series of tugs on the rope in place of the standard voice commands. To avoid any possible confusion with interpreting multiple rope tug commands, use only one. Rope tug commands may also have a tactical application where noise discipline is particularly important. Units or climbing teams should develop their own SOPs for rope tug commands. These commands should always be discussed and confirmed prior to a climb.

### Training Considerations for Lead Climbing

10-113. Due to the high consequences involved, advanced and master military mountaineers must plan and supervise mountaineering training that involves lead climbing. They advise the commander on training requirements, priorities, and equipment needs, as well as plan, organize, and conduct training. Lead climbing training is particularly demanding of trainers and supervisors. For inexperienced climbers, a 2:1 ratio is often required. Supervision of climbing training must be thorough and deliberate. A large part of this supervision is inspecting every component of the rope system. The ability to balance the rigid process of supervision and inspection with the soft skills of mentorship and coaching can help maximize the safety and value of the training.

10-114. Assess the mental readiness of the climber. The fear of heights is real and is not something that can be ignored. A Soldier becoming panicked while lead climbing can create hazardous situations and may dissuade the Soldier from continuing further climbing training.

10-115. Consider climber skill when selecting routes for training. Climbs should be challenging but achievable with a couple of tries. Routes for new climbers should not exceed intermediate difficulty (see appendix A for more on climbing classification). A common mistake is to place Soldiers on climbs that are too difficult, steep, or strenuous, where they cannot first focus on the processes. Established bolted climbs are often an excellent place to begin lead climbing training. Traditionally protected routes can be attempted after proficiency has been achieved with these. For initial training, the length of climbs is ideally less than half the length of the available ropes to allow quick transitions to rappel and maximize repetitions. These shorter lengths also make it easier for the supervising trainer to ascend next to the climber when necessary.

10-116. Soldiers should already be proficient at placing protection, clipping ropes into protection, belaying leaders, belaying follow-on climbers, and constructing multipoint anchors. These skills can often be practiced on the ground in advance.

10-117. Mock lead training is a useful tool to prepare Soldiers for lead climbing. Mock leading occurs when a Soldier climbs a route on top rope while also leading up with another rope. This allows the Soldier to focus on placing protection, clipping, and managing the rope on lead with the security of having a top rope as backup in case of a fall.

## Section VI – Alpine Belay Techniques

10-118. In broken alpine terrain, with secure stances, and no chance of second climbers falling and hanging from a belay, hasty techniques may provide adequate protection for the team. Safety should never be compromised for the sake of speed. Use of standard belay techniques should be used if there is any question as to security.

## Body Belays

10-119. Body belays can sometimes be used on short sections of less steep terrain where a fall is unlikely and there is a good, braced belay stance of which to take advantage. To create a body belay, simply run the rope around the body at the waist to create friction, with the chest facing the direction of pull. The load rope is at one hip and the brake strand is in the opposite side hand. To take in rope, pull in on the load rope with the guide hand and out with the brake hand. Grab both ropes above the brake hand with the guide hand and slide the brake hand back towards the body. To brake, pull the brake hand up and across the chest. The body belay can be used in either the sitting or standing position; however, the sitting position offers a lower center of gravity and is preferred. It can also be used with an anchor or without. The advantage of the body belay is its speed and simplicity for belaying short sections where a climber is moving quickly. However, there are disadvantages as well. Because the force of a fall is dissipated as friction against belayers’ bodies, serious rope burns can occur to the body and hands, possibly causing them to lose control of the belay. It also generates less braking force and causes more slippage than other techniques, causing the climber to fall farther. For this reason, it is only used on low-angle easy terrain. The belayer must have a good, braced position and must keep the rope tight. (See figure 10-21.)

## Shoulder Belay

10-120. A shoulder belay is a way to add a level of friction to the rope as the belayer pulls it tight with a climber below. The shoulder belay shares many of the advantages and disadvantages listed above for the body belay. The level of friction created with this method is relatively low and should only be used on short steps where there is no risk of the climber hanging from the belay in a fall. A classic example of terrain where the shoulder belay is appropriate is a short, steep step where the belayer can stand directly above the climber below. The rope is routed under the arm, over the opposite shoulder, and down to the opposite hand and is kept tight with the climber below. The belayer’s body creates friction on the rope and acts as the anchor at the same time. A shoulder belay is fast to create, but it is only suitable on rare occasions. (See figure 10-22, page 270.)

*[Figure 10-22. Shoulder belay — illustration not reproduced.]*

10-121. A terrain belay can be used when there is a feature that the rope can go around and create enough friction to arrest a fall or lower a load. The natural feature must be solid and not have sharp edges. Terrain belays are an effective means of belaying a climbing partner, but they require good judgement. When using a boulder or horn of rock, the belayer must ensure the rope will not slide off the rock in the event of a fall and that the rock is wedged securely. Trees are another good example of a natural object that can be used as a terrain belay. (See chapter 9 for more information on tree selection.) (See figure 10-23.)

## Section VII – Climbing in a Team of Three

10-122. Level 2 mountaineers are trained to work as a team of two. There are numerous examples of when it may be beneficial to work and move as a team of three. Moving as a team of three has added complexity and much more challenging rope management at each belay. To progress into managing two ropes, a climber must first become proficient in managing one.

10-123. It is good for military mountaineers to have options for roped travel through terrain with changing hazards. The mountaineer must constantly assess terrain and hazards and fluidly adapt the technique to mitigate those hazards as best as possible. Time is always against mountaineers, and efficient transitions from one roped movement style to the next are important. A mountaineer should not default to using one style of movement for all types of terrain and should know how to properly apply different roped movement techniques to different terrain.

## Caterpillar

10-124. Caterpillar technique is usually the first technique learned for managing a party of three. The lead climber and second climber tie into the ends of one rope. The second also ties into another rope with the third climber. The leader leads as usual, clipping protection, and then builds an anchor and belays the second. Rather than cleaning the protection, the second climbers remove their rope and clip the second rope into the protection. Once the second climber is anchored, the leader then belays the third climber.

10-125. Climbing while using the caterpillar technique is often slower than other techniques. It can take the party twice as long to cover a pitch in some situations but is the simplest method of managing a party of three. It builds on the skills already used for two-party climbing, with only slightly more complex rope and station management. This technique also reduces the workload on the following climbers.

## End Roping

10-126. In easy terrain, two climbers can tie into the end of the same rope. One ties into the end of the rope, the second attaches with a cow’s tail and a bight of rope long enough that they can move up and down independent of the climber below. (See figure 10-24, page 272.) It is tied with an overhand on a bight loop with a 30-cm bight. This bight should be far enough from the other climber that the two do not interfere with each other as they climb, usually about 4 meters. The second attaches to the cow’s tail with a clove hitch around two opposite and opposed locking carabiners. This is almost as fast as climbing in a party of two. Rope management is simple for the leader and no different from climbing in a party of two. The two followers can be clove hitched into the anchor on the rope between them, securing them both. The followers can also coach and assist each other with the climbing.

10-127. This is only appropriate in easy terrain, where there is little risk of a fall. It is used primarily in 4th-class terrain.

*[Figure 10-24. Clipping to a cow’s tail and end roping — illustration not reproduced.]*

**Note**. When attaching to a rope to climb, the preferred method is to be tied directly into the rope. Locking carabiners can unlock and open if they are not being monitored, and while climbing, they may end up in a position where they can be cross loaded. When clipping into a rope to climb, it should be with two lockers, opposite and opposed, and with a knot that secures around the carabiners to keep them oriented correctly.

## Parallel

10-128. For parallel technique, the lead climber ties into both ropes and the two followers tie into the other ends. The leader leads on one strand, clipping it into the protection. The second rope can either be clipped into the protection as well, clipped into just specific pieces as directional, or routed on a separate line, depending on the terrain and the hazards. (See figure 10-25.) At the anchor, the leader sets up to belay the two followers.

10-129. Belay devices with a guide mode (plaquette or autolocking) can be used to belay two climbers, on two ropes, simultaneously. For rock climbing, with little rockfall hazard, both followers often climb together, offset enough so that they do not fall onto each other. Where loose rock is a concern or when ice climbing, this must be managed differently, often one climber climbing at a time, either for the whole pitch, or bounding from sheltered stance to sheltered stance. (See figure 10-26.)

10-130. Rope management is more difficult with two ropes and two climbers moving simultaneously. Organization at the belays requires additional thought, ensuring that the followers are anchored in a manner that the ropes are not twisted, and both strands feed up from them.

10-131. The parallel technique is used frequently. In steep terrain, it is often the fastest way to move as a party of three. However, to avoid complications, it requires mastery of the basics and practice.

## Fixed Strands

10-132. It is also possible for leaders to fix the strand of rope they led onto the anchor at the top. This frees up leaders from any belaying responsibilities and allows them the ability to provide security, if necessary. This concept of the leader fixing strands of rope after leading a pitch provides many options for moving Soldiers through terrain. Leaders are not limited to just fixing the strand of rope they led on. They can pull up the rope, fix the middle, and have two strands for others to ascend if the rope reaches. The leader could trail another rope and fix it in another location after having reached the top. Having second climbers ascend rather than climb removes any possibility of a second not being able to reach the top due to a lack of climbing ability. The second would then ascend the rope rather than climb on belay. It is not appropriate in all terrain due to various terrain hazards. The weighted rope can dislodge loose rock. The rope is easily damaged or cut if it runs over a sharp edge while the followers ascend. (See chapter 11 for more information on ascending ropes.)
