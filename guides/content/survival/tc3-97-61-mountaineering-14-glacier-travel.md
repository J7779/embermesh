# Glacier Travel and Crevasse Rescue

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 14. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Ten percent of the Earth’s land mass is covered by glaciers. Glaciers are rivers of ice flowing under the influence of gravity, constantly restructuring the mountains around them. They often provide the best route in otherwise impassable terrain but can contain crevasses and other hazards that can cause injury, death, or compromise a unit’s mission. Soldiers operating in glaciated terrain must understand these hazards, be well versed in the fundamentals of glacier travel, and be proficient with crevasse rescue techniques.

## Section I – Glacier Fundamentals

14-1. Glaciers can be large enough to cover an entire continent. They may move only inches or hundreds of feet a year. Movement is caused by gravity, slippage over the bedrock, or by the internal flow in the ice. It is important for route planning and risk management to understand glacial features and understand how they form and move. Dismounted movement across glaciers is hazardous; however, glaciers often provide the easiest passage through more technical terrain on surrounding slopes and faces.

## Glaciers

14-2. Glaciers form over time by the weight of accumulating snow compressing older layers of snow into ice. As snow accumulates, gravity takes over and the ice begins to move downhill. The weight of millions of pounds of ice makes a small film of water that acts as a lubricant, enabling it to slide.

14-3. The upper part of the glacier where the annual snow accumulation exceeds the melt is called the accumulation zone. Further down the glacier, the area where melt exceeds accumulation is an area called the ablation zone. The imaginary line between the two zones is known as the firn line.

### Classification

14-4. There are two classifications of glaciers: alpine glaciers and ice caps. Alpine glaciers form in high mountain bowls. Ice caps can cover thousands of square miles, such as those that cover Greenland and Antarctica.

### Types of Alpine Glaciers

14-5. The following are four types of glaciers:

- Valley glacier—resides and flows in a valley.
- Cirque glacier—forms and resides in a bowl. These do not advance beyond the bowl.
- Hanging glacier—these are a result of valley or cirque glaciers flowing and deteriorating. As the movement continues, portions separate and are sometimes left hanging on mountains, ridgelines, or cliffs.
- Piedmont glacier—formed by one or more valley glaciers, spreads out into a large area.

## Glacier Obstacles

14-6. Units intending to use glaciers for travel routes must be familiar with their hazards and characteristics to ensure safe and efficient movement on them. Failure to identify and prepare for obstacles, which can come in the form of vertical terrain, can slow or prevent movement. Failure to identify and prepare for hazards can result in significant injury or loss of life.

14-7. Glaciers contain many different features that can impede travel. (See figure 14-1.) They are—

- Bergschrund—the bergschrund is a giant crevasse found at the upper limit of glacier movement. It is formed where the moving glacier breaks away from the ice cap or snowfield above. The bergschrund can be high and overhanging and can create an obstacle to movement when moving from the valley floor onto the mountainsides.
- Crevasses—crevasses are cracks in the glacier caused by irregularities in the bedrock under the glacier as it moves and flows. Crevasses often form where the angle of the slope increases greatly, causing tension to split open the snow and ice. They also commonly form wherever the glacier makes a turn. They can be transverse, longitudinal, or lateral, depending on their orientation to the direction of the glacier’s movement. Crevasses can be 200-feet deep but are normally 40- to 100-feet deep. Crevasses often hinder travel across glaciers. Snow often conceals crevasses, causing possible major falling hazards to Soldiers. Bypassing them can increase travel time and distance. Snow bridges that form over them can give way without warning. During the summer, the ice is less covered with snow and most crevasses are exposed. They can still be hazardous at night or in reduced visibility.
- Moat—the moat is a gap formed where the snow and ice are melted by the warmer rock along the edge of a glacier. The edge of a moat is often undercut. Depending on its size, the moat may be an obstacle to moving on or off the glacier.
- Icefalls—icefalls result from the flow of the glacier down a steep ridge. The ridge forces the ice up into a jumbled mass as the glacier flows over the rock. These present formidable obstacles and should be avoided.
- Serac—seracs are ice walls and towers that form in icefalls or on the lower edge of hanging glaciers. They are unstable and can fall unexpectedly. Travel below seracs or hanging glaciers should be avoided when possible.
- Nunatak—a nunatak is an island of bedrock that protrudes up through the glacier, creating an obstacle to glacial movement. It creates pressure in the ice that can form crevasses, seracs, and icefalls.
- Water hazards—streams that run on the surface and then disappear into a hole are called moulins. Moulins form when meltwater from the glacier finds a crevasse and moves to the ground below the ice. A fall into a moulin can carry a Soldier into the glacier and result in almost certain drowning. The glacial streams above this point must also be treated with respect. They are cold, can be deep, and the slick walls of their channels make them difficult to escape. In the summer, glacial thaw water can form troughs. This water can freeze at night, forming glacial swamps.
- Steep slopes—glaciers often include steep slopes that can create falling hazards, especially in firm snow conditions.
- Moraines—moraines are piles of rock and other debris that have either fallen onto the glacier or been pried loose by it as it moves along. There are four basic types:
  - Lateral moraine—this is the rock debris along the valley wall.
  - Medial moraine—formed when two glaciers come together, and the lateral moraines are forced out into the middle of the glacier.
  - Terminal moraine—the rock debris at the toe of the glacier, which is being dredged up and pushed down the valley. A glacial stream often flows out from under the terminal moraine.
  - Ground moraine—the rocks and debris under the glacier that can be exposed if the glacier stops and then begins to move again.
  - Generally, the outside of a moraine wall is stable, but the side facing the glacier can be steep and loose and should be avoided. A moraine may be the only path, but it can be awkward and tiring due to the unstable rocks.

## Section II – Fundamentals of Glacial Travel

14-8. Glaciers are often good avenues of approach to the interior of a mountain range. Travel can be slow, and dangerous but easier than traveling on steep, loose, or exposed ridgelines. Glaciers are typically large open danger areas and should be treated as such during tactical operations. Units traveling on glaciers are subject to observation from all the surrounding high ground. A capable detachment may be required to provide an overwatch for the force traveling below.

## Equipment

14-9. A variety of specialized mountain equipment may be necessary for Soldiers traveling on glaciers. The following items are not a comprehensive list.

### Ropes

14-10. The use of ropes allows Soldiers to move in small rope teams and have some protection from crevasse falls. The type, length, and diameter of ropes selected for this purpose may vary depending upon how steep and heavily crevassed the glacier is. It is also determined by the number of Soldiers per rope team, and whether there are mutually supporting teams. Smaller diameter, lighter weight ropes are generally preferred.

### Harnesses

14-11. Harnesses enable the individual connection to the rope team.

### Ice Axe and Crampons

14-12. Ice axes and crampons may be necessary for movement over snow or ice on a glacier but also provide the ability for a Soldier on a rope team to self-arrest to control, stop, and hold the fall of another climber. Ice axes may also be used for snow anchor construction for crevasse rescue.

### Skis and Snowshoes

14-13. In deep snow conditions, skis or snowshoes make movement more efficient. They also distribute weight over a greater area when crossing crevasses, which can reduce the likelihood of breaking through a hidden snow bridge. Snowshoes may be more practical than skis for roped glacier travel unless all members of a rope team are skilled skiers.

### Prusiks, Slings, and Cordelettes

14-14. Short prusik loops, slings, and cordelettes can be used for ascending a rope after a crevasse fall. They are used for belaying rope team members and creating anchors, load transfers, and hauling systems in crevasse rescue. Slings can be used to improvise a chest harness, if necessary, and can be useful to rig the rucksack for removal in the event of a fall. Each Soldier should carry a cordelette, a sling, and a short prusik.

### Snow Anchors

14-15. Snow or ice anchors, such as pickets and ice screws, are often carried for use in crevasse rescue. In some snow conditions, ice axes, skis, and other items may be used to create an anchor.

### Wands

14-16. Wands are sticks used as markers and can be utilized in many ways. They can be used to mark the location of known crevasses on frequently used routes or used in pairs forming an X to indicate danger. They can be used while setting up bivouac sites to mark the boundaries of safe areas. They are sometimes useful for whiteout navigation in heavily crevassed terrain. Wands can be made using 4-foot bamboo garden stakes with tape or flagging.

### Other Glacial Gear

14-17. The following items are typically carried and necessary for connecting to rope, connecting to prusiks, securing rucksacks or sleds, and facilitating crevasse rescue.

- 2 to 3 locking carabiners.
- 2 or more nonlocking carabiners.
- Belay or rappel device.
- Rescue pulley (optional).

## Use of the Rope

14-18. A central question when considering travel over glaciers is whether to use a rope for fall protection. The hazard of hidden crevasses on a glacier varies considerably. In some circumstances, it may be preferable to travel unroped on crevassed glaciers; however, this is not typical. Deciding whether to rope together as a team depends upon the season, mode of travel, snow conditions, and the conditions of the glacier itself.

14-19. Traveling unroped is faster. It makes communication and dissemination of information easier; it can be done with untrained Soldiers. It eliminates the transition times required for tying in and preparing for roped travel. It also allows for movement in tactical formations and a better security posture.

14-20. During the summer season, with clear skies and good visibility, crevasse risk on a glacier below the ablation zone is often negligible because crevasses can easily be seen as avoided due to lack of snow cover. In early season, or in low visibility, this same glacier could be quite dangerous. The decision of whether to rope up should not be taken lightly and should be made by only experienced mountaineers. Below is an example of various risk levels and guidance:

- Zero risk—bare glacier ice, free of snow and with few crevasses. There is no concern for hidden crevasses because all crevasses are visible. In periods of limited visibility, rope up. In good visibility, travel unroped. If there is a danger of a sliding fall that could lead to a crevasse fall, use the belayed climbing techniques covered in chapter 10.
- Low risk—hard, dense snow conditions in summer, or early morning frozen conditions after a cold night. Concern for hidden crevasses is low and snow bridges are strong. In limited visibility, rope up. In good visibility, travel unroped with caution and be alert for changing conditions. Have harnesses on and equipment available to make the transition to roped travel easier.
- Moderate risk—soft summer snow conditions. Breaking through a snow bridge or into a hidden crevasse is possible. Rope up.
- High risk—soft or weak snow on a glacier with many crevasses. Crevasses are hidden and snow bridges are weak. Breaking through snow bridges and falling into hidden crevasses is likely. Avoid travel, if possible. If travel is required, rope up and use cautious route finding.

### Determining Fall Hazard Versus Crevasse Hazard

14-21. In glaciated terrain, there can be both a risk of falling on steep ground as well as a risk of falling into crevasses. These risks are not managed in the same way. Crevasse hazard is best managed with good route finding and by keeping 8 to 10 meters of rope between Soldiers. Rope teams can usually stop crevasse falls because these occur on low-angled terrain without falling hazard. On flat terrain or soft snow, the arresting Soldiers are not typically at risk of sliding into the crevasse and are often able to stop the fall by slumping in the snow, rather than a full team self-arrest.

14-22. Falls on steep firm snow or an icy slope have almost no chance of being stopped by a rope team. Steep fall hazard is better managed by belaying Soldiers individually as described in chapter 10 or simul-climbing with protection placed for the team. It can be tempting to travel on steep slopes using crevasse fall protection techniques, but this is an illusion of safety. A fall can pull the entire rope team from the slope, often with fatal consequences.

14-23. Since these risks can change often from crevasse hazard to falling hazard and vice versa, changing strategies is important. The following paragraphs discuss using the rope for crevasse fall protection only.

### Determining Rope Team Size

14-24. When using the rope, team size can be a difficult decision, and several factors must be considered. Smaller rope teams are faster and can move more efficiently than larger teams. Larger teams can easily arrest a fall and have more immediate resources to haul someone out of a crevasse. Training levels and overall unit size are also factors in determining team size. The following list describes advantages and disadvantages of various rope teams:

- Two-person teams—travel is faster and more efficient. However, in the event of crevasse fall, one Soldier must hold the fall. When not traveling with other rope teams nearby, that Soldier must also construct an anchor while holding the fallen Soldier, transfer the weight, and then conduct a rescue. For this reason, only use two-person teams when both team members are highly experienced. Requires approximately 60 meters of rope.
- Three-person teams—generally preferred for most glacier travel. Maintains a relatively low team size, while having two Soldiers to hold a fall and potentially haul the Soldier out. It is possible to have one untrained or minimally trained Soldier on this team when placed in the middle. End climbers must both have good route finding and crevasse detections skills and must be proficient with all crevasse rescue strategies. Requires 45 to 50 meters of rope.
- Four-person teams—travel is slower and less efficient, but the large team is more likely to hold a fall. May be preferred versus multiple two-person teams when there is more than one untrained or inexperienced Soldier. Requires 30 to 40 meters of rope.
- Teams of five or more—travel is slow and cumbersome. Obstacles slow the group even more with each additional Soldier. The entire team must slow down or wait as each member negotiates it. Rope management and communication between members become more difficult. Teams of five or more are not recommended but may be necessary in situations where there are many Soldiers without sufficient experience. Multiple ropes or sections of ropes may be necessary to ensure adequate spacing between Soldiers on rope teams of more than five.
- Mutually supporting teams—mutually supporting teams, such as two teams of three, (rather than one team of six), or a team of two and a team of three, (rather than one team of five), should be used when experience levels make it possible. Multiple teams allow one team to simply hold the fall and supporting teams to use their own rope and resources to conduct a crevasse rescue. This strategy can also decrease the amount of excess rescue rope that individual teams must carry.

### Determining Spacing Between Soldiers

14-25. It is best to have no more rope between Soldiers than necessary. Longer rope distance slows progress and makes it more difficult to communicate and coordinate movements. Smaller teams need slightly more spacing than larger teams. This distance needs to span crevasses so that only one Soldier is at risk at a time when crossing. Some general guidelines are given but spacing may need to be increased for exceptionally large crevasses.

- Two-person teams—10 to 12 meters.
- Three-person teams—8 to 10 meters.
- Four-person teams—7 to 9 meters.

### Two-Person Teams

14-26. In the event of a crevasse fall, each member of a two-person rope team must have enough spare rope available to reach fallen Soldiers and back to haul them out. (See figure 14-2.) This usually involves 60 meters or more of rope to have adequate spacing between Soldiers. With Soldiers spaced approximately 12-meters apart, they would each have 24 meters of spare rope to be used for rescue. A figure eight on a bight can be tied at 6 meters on each side of the rope’s middle mark. These can be used as attachment points for team members and are each clipped into the harness with a locking carabiner and a nonlocking carabiner opposite and opposed. The remaining rope can be coiled and worn over the shoulder or placed into the rucksack. Soldiers must also tie several stopper knots on the rope connecting them. These additional knots aid in arresting a fall by catching in the lip of the crevasse and can often greatly reduce tension on the arresting climber, making it easier to construct an anchor. These are mandatory for two-person teams and can be either alpine butterfly knots or overhands on a bight. Since each knot takes up about 1 meter of rope, this length must be factored in as well.

*[Figure 14-2. Two-person rope team — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Three-Person Teams

14-27. For three-person teams, the rope is broken up into quarters. (See figure 14-3.) Each end Soldier carries enough extra rope to reach the middle Soldier. So, with 10 meters of spacing between people, each end Soldier will be carrying an additional 10 meters or more of rope each. Stopper knots are unnecessary in three-person teams because there is generally sufficient holding power. A figure eight on a bight is tied and attached to the middle person’s harness with a locking carabiner and nonlocking carabiner opposite and opposed. Each end Soldier measures approximately 10 meters out from the middle and attaches the same way with a figure eight on a bight. Remaining rope can be coiled and worn over the shoulder or stuffed in the rucksack.

*[Figure 14-3. Three-person rope team — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Four-Person Teams

14-28. Four-person teams can simply divide the rope into thirds and use the entire length. The end Soldiers tie into the ends of the rope and the middle Soldiers clip in as described for two- and three-person teams. If a longer rope is used and shorter spacing between Soldiers is desired, end climbers can coil and stow rope as described above.

### Other Preparations

14-29. Mountaineers often pre-place one or more prusiks on the rope. (See figure 14-4, page 340.) These can be used as part of a system to ascend a rope for self-rescue in a crevasse fall or for load transfer when rescuing another Soldier. Having at least one is useful for keeping slack out of the rope when grouping in one location to communicate or conduct other tasks. The prusik can be attached to the harness with a separate locking carabiner or the same locking carabiner used for the attachment to the rope.

14-30. A 120-cm sling (48 inches), or two 60-cm slings (24 inches) attached together, can be improvised quickly to construct a chest harness. To do this, pass the loop through both arms and behind the back, forming an X in the back, then connect the two bights in the front with a carabiner. This is only to be used after a crevasse fall to remain upright and make self-rescue easier. Chest harnesses are worn when a crevasse fall is likely.

14-31. Mountaineers can prepare the rucksack with a sling and carabiner so that in the event of a crevasse fall the Soldier can remove the rucksack immediately and clip to the rope to dangle below.

14-32. Snow anchors, wands, or other equipment should be distributed among the team.

*[Figure 14-4. Preparation chest harness, rucksack removal sling — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Rope Management

14-33. The rope should be kept extended on a glacier. It should not have unnecessary slack or be taut. Excess slack will cause a Soldier to fall deeper into a crevasse, placing more force on other members of the team and increasing the difficulty of arresting the fall. The falling climber is more likely to hit something or become stuck or wedged.

14-34. A rope with the correct amount of tension lies on the snow with a gentle curve, out of the way of the feet. It can be helpful to hold the rope to the front when traveling downhill and to the rear when traveling uphill. Pace should be adjusted to maintain correct tension in the rope by feel, especially during stops and starts. The Soldier in the lead should set a pace that others can easily follow.

14-35. During sharp turns, the rope tends to go slack as the lead Soldier makes a change in the direction of travel, but then it becomes taut as the next Soldier approaches the turn. During turns, team members should adjust pace and keep appropriate tension. New tracks may need to be made outside the leader’s footprints to do this.

14-36. As a rope team reaches a rest area or bivouac location, the rope must remain extended until the area has been probed for crevasses. Once it has been deemed safe, the lead member may belay team members into the area by pulling rope through the prusik, with ice axe in hand, ready to self-arrest. Soldiers in the rear may also belay themselves into the location in the same manner. When leaving the location, the same process happens in reverse. (See figure 14-5.)

## Route Finding and Crevasse Detections

14-37. Crevasse falls are extremely dangerous, even when traveling roped up with experienced Soldiers. Even when all Soldiers manage the rope well and the fall is stopped immediately, severe injuries can result. Ice axes, crampons, and collapsing snow bridges can cause injuries. Detection and avoidance are the primary skills needed to eliminate the need for crevasse rescue. On heavily crevassed glaciers, it is advisable to use an advanced party to scout and mark the safest route in advance of the main body. This can save hours of wandering, slow progress, and running into dead ends. Once relatively safe routes are established, it can reduce the skill and experience level required of the main body.

### Information Gathering

14-38. Big picture views of the terrain can be very helpful. A maze of crevasses can be analyzed more easily from above than from within. It is a good idea to take digital photos whenever high ground provides a vantage point. Because glaciers move so slowly, crevasse patterns remain constant from year to year, making even old photographs useful for study. Intelligence products or online mapping resources (see appendix B) that contain satellite imagery can provide overhead views. These may contain imagery taken during the summer or late season and show the general trends and locations of dense crevasses that may be only partially filled in or have suspect snow bridges when traveling during winter and early season.

### Timing

14-39. On snow covered and heavily crevassed glaciers, snow bridges are firmer at night or early in the morning. If stable, they can often provide more direct lines of travel. Warmer daytime temperatures can soften these snow bridges, increasing the likelihood of crevasse fall. This may require finding a longer alternative route.

14-40. Be cautious after snowstorms. Fresh snow can fill in and blend in what might otherwise be detectable crevasses.

14-41. Seasonal timing is equally important. Although unit leaders may not have much say in the time of year they are required to travel on glaciers, a basic understanding of how seasonal changes affect surface conditions is important. Below are descriptions of typical conditions in the midlatitudes:

- Autumn—in late season conditions, most crevasses are clearly visible and snow bridges have melted out to their maximum extent. Travel is generally safest, as there is little risk of unexpected crevasse fall, but route finding can be difficult and require long detours and lengthy movement times around crevasse fields. In steeper terrain, firm conditions can make falling hazard a higher concern.
- Winter—early winter is especially hazardous. Unconsolidated snow hides crevasses but often does not effectively bridge them. Crevasses are more difficult to detect, and unexpected falls are more likely. In late winter, deeper snow conditions may provide more effective snow bridges. Increased snow loads may cause bridges to sag, making detection easier. Skis or snowshoes are often required.
- Spring—snowfall has reached its maximum depth, making glacier travel safest. Snow bridges are at their strongest and the snowpack strengthens from warmer weather. Skis or snowshoes are often required, but lines of travel are typically much more direct.
- Summer—early morning travel on firm snow surfaces makes travel easier, but snow bridges are beginning to weaken. As crevasses open, finding direct routes becomes challenging again. Seracs and icefalls also become more hazardous.

14-42. In some geographical regions further from the poles, these trends can be nearly opposite with surface conditions revolving strictly around dry and rainy seasons.

### Zones of Tension and Compression

14-43. Crevasses are formed by tension. Tension is created as glacier ice stretches over rolls in the terrain and where it stretches due to different rates of flow within the glacier. These crevasses form perpendicular to the direction of the stretch. Recognizing where this tension is likely to occur can help with predicting their likely locations when snow fills in or conceals them. Crevasses are more likely at the top of a roll, where the terrain gets steeper and drops away below it. They are also common near the edges of valley glaciers, where additional drag and friction occurs against the valley walls, and the center of the glacier is moving faster. These “marginal” crevasses point toward the center of the glacier at an angle. For this same reason, the centers of glaciers generally have fewer crevasses and are preferred travel routes.

14-44. Compression zones occur where the underlying bedrock flattens out and where glacial ice slows down or piles up. Here, crevasses become narrow and eventually disappear. Glacial ice can also pile up in bowls and flat benches in the terrain and where glaciers flow together. These areas of compression can be relatively crevasse-free. Observing these zones and working to link them together are keys to good route finding.

### Surface Clues

14-45. Clues to locating crevasses below the surface of the snow can be subtle and difficult to recognize, but it is important for determining an appropriate crossing technique. It can also be just as important to recognize similar clues that do not indicate crevasses and to not waste time and effort on long detours.

- Sagging—snow covering larger crevasses gradually deforms and sags under its own weight. Early in the process, this may be barely noticeable, but later the outlines of the crevasse become more defined. Sometimes a slight change in sheen or texture can be observed. Other times, a slight change in color, from dust or windblown snow settling in a subtle depression, can be visible. These should be approached with caution. Snow is, however, an inherently uneven surface, so not every dip, groove, or color change represents a crevasse. In low visibility or flat light, these can be impossible to recognize.
- Nearby crevasses—since crevasses are often found in groups and tend to form parallel to one another, look for other crevasses, patterns, or depressions nearby that have similar orientation.
- Parallel cracks—in the case of larger crevasses, parallel thin cracks can sometimes form at the edges of a giant snow bridge. Although these can have the appearance of two narrow crevasses, they can be the boundaries of a single, giant snow bridge.

### Probing

14-46. Snow probing in areas that look suspicious can be done using an ice axe. It can identify crevasses, determine its true lip, and investigate the extent, length, and width of a crevasse. To probe, the shaft of the axe is thrust into the snow a couple feet ahead of where one moves, looking for a noticeable reduction in resistance. If there is a sudden drop in resistance, there is likely a crevasse, but it can also be a soft snow layer. Probing requires experience to accurately interpret. The short length of an ice axe makes this difficult. Avalanche probes and ski poles with the basket removed are better choices, if available. Probing is also required for determining safe bivouac locations.

### Crossing Techniques

14-47. There are a variety of options and techniques for crossing crevasses, and leaders must understand and be capable of incorporating any combination of them to adapt to the terrain. Rope teams should travel perpendicular to the direction of crevasses to reduce the risk of exposing more than one Soldier at a time. (See figure 14-6, page 344.)

*[Figure 14-6. Crossing perpendicular to crevasses — illustration not reproduced.]*

### End Running

14-48. There is no guarantee as to the dependability of snow bridges. Sometimes it is preferable to cross at one end of a crevasse where its width narrows, rather than take a chance with a snow bridge. (See figure 14-7.) With large crevasses, this could lead to traveling a quarter of a mile or more to gain 30 feet of forward progress. In winter or spring, seasonal snow may still cover the narrow ends of a crevasse. What looks like the end of the crevasse might not be its actual end. This assessment can require some judgement. Make a wide swing, probe carefully, and cross at a right angle to the crevasse.

*[Figure 14-7. End running crevasses — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Using a Snow Bridge

14-49. When end running is not practical, use snow bridges to cross. (See figure 14-8.) Always attempt to get a look at the crevasse from the side before committing to it, and probe when approaching the lip. The remaining Soldier in the team provides a moving belay by controlling the slack and remaining ready to self-arrest. Once the leader is across, the rest of the team must follow exactly in the leader’s footsteps. At this point, the leader controls slack and remains prepared for self-arrest. Prusiks can be used here to manage slack in the rope during this process as well. If there is doubt as to one’s ability to stop a fall, a fixed belay must be constructed with a snow or ice anchor.

*[Figure 14-8. Using a snow bridge — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Jumping

14-50. Only conduct jumping when the gap is short. Running leaps requiring big push offs from suspect crevasse lips and falls can result in serious injury. Jumping should not be attempted unless there are no other options. Enough slack must be provided for the jumper to clear the crevasse without question. Be wary of catching a crampon and use common sense.

### Climbing into the Crevasse

14-51. Occasionally, it may be practical to climb down into a shallow crevasse and climb back out the other side. Standard belay techniques (see chapter 10) should be used. Be especially aware of bottoms that appear solid but are not.

### Echelon Formation

14-52. When it is impractical to travel perpendicular to existing crevasses and Soldiers must travel parallel, the echelon formation can be used. (See figure 14-9.) This keeps team members still to the rear but offset slightly to the side. This provides an alternative to following the leader’s tracks in a single-file line. This should only be used on heavily crevassed sections where all crevasses are visible and there is very little risk of falling into a hidden one.

## Section III – Crevasse Rescue Techniques

14-53. Solid crevasse rescue skills are essential for elements traveling in glaciated terrain. While this may be impractical in circumstances involving large numbers of personnel, the ability to react appropriately to crevasse fall cannot be overlooked.

## Crevasse Fall Considerations

14-54. Mutually supporting rope teams should have a minimum of one Soldier per team who is trained in crevasse rescue. This will ensure that rescue capability can always be provided, even when the more experienced member of another rope team is the one to fall in. Two trained Soldiers are necessary in rope teams traveling independently, away from supporting teams.

14-55. Crevasse falls are rarely dramatic and sudden falls that rip team members off their feet and result in someone dangling down inside an abyss. In fact, most crevasse falls involve breaking through a snow bridge but falling no further than waste deep, and with minimal force exerted on team members. Despite this, Soldiers must be trained to deal with more severe falls and be able to adapt and adjust to a variety of circumstances. Options for crevasse rescue involve an abundance of variables. They depend largely upon the breakdown of rope teams, the number of Soldiers per rope, conditions of the glacier, and specific equipment carried. A comprehensive and detailed description of crevasse rescue techniques is well beyond the scope of this publication. Instead, the steps and decision process typically used for a three-person rope team are described below.

## Steps to Take for Crevasse Fall

14-56. The following steps should be considered in the event of a crevasse fall with a three-person rope team.

### Stop the Fall

14-57. Stop the fall immediately. Some falls may be barely noticeable, while others may require dropping into the self-arrest position in the opposite direction. Stopping falls on firm surfaces can be more difficult than soft ones. Once the fall is arrested, both rescuers should kick crampon points into the snow to establish a strong, stable position to prevent the victim from slipping any further. Communication can often be made with the victim at this point. In circumstances where Soldiers have only fallen a few feet or are stuck in the snow bridge, they may be able to climb out unassisted. If a Soldier is not able to quickly self-recover, then the following steps must be taken. (See figure 14-10, page 348.)

*[Figure 14-10. Stopping a crevasse fall — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Construct an Anchor and Transfer the Load

14-58. When another rope team is available, then they can construct an anchor and help the team transfer the load of the fallen Soldier onto it. (See figure 14-11.) Otherwise, the Soldier on the opposite end is usually the one to complete this step. End Soldiers slowly get out of the self-arrest, making sure that middle Soldiers can still hold the weight. End Soldiers establish an anchor (see chapter 9 for snow and ice anchors) below the middle climber and towards the crevasse. They attach a prusik to the loaded rope and clip it to the anchor with a locking carabiner. If there is limited distance between the middle climber and the crevasse lip, the anchor can be constructed above the middle climber. In this case, the slack rope itself, between the middle and end climber can be used to attach directly to the anchor.

14-59. Next, the end Soldiers get back into the self-arrest position to back up middle Soldiers as they transfer the weight of fallen Soldiers onto this prusik. Once pressure is relieved from middle Soldiers, it is common for them to continue backing up this anchor while the end climber improves it or constructs additional components. This anchor must be unquestionably strong, as it may be used to haul the Soldier out. The prusik should also be backed up with a suitable knot clipped to the anchor.

*[Figure 14-11. Construct the anchor and transfer the load — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Communicate with the Fallen Climber and Make a Plan

14-60. To make a clear assessment and develop a plan, it is best to approach the edge of the crevasse where the fallen Soldier can be seen and heard. This can be done by one of the team members using a safety prusik attached to the unweighted portion of the rope. Exercise caution approaching the edge. Determination of what to do next will depend on how far down the climbers are, whether they sustained injuries, extent of any injuries, and characteristics of the terrain in and around the crevasse. The loaded rope can often become entrenched into the lip of the crevasse, complicating rescue options. This may be a good time to pad underneath the rope to prevent this from becoming worse with an ice axe shaft, foam pad, or rucksack slid underneath the rope as close to the edge as can be reached. This can take some work, and items used for padding should be anchored so that it cannot fall into the crevasse. Various options for rescue are described below.

### Self-Rescue

14-61. Self-rescue can be achieved by the fallen climber ascending the rope itself using a prusik ascent (see paragraphs 11-39 through 11-49, pages 282 through 284) or other techniques discussed in chapter 11 and is also shown in figure 14-10, page 348. When possible, this is often the easiest and fastest option. The prusik ascent prevents the fallen Soldier from becoming cold waiting for rescuers. Getting the weight of the rucksack off the body and onto the rope is also particularly helpful for staying upright. Assistance from team members may be required to surmount the crevasse lip. The method does not work if the fallen Soldier sustained significant injuries.

### Belay

14-62. Belays may be a fast, easy option when crampons and ice axes can be used to climb out of the hole, especially if or when the rope is too entrenched in the crevasse lip to prusik out. The remaining free end of the rope can be passed down to the fallen climber to tie into the harness with, while a team member above creates a belay at the anchor. Again, if the fallen Soldier is injured, this method may not be possible.

### Haul

14-63. If none of the above options are viable, a more complex solution of hauling the fallen climber out will be needed.

### Hauling

14-64. Hauling techniques used for MA described in chapter 12 may be used for hauling; however, there are circumstances specific to working with crevasses that must be considered.

### Direct Haul

14-65. This refers to hauling systems constructed on the fallen climber’s weighted strand of rope. A common example is the 3:1 hauling system. (See figure 12-4, page 307.) This is constructed by untying the backup knot behind the prusik and using this remaining rope to construct the system. A disadvantage to the direct haul is that it does not work if the rope is entrenched in the crevasse lip. This method is also impractical if there are knots tied in the weighted rope to help hold the crevasse fall. (See figure 14-12.)

*[Figure 14-12. Direct haul using a 3:1 hauling system — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Drop Loop

14-66. Drop loops refer to hauling systems that use a bight of rope sent down and attach to the fallen climber’s harness, independent of the climber’s weighted strand. (See figure 14-13.) An example is the 2:1 hauling system. (See figure 12-3, page 307.) This is often the preferred method because the crevasse lip can be padded prior to hauling and because the victim can often assist by pulling on the strand opposite the haul strand. Drop-loop hauls do require victims to clip the bight of rope into the harness, so in the case of an unconscious victim, this step may need to be done by first rappelling to them then re-ascending. Drop-loop systems can be performed using the remainder of the rope or by a separate rope team utilizing a different rope altogether. Progress must still be captured during the haul for safety. This may be done by pulling rope through the prusik on the original tensioned rope.

## Section IV – Training

14-67. Even when mutually supporting rope teams are used, good route finding, and crevasse detection skills are critical. Every rope team must have at least one Soldier with solid crevasse rescue skills. Commanders cannot just hope for seasonally low-risk conditions, nor can they rely solely on advanced parties or recon teams to consistently find routes that are passable without the need for ropes. A lack of expertise can leave units with a large capability gap.

14-68. Training and experience in glacier travel are most productive when conducted on glaciers. Most glaciated terrain in the lower 48 states is in the Pacific Northwest. The high cost of travel and time commitments to access them make training plans difficult for units to execute. Most of those glaciers involve acquiring climbing permits. It can take the better part of a training day for the approach on foot to these higher elevations; any training would typically involve a bivouac. This makes it inconvenient even for units located at Joint Base Lewis-McChord, Washington. Ambitious commanders, however, might utilize these or other glaciated terrain in Alaska, Europe, or elsewhere, to create training events that combine glacier experience with other aspects of mountain warfare.

14-69. In the absence of glaciated terrain, units can still conduct simulated training exercises focusing on the basic strategies for roping up and preparing individual and team equipment. Crevasse rescue skills can be rehearsed using short sections of steep terrain. This should be conducted in locations without serious falling hazard, and back-up ropes or belays should be considered while techniques are being learned. Having a few unit members with advanced level training will improve the quality and outcomes of this training.
