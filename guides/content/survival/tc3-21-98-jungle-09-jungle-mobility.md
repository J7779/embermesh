# Jungle Mobility: Terrain Classes, Equipment and Rope Installations

*From TC 3-21.98, Jungle Handbook (US Army, 18 August 2026), Chapter 9. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Navigating and maneuvering through dense jungle terrain presents unique challenges that demand specialized planning and techniques. This chapter details the considerations for successful jungle mobility, encompassing both individual and unit movement. This chapter explores essential equipment, dismounted movement techniques, rope system installation, and various insertion/extraction methods. Mastery of these skills is critical for maintaining operational tempo and achieving objectives within a complex environment.

## Section I – Planning

9-1. All classes of terrain are common in the jungle, but the prevalence of more intense Classes III to V terrain can vary based on the geographical region in which the CCTE is located. Steep Classes III to V terrain can be a result of water erosion over time or a result of the formation of the land mass such as the case in some Pacific Island chains. Paragraphs 9-2 to 9-6 are the classifications of terrain to be considered when planning a route.

## Classes of Terrain

9-2. Class 1 terrain is identified as gentle slopes or easy trails. This terrain is negotiated with simple walking techniques. Class 1 terrain is considered easy terrain.

9-3. Class 2 terrain involves off-trail scrambling in steeper, more rugged terrain. Use of hands may be needed to negotiate Class 2 terrain, but generally the likelihood and consequence of a fall is low. Good physical conditioning is required, but no technical solutions are needed for safe movement. Moving a casualty will generally require technical systems. Class 2 terrain is considered easy terrain.

9-4. Class 3 terrain usually necessitates moderate scrambling, with the use of hands needed for balance. Movement may be easy, but death or serious injury may be the consequence of a fall. Experienced mountaineers will usually travel un-roped. Use of fixed ropes or belay techniques may be required for beginners to cross sections with greater exposure or when heavy rain and muddy surfaces are present in the jungle. Class 3 terrain is considered moderate terrain.

9-5. Class 4 terrain is steeper terrain that involves belayed climbing or fixed ropes. There is moderate to difficult scrambling which may have some exposure. Serious injury or death can occur if an un-roped fall occurs. Basic mountaineers are needed to negotiate this terrain, with assault climbers finding the route, fixing the rope, and providing belays where necessary. It requires significant time and resources to manage risk for large elements and is more efficiently traveled by small teams. Class 4 terrain is the most difficult terrain to evaluate and manage risk. Class 4 terrain can be considered moderate to hard depending on the exposure and consequence of a fall. Common examples of Class 4 terrain in the CCTE are steep draws or spurs, likely covered by ferns or vegetation which mask the ground shape, and steep terrain bordering waterways.

9-6. Class 5 terrain is vertical terrain always involving hands and feet while moving. This terrain is complex, dangerous ground where significant injury or death can occur from an un-roped fall. Class 5 terrain requires Soldiers to be roped up, use belay techniques, and place intermediate protection. Assault climbers are required to negotiate this terrain. Assault climbers may be able to travel 1:1 with basic mountaineers, if time is given for rehearsals prior to an operation, but generally only assault climbers will be climbing in Class 5 terrain. Class 5 terrain is considered hard terrain when planning (see figure 9-1). Examples of Class 5 terrain in a CCTE are waterfalls, terrain off ridgelines, canyons, and cliffs.

## Planning Considerations

9-7. The use of rope based dismounted mobility should be trained and rehearsed prior to implementation on an operation. A variety of methods exist that vary on difficulty and time required. The following should be considered when planning a route and deciding on techniques:

- Likelihood of enemy contact and time required.
- Risk from fall and risk from compromise.
- Equipment available.
- Level of training and fitness of Soldiers.

9-8. Other considerations/TTPs for the CCTE include the following:

- Ferns and vegetation regularly mask the ground underneath, hiding steep drops.
- Dense vegetation in vicinity of vertical or near vertical terrain may require different rope systems than normally used in a high-altitude environment.
- Simple mobility aids may be effective for navigating moderate terrain.
- Muddy/slippery ground can effectively upgrade the classification of terrain.
- Consider what equipment is left behind if dealing with enemy tracking or counter tracking teams.

9-9. It is important to understand what constitutes a good anchor point for use in mobility operations. This rule is “5, 5, Alive,” all anchor points should be at least 5-feet tall, 5 feet in diameter, and a currently living tree.

## Section II – Equipment

9-10. Specific mobility equipment carried on a mission is dependent on METT-TC (I), however, the following is a recommendation for a basic kit that should be carried by all members of the squad (see figure 9-2):

- 1 x 30 feet, 1-inch tubular nylon webbing.
- 2 x locking carabiner.
- 1 x 5 feet, 7-mm accessory cord.
- 1 x air traffic control or belay device.

9-18. Paragraph 9-20 is an assessment that can be used to assess a squad on their ability to implement rope systems and move through the restrictive jungle terrain. This is a team drill with a 30-minute time limit.

9-19. Equipment: Squad should have the minimal equipment needed to accomplish the task, or any equipment that is planned to be used operationally.

9-20. Standards: All mobility operations conducted as a part of this drill are done safely and with the correct checks from the mobility team leader assigned. The team is allowed to leave behind any expendable equipment that would be left on operation (tubular nylon, rappel ring, quick link).

9-21. Sequence:

- 1. Team builds anchor on designated anchor point on top of slope or wall.
- 2. One member rappels down to a casualty on a Munter to a simulated casualty.
- 3. The team member mules off and converts a system to a 2:1 haul system and is hauled back to the top.
- 4. The medic will hasty rappel down to the simulated casualty and attaches properly to the litter while the remainder of the team at the top converts the anchor to a 3:1 line haul system.
- 5. The team hauls the patient and litters to the top.
- 6. Every member of the team that has not been to the bottom hasty rappels down.
- 7. All team members ascend the rope back to the top.
- 8. The team builds a lowering system once at the top and lowers the patient and medic to the bottom.
- 9. The remaining team members rappel down the rope using the method of their choosing. The anchor should be converted to a recoverable system if not already done so.
- 10. The last Soldier to rappel down recovers the rope.
- 11. Time ends when rope is recovered.

## Section V – Insertion/extraction Methods

9-22. Due to the challenges of movement through the jungle formations can utilize special insertion and extraction methods through the air.

## Air Assault

9-23. Deploy Soldiers via rappelling with equipment at heights up to 120 feet into AOs that have no viable LZ.

- Advantages:
  - Height at which can be executed (up to 120 feet).
  - Deployment of Soldiers without landing can penetrate minor canopy.
  - Safer method of insertion due to being harnessed in.
  - Soldiers can rappel with ruck/equipment.
- Disadvantages: slower rate of Soldiers deploying to ground, unable to jettison ropes, either pull in or cut.

## Fast Rope Insertion

9-24. The Fast Rope Insertion (FRIES) is used when a fast exit into a small or restricted area, to include overwater operations, is required. It provides a method for inserting and extracting personnel in critical areas when the aircraft cannot land.

- Advantages:
  - Fast deployment of Soldiers.
  - Can be deployed into water.
  - Can double as extraction method as well.
  - Easy recovery of ropes.
- Disadvantages:
  - Higher risk due to not being harnessed on rope.
  - Additional descending tools required for equipment over 60 pounds.
  - FRIES operations will not be conducted in densely wooded areas unless there are clear area(s), large enough to support the rope operation with 60 feet maximum deployment.

## Special Purpose Insertion

9-25. Special Purpose Insertion (SPIES) was developed to rapidly insert or extract personnel from an area where landing is not possible. SPIES procedures can be used for rough terrain as well as water extraction (wet-SPIES) operations.

- Advantages:
  - Safest extraction method when landing is not possible.
  - 120 feet in length.
  - Can penetrate minor canopy.
  - Can carry 14 personnel and up to 5,000 pounds of equipment.
  - Can be used in waterborne operations.
- Disadvantages: maximum of 20-minute flight time (waivable).
