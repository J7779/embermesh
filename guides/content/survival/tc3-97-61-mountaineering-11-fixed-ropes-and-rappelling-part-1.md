# Fixed Ropes, Rappelling and Ascending (Part 1 of 2)

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Chapter 11. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

Fixed ropes are an important part of military mountaineering as they facilitate the movement of Soldiers through rugged terrain. Fixed ropes allow large groups of Soldiers to safely access terrain that provides tactical advantage over the enemy. They have proven useful in past conflicts. During World War II, they were successfully used during Operation Neptune (commonly referred to as D-day) and the famous assault on Riva Ridge by the 10th Mountain Division. Proficiency in using fixed ropes allows units to overcome mobility limitations and, therefore, achieve greater lethality in mountainous terrain.

## Section I – Basic Fixed-Rope Techniques

11-1. A fixed rope is any rope system attached to at least one anchor that can be used to improve security and efficiency of movement through difficult terrain that subjects Soldiers to a fall hazard. They can be used for ascending, descending, or traversing across steep terrain in which a fall hazard exists. They may simply provide Soldiers with a handhold through a short section of steep terrain or as complex as hundreds of feet of broken hazardous terrain involving numerous sections that prevent attached Soldiers from being able to fall. They can be used to assist the movement of small teams to large units. They allow units to overcome mobility limitations and access tactically advantageous terrain.

## Fixed-Rope Considerations

11-2. Fixed ropes should be used whenever a group of Soldiers cannot move safely through a section of terrain unroped. Fixed ropes are usually the most efficient means of protecting groups larger than team sized, especially if the groups have not received advanced military mountaineering training. Common factors contributing to the necessity of building fixed ropes are fall hazard, slippery surfaces, limited visibility, heavy loads, low mobility skills, and fatigue.

11-3. The two basic types of fixed-rope systems are simple fixed ropes and fixed ropes with intermediate anchors. Both types have applications in every class of terrain. Simple fixed ropes are anchored only at the top, can only be used by one Soldier at a time, and require a straight path of movement from bottom to top. Fixed ropes with intermediate anchors have multiple anchors and rope sections, allowing more Soldiers to move on them simultaneously and the path to change directions. Each type is determined by route and availability of anchors along it, the number of Soldiers to use the system, and the type of available equipment to attach to it.

11-4. All types of fixed ropes are built from the top down over, or through, terrain obstacles. However, fixed rope installation teams may need to ascend terrain using other techniques prior to building a fixed-rope system. (See chapter 10.)

11-5. Units planning to use fixed ropes must establish installation teams capable of locating routes, moving safely through various terrain obstacles, and building a variety of installations. They should be led by advanced military mountaineers but may be assisted by basic military mountaineers. These teams should be experienced in building fixed ropes in the type of terrain to be negotiated, equipped appropriately for the task, and familiar with the capabilities of the Soldiers who use them.

11-6. In a tactical environment, any terrain that requires a fixed rope should be considered a VDA, which is steep terrain that puts a unit moving through it at significant tactical disadvantage. The process for establishing even the simplest fixed rope comes at the cost of time and exposure to enemy attack. Adequate security must be established prior to deploying the installation team. In complex terrain, security teams may need to be adjusted or leap-frogged forward if fixed-rope systems cover large pieces of terrain. Emplacing security teams in a complex terrain may require high-mobility skills as well as substantial time. Typically, it is best to approach fixed-rope terrain from above because security is more easily established on higher ground. But that is rarely possible. Units that anticipate building fixed-rope systems for movement in a combat environment should be well-rehearsed in crossing VDA TTP. (See ATP 3-21.50.)

## Simple Fixed Ropes

11-7. A simple fixed rope is a rope that is anchored at the top of steep terrain that can be used to increase safety or efficiency of movement up or down. The method of anchoring the rope is determined by anchor features available but can be as simple as a bowline tied to a tree or as complex as a multipoint anchor established with rock-climbing gear. The principal benefit of simple, fixed ropes is their simplicity and speed of construction.

11-8. Simple fixed ropes are typically established by one mountaineer climbing the obstacle, dragging a rope, and then anchoring it at the top. They can also be established by approaching the top of a steep obstacle by some other means, anchoring a rope, and lowering the other end down for others to use.

11-9. Some important considerations for using simple fixed-rope systems are that they must follow a path straight up the terrain feature, the attachment technique is appropriate to reduce fall risk, and they allow for a large enough loading and unloading platform to manage the site safely. Using a friction hitch or mechanical progress capture to attach to the rope provides increased security during movement.

### Easy Terrain

11-10. Short 2nd- or 3rd-class slopes with low fall consequence can be negotiated using simple fixed ropes without the necessity of attaching to the rope. Soldiers using the fixed rope simply grasp the rope with the thumb downward and use it in a hand-over-hand fashion to assist with balance. (See figure 11-1.) This technique is appropriate when there is an increased likelihood of fall but low consequence. Usually this means short slopes that Soldiers could easily negotiate normally, but slippery conditions, fatigue, or heavy loads increase the chance of a fall. Knots can be added to increase the security of Soldiers moving on the rope, making it less likely their hand slips on the rope.

*[Figure 11-1. Simple fixed rope use in easy terrain — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Moderate Terrain

11-11. Short sections of steep 3rd or 4th class with higher fall consequence can be negotiated by attaching to simple fixed ropes with a progress capture, such as a prusik or other mechanical rope-grabbing device. This technique requires Soldiers to wear some type of harness. A significant drawback to this technique is that it takes a lot of time to move a group of Soldiers. Only one Soldier can move at a time on a rope, and time is lost during attachment and detachment. (See figure 11-2, page 278.)

### Prusik Technique

11-12. A common technique involves tying a prusik on the rope with a cordelette, then tying a fixed loop in the other end of the two tails. Clip the fixed loop to the harness with a locking carabiner. The optimum length of the attachment is one outstretched arm.

11-13. Ascend the fixed rope by using the legs to climb the slope and the rope as a handhold for balance, sliding the prusik up to capture progress and prevent fall in case of a slip.

11-14. The prusik technique is recommended only on simple fixed ropes without intermediate anchor points because friction hitches cannot be easily or safely transitioned past intermediate anchors.

### Mechanical Ascender Technique

11-15. It is also possible to use a rope-grabbing device, such as a mechanical ascender, to create a progress capture for Soldiers moving on fixed ropes. These devices are easier and faster to use but require every Soldier to carry a piece of equipment that has limited uses. They should be used when fixed rope ascents are planned for large groups in moderate terrain.

11-16. Mechanical ascenders are devices shaped like a handle that can be attached to a rope by means of a toothed cam. It can slide in one direction on a rope but not the other. When weighted, the camming mechanism activates and grips the rope. Soldiers can attach ascenders to their harness and slide them along fixed ropes during movement, capturing their progress and preventing a fall during a slip (see figure 11-2, page 278). Ascenders should never be dynamically loaded as their teeth could damage the rope. Ascenders are included in the Army ACTK, but Solders can use other similar ascenders to ascend fixed ropes. Some are much lighter but are less easy to use. Advanced military mountaineers should familiarize themselves with the options available and units should obtain those best suited to the mountaineering tasks they must perform.

11-17. To use a mechanical ascender, attach it to the fixed rope. Then girth hitch a sling to the harness and clip it to the ascender with a locking carabiner. (See figure 11-2, page 278.)

11-18. Ascend the fixed rope by using the legs to climb the slope and the ascender as a handhold for balance, sliding it up to capture progress and prevent fall in the case of a slip.

*[Figure 11-2. Simple fixed-rope attachments in moderate terrain — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Vertical Terrain

11-19. Moving on simple fixed ropes in terrain where significant sections are very steep or vertical requires rigging that is more complex. These rigging techniques provide secure attachment to the rope and aid movement up the rope. (See paragraphs 11-36 through 11-52, pages 282 through 286, for more information on ascending techniques.)

## Fixed Ropes with Intermediate Anchors

11-20. Larger or more complex sections of steep terrain require fixed ropes with intermediate anchors to ensure adequate safety and efficiency of movement. This may involve various sections of 3rd, 4th, or even 5th-class terrain. The principal difference between simple fixed ropes and fixed ropes with intermediate anchors is the use of intermediate anchor points to create independent sections and changes in direction of travel. This allows the movement route to follow the easiest, safest, and most efficient path. This type of fixed rope is commonly used along exposed ridges and narrow mountain passes.

11-21. Advanced or master military mountaineers with substantial experience are required to establish fixed ropes with intermediate anchors, but they can be assisted by basic military mountaineers.

### Fixed-Rope Construction with Intermediate Anchors

11-22. The primary guiding principles for building a fixed rope with intermediate anchors are that they provide adequate fall prevention while maximizing the speed at which Soldiers can move using them.

### Path

11-23. Establishing a fixed rope with intermediate anchors starts with thorough terrain analysis and route finding. When selecting a path for the rope, mountaineers must consider the anchor options available, difficulty of movement on the route, and any other objective hazards present. The characteristics of the Soldiers who use the fixed rope are also useful information. Particularly, what their mobility and technical skill levels are and what equipment they have for attaching to the rope.

### Intermediate Anchors

11-24. Terrain and vegetation determine the equipment mountaineers need to build the fixed rope. Heavily vegetated terrain or broken rocky terrain may only require cordelettes, slings, and carabiners. Slabby or steep rock faces require cams, nuts, and other lead climbing protection to build anchors. Intermediate anchors must be built robust enough to support the weight of a fall by the number of Soldiers who will move on each section at a given time. When possible, anchors should be built at about waist height in places where good footing is possible so that transitioning past anchors is easier for Soldiers moving on the fixed rope (See figure 11-3 for common methods of attaching ropes to single component anchors.)

### Rope Section Slack

11-25. Fixed-rope sections do not have a standard amount of slack. There should be enough slack to allow Soldiers to follow the easiest path through terrain without adding fall risk. Sometimes a lot of slack is necessary to go around an obstacle or steep section. This is usually better than running a tighter rope over these things, because going around is easier, which reduces the chance of a fall. Other times, a section may need to be tight to reduce fall distance, such as traversing across a cliff on a narrow ledge.

*[Figure 11-3. Intermediate anchors in moderate terrain, example — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Attachment and Movement

11-26. The method for movement on fixed ropes with intermediate anchors is as important as how they are constructed. Two primary methods exist for use in steep terrain: the two personal safeties method and the ascender-carabiner method. Choosing between the two is a tradeoff between not carrying heavier single-purpose items and having more security during movement. The two personal safeties method requires no special equipment but cannot eliminate fall risk for a Soldier. The ascender-carabiner method prevents falling but requires each Soldier to carry a piece of equipment that has little other use. Both techniques have their place, and both must be considered for use when planning fixed-rope movements.

### Two Personal Safeties Method

11-27. This method involves having two separate girth hitched slings attached to the tie-in portion of the harness with a single nonlocking carabiner in each. The carabiners are then attached to the fixed rope. Always keep the two carabiners attached to the rope unless they move past an intermediate anchor. To move past an intermediate anchor, clip one carabiner at a time above the anchor. When both carabiners are above the anchor, continue movement. (See figure 11-4.)

11-28. The two personal safeties method is appropriate for situations where the fall consequence is high, but the likelihood is low. It can be used up to 4th-class terrain under good conditions (such as good surface conditions, moderate mobility skills, light loads, well-rested, high visibility) provided that intermediate anchors are appropriately spaced to prevent serious fall injury. Since falls using this method are only stopped by the last anchor a Soldier passed, there is always a chance of injury. This should be considered carefully before choosing it.

*[Figure 11-4. Two personal safeties attachment — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Ascender-Carabiner Method

11-29. The ascender-carabiner method is the fastest and most secure way to travel up very steep fixed ropes. It is preferred when fall risk is substantial or sections of the fixed rope are long, because the ascender captures the Soldiers’ progress as they move.

11-30. To use this technique, girth hitch two slings into the harness. Clip one nonlocking carabiner into one extension. Clip a mechanical ascender to the other extension with a locking carabiner.

11-31. Ascend in the same manner as with simple fixed ropes. To move past the intermediate anchor, clip the nonlocking carabiner above the anchor, and then move the ascender to above the carabiner on the fixed rope. (See figure 11-5.)

11-32. This method is appropriate when both fall consequence and likelihood are high. It can be used in terrain up to 4th class under any conditions. It is the best method when surface conditions are slippery, loads are heavy, mobility skills are low or Soldiers are fatigued, during low visibility, and when intermediate anchors cannot be established to prevent fall injury.

*[Figure 11-5. Ascender-carabiner method — illustration not reproduced.]*

## Fixed Ropes in Vertical Terrain

11-33. Fixed ropes are sometimes necessary for moving in vertical terrain during complex rescue operations or other special operations in permissive or nontactical environments. They are rarely practical for tactical operations, given their extreme exposure to enemy observation and direct fire. However, vertical terrain is not always avoidable during combat operations, and there are historical examples of their use during pivotal cliff assaults. Vertical terrain is the most extreme example of VDA. Soldiers moving on vertically fixed ropes require well-established overwatch security since they are totally exposed and can do little but ascend the fixed rope.

11-34. Moving on fixed ropes in vertical terrain requires Soldiers to possess the equipment and skills necessary to ascend rope, relying entirely on their attachment to it to prevent fall. Efficient ascending requires specialized equipment.

### Constructing Fixed Ropes in Vertical Terrain

11-35. Fixed ropes in vertical terrain are constructed in the same fashion as moderate terrain; however, they take greater technical rope skills, experience with complex anchoring, and time to establish. With fixed ropes in moderate terrain, it would be ideal for the installation team to approach the route from the top and build the system top-down. However, this is rarely possible. Typically, they must be established from the bottom up by advanced military mountaineers employing lead climbing techniques to get the rope to the top. This process is time-consuming and inherently risky.

### Ascending Techniques

11-36. Many situations may require a Soldier to ascend a rope in very steep or vertical terrain. Soldiers must be prepared and equipped to do so when operating in 4th- and 5th-class terrain. Typically, ascending rope is a more efficient technique than climbing for moving followers over hard climbing terrain, to include those members of small assault climber teams.

11-37. The techniques described below are for single strands of rope, so it is imperative that appropriate measures are taken to pad the rope near sharp edges. Whenever Soldiers plan to ascend vertical terrain, they should use mechanical ascenders to achieve maximum safety and efficiency during movement.

11-38. Ascending techniques are also key skills used in rescue operations, self-rescue during crevasse fall, and whenever rappellers find their rope does not reach the ground.

### Prusik Ascent

11-39. Two prusiks can be used to ascend rope. One prusik is tied to the fixed rope using a cordelette at approximately forehead height. A fixed loop is tied in the hanging tails to create an attachment point at about waist level. This is then secured to the harness with a locking carabiner. Another prusik is tied to the fixed rope at about waist level. The hanging tails are then tied to one or both feet using any appropriate knot to form a foot stirrup. (See figure 11-6.)

11-40. The upper prusik serves as the “progress capture,” during ascent, by holding the Soldier up, while the lower friction hitch serves as the “progress” or makeshift foothold. During movement, the lower hitch is slid up as high as possible while hanging from the upper hitch. Soldiers then stand up on the lower hitch, pushing down and behind them with their leg, while sliding the upper hitch as high as possible. They then rest in the harness from the upper hitch and continue. Safety knots should be tied in the fixed rope below the lower hitch and attached to the harness with a locking carabiner every 10 to 15 feet. This provides a backup against failure of the friction hitch to grab. This technique should be considered an improvised technique for use when ascending is not expected.

*[Figure 11-6. Prusik ascent — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Prusik Ascender Method

11-41. Mechanical ascenders are a one-directional device used to capture progress along a rope and assist climbers as they ascend steep, vertical, or free-hanging terrain. They can be substituted for one or both hitches described in prusik ascent. They make ascending efficient and fast.

11-42. To use an ascender as a progress capture, attach it to the rope at about head height. Girth hitch a sling or cordelette to the harness, and then use a locking carabiner to secure the other end to the bottom of the ascender, forming an arm-length extension between them. Commonly, a nonlocking carabiner is clipped around the rope through the top of the ascender for added security. This prevents the device from twisting off the rope.

11-43. To create the progress with an ascender, attach it to the rope at about waist height. Girth hitch or use any carabiner to attach a sling or cordelette to the bottom of the carabiner and tie it to one or both feet using any appropriate knot. The movement process is the same as described above for prusik ascent.

11-44. There are dozens of devices available that work the same as an ascender. Ensure the rope type and diameter are appropriate for the device used, as most mechanical devices have a specified range of acceptable use. Read all manufacturers’ instructions to ensure safe use.

11-45. Stopper knots should be tied in the fixed rope below the lower hitch or device and attached to the harness with a locking carabiner every 10 to 15 feet. This provides a backup against the rope grabs slipping. This technique should be used when ascending is planned but not enough ascenders are available for Soldiers to be issued two.

### Double-Ascender Method

11-46. This technique is extremely efficient once it has been practiced. It is a recommended technique for ascending long distances. An etrier or cordelette forming a foot stirrup is hung off each ascender with a locking carabiner. Both ascenders are then placed on the rope. A cordelette or sling also hangs off each ascender and is girth hitched to the user’s harness, creating two attachment points to the rope. (See figure 11-7.)

11-47. To use the system, the Soldier places a foot in each etrier (or stirrup) and slides the upper ascender up the rope. This upper ascender is easier to move because the Soldier’s weight is on the lower ascender. The lower ascender is moved up next, while the upper ascender supports the Soldier’s weight. Sometimes it is necessary to slightly release the camming mechanism before moving an ascender.

11-48. It is also recommended to tie back-up knots periodically in the same manner as prusik ascent.

11-49. This technique is the most efficient and is recommended for use whenever ascending is a planned movement technique.

*[Figure 11-7. Double-ascender ascent — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Special Techniques and Considerations

11-50. There are too many considerations for using and improving fixed ropes to cover them all in this training circular. However, the following techniques should be considered for use with fixed ropes.

### Weapon Management

11-51. Unless enemy attack is imminent, weapons should be slung to the rear, allowing Soldiers to scramble more easily in difficult terrain. Heavier weapons, such as sniper weapon systems, mortars, and machine guns, may need to be attached to the top of rucksacks.

### Improving Steps

11-52. Soldiers are less likely to fall and move more quickly if they have good footing. Hard snow is especially easy to carve steps into and can allow Soldiers to move with less exertion on their calf muscles. Carving steps should be done first in steeper sections and, as time allows, in other areas too. Platforms can also be made where Soldiers are transitioning past intermediate anchors. If a Soldier can easily stand near an intermediate anchor, they can clip around it much faster. This greatly lessens the time required for the group to navigate terrain where objective hazards may be present.

### Etriers or Slings at Difficult Steps

11-53. The hanging of a sling or etrier (for use as a hand or foothold) where there is a difficult step can help reduce fall potential and increase speed of the group. An etrier is a short ladder that can be made from any piece of rope or cord and creates an artificial step in a place where there is no natural feature to step on. The etrier can be hung off an intermediate anchor or its own anchor. (See figure 11-8.)

### Caving Ladders

11-54. Manufactured ladders can be useful for large steps and crevasse negotiation. They also enable Soldiers to navigate challenging terrain in the dark, much easier than if they had to search for hand and foot placements. Some are constructed of materials that can be rolled up for transport. While they may make the ascent of a blank wall easier, they are cumbersome to carry.

### Cables and Via-Ferrata

11-55. If terrain is to be repetitively used for access to higher points for something semi-permanent such as an observation post, it may be in the unit’s best interest to build a via-ferrata style system for access. A via-ferrata works similarly to a fixed rope with intermediate anchors, except it uses cable instead of rope. Using cable reduces the need for constant inspection and greatly increases durability. They are much more time consuming to build, but it may be the most feasible option if a unit uses a piece of terrain for an extended period. Given the cable’s rigidity, the attachment method used by Soldiers needs to provide the stretch to lessen fall forces. This equipment can be found on the civilian market. Another component of a via-ferrata-style system is the addition of metal ladder style and foot and handholds. These are for areas and routes that will see traffic over a long time, making the use of soft materials impractical.

## Section II – Types of Rappelling

11-56. Rappelling is a method of descending steep terrain using a rope and a means of creating friction to control the rate of descent. This provides Soldiers with a safe way to descend terrain that would otherwise expose them to very high fall risks.

11-57. If the descent distance is greater than a rope length (for example, multipitch), multiple, simple fixed lines can be linked together with anchors at the top and bottom of each rope. Soldiers can then rappel from anchor to anchor and transition from rope to rope until they reach the ground. This method requires many resources and numerous personnel to establish and manage safely.

11-58. Small teams with fewer resources must use the one or two ropes they can carry to rappel the whole group from one anchor to a lower one, then retrieve the rope, and repeat the process until the ground is reached.

## Rappelling Fundamentals

11-59. Rappelling is a quick method of descent, but it can be extremely dangerous. The dangers include anchor failure, equipment failure, and individual error. Carefully select anchors in a mountainous environment. In a mountain environment, take great care to load the anchor slowly and to ensure that no excessive stress is placed on the anchor. To ensure this, prohibit bounding while rappelling; Soldiers should slowly descend. Soldiers should avoid dragging their feet when moving backwards towards the edge as this causes rockfall.

11-60. Soldiers rappelling with weapons should sling them on the back or to the side when wearing packs. Soldiers carrying particularly heavy weapons, such as machine guns, sniper rifles, or mortars may need to strap them to the top of their pack and use additional techniques described below to make rappelling less difficult.

### Rappel Site Selection

11-61. Selecting the rappel point depends on factors, such as mission, cover, route, anchor availability, and edge composition (for example, avoiding those with loose or jagged rocks). The anchor selected should leave no doubt that it will work for its intended purpose.

11-62. Rappel ropes anchored at waist level or above make transitioning over the edge much easier and reduce the likelihood of Soldiers stumbling or falling. Stumbles and falls are likely to increase the risk of rope damage on sharp rock as well as increase force exerted on anchors.

11-63. Rappel sites with suitable loading and off-loading platforms should be selected. Ideally, they are flat and allow for anchoring far enough away from the edge that Soldiers do not need to be attached to an anchor to prevent accidentally falling off the edge. If this is impossible, use fixed-rope techniques described in section I when approaching the rappel point. A staging area large enough to hold the group in a safe place nearby is also desirable.

### Installation Considerations

11-64. The two major factors in deciding how to attach a rappel rope to an anchor are whether the rope needs to be retrievable from the bottom and whether redundancy in rappel rope strands is necessary for safety. Both characteristics cannot be achieved at the same time.

11-65. In an operational environment, or during advanced military mountaineering training, ropes must be retrievable. This is because there is presumably no other feasible way to negotiate the vertical obstacle, or the group would be using it. All people and equipment must rappel, and the rope must be recovered for future use. This means that the rope cannot be fixed to the top anchor when the last Soldier begins rappelling.

11-66. When training basic rappelling, retrieving ropes is not necessary. Appropriate rappel sites for inexperienced rappellers should include access between the top and bottom by some other safe means for set up and breakdown. This allows for a greater safety margin by using two fixed independent strands for rappel ropes. Not only does this add redundancy to the system, but also using two strands replicates typical rappelling in an operational environment.

11-67. Anchors that support the maximum force rappellers could exert should be established as described in chapter 9. All rigging should be inspected by a qualified advanced military mountaineer, at a minimum.

### Retrievable Rappels

11-68. Retrievable rappels can be established using appropriate single-point anchors (such as a tree, boulder, horn of rock) using only the rope, or they can be attached to cordelettes, or webbing tied around the anchor feature. If other materials are used, then they cannot be retrieved from the bottom.

11-69. Multipoint anchors require additional materials to establish. This material cannot be retrieved from the bottom.

11-70. The minimum necessary equipment should be used in building retrievable rappel anchors.

### Half-Rope Length

11-71. The simplest and most common method is to just run one end of the rope around anchor feature, and then double back stack it until the middle of the rope is around the anchor feature. This ensures both strands are the same length. Tie a stopper knot at the bottom end with both strands leaving 12- to 18-inch tails. This prevents accidentally rappelling off the end of the rope. The ropes are then thrown or back stacked over the edge.

11-72. Sometimes the rope cannot be routed directly around the anchor. The rope can then be back stacked so that the middle of the rope passes through the attachment point for an anchor made from cordelette or webbing or clipped to it using a locking carabiner.

11-73. If a rope could be cut, it is possible to make the system redundant for all but the last rappeller. This system is established in the same manner as above, but once complete a BHK is tied close to the anchor with both strands. This creates two fixed independent strands and adds redundancy to the system. Before the last Soldiers begin to rappel, they simply untie the BHK, and the system is retrievable again.

### Full-Length Rope

11-74. Full-length rappels require two standard-length ropes. Route the end of one rope around or through the anchor point and tie a flat double overhand with the ends of both ropes joining them. Double back stack the ropes and tie a stopper knot as described above. Using a flat overhand to join the rappel ropes reduces the likelihood of the knot being stuck in vegetation of rock features during retrieval.

11-75. Full-length rappels can be made redundant for all but the last rappeller in the same manner as is described above.

### Rappel Rope Retrieval

11-76. Once all Soldiers have reached the bottom and any stopper knots have been untied, the rope can be retrieved by pulling one strand. The other strand travels up to the anchor and falls. If there are any knots in it, they can become jammed in the anchor and prevent rope retrieval. It is a good idea to get excess personnel away from the area prior to pulling in case any loose rocks are dislodged above.

11-77. When pulling the rope on a multipitch rappel, it is a good idea to route the end being pulled through the next anchor, so the rope is less likely to be dropped.

### Rappels with Fixed Strands

11-78. Whenever a threat of the installation rope being cut is identified, two fixed independent strands should be used. Two strands add redundancy and reduce the likelihood that a fall may occur in a cut rope incident. Installations with two fixed independent strands are typically the standard for rappelling training whether a hazard has been identified.

### Half-Rope Length

11-79. If using a single anchor, anchor the middle of the rope to it with a bowline knot. If using a pre-equalized multipoint anchor, tie a fixed loop in the middle of the rope, and attach it to the anchor with two opposing locking carabiners.

### Full-Rope Length

11-80. Anchor the rappel ropes by tying a bowline knot with the top ends of both ropes.

11-81. Situations may arise where the distance of anchors from each other, or the distance of the anchors from the rappel point, requires using a second rope to construct an anchor, or extend the anchor master point closer to the loading platform, allowing for the maximum amount of rope to be utilized for the rappel. In this case, tie a fixed loop with the top ends of both ropes, and clip it to the anchor system master point using two opposing locking carabiners.

### Rappel Installation Special Techniques

11-82. There are a few problems that mountaineers should be prepared to solve related to rappelling. The common problems are damaged ropes, short ropes, and the threat of a rope being cut when retrievability is necessary.

### Damaged Rope

11-83. Ropes occasionally become damaged but may need to be used until they can be replaced following an operation. Mountaineers must be familiar with how to safely use damaged ropes for rappelling.

11-84. First, tie an overhand knot, isolating the damaged spot in the rope. The rope can then continue to be used for many mountaineering tasks. However, the knot in the rope prevents Soldiers from rappelling past.

11-85. If a retrievable rappel is necessary, the damaged rope can be used as follows. The rope is back stacked around the anchor as usual. A fixed bight is tied in the damaged strand near the anchor. That bight is then clipped to the nondamaged strand with a locking carabiner. When loaded, the carabiner fits snug to the anchor like a slip knot. (See figure 11-9.) Both strands of rope are thrown or back stacked off the cliff.

11-86. Soldiers can then rig for rappel using only the nondamaged strand and descend the obstacle.

**Note.** Rappelling on a single strand creates less friction.

11-87. Once all Soldiers have rappelled, the damaged strand, or tagline, can be pulled to retrieve the rope. The nondamaged strand travels up through the carabiner, around the anchor and back down. More care should be taken when pulling the rope because it is more likely to become hung up using this technique. This technique also works when a full-length rappel is necessary and one of the ropes is damaged near the middle.

*[Figure 11-9. Tagline retrievable rappel using a tagline — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Short Rope

11-88. Sometimes a group may only have one rope that is not long enough to reach the bottom of a cliff, and the rope cannot be retrieved because the cliff is taller than a half-rope length.

11-89. If the group has enough cordelettes or shorter sections of rope, it can solve the problem. To do so, one end of the rope is lowered until it is on the ground. Cordelettes are then joined by a single strand to the other end until they reach the ground.

11-90. The installation is then rigged to be retrievable with a tagline (as described in paragraphs 11-83 through 11-87, page 290). Soldiers can then rappel on the strand of static rope. When personnel are at the bottom, the end with the cordelettes can be pulled to retrieve the rope. Take more care when pulling the rope because it is more likely to become hung up using this technique.

11-91. Small units can use this technique to reduce the weight of rope they need to carry. Rather than carry two full-sized ropes to rappel a full-rope length, they can carry one rope and a 6- or 7-mm tagline that is cut the same length from a spool, or plan to use joined cordelettes that are spread out amongst their members.

### Threat of Cut Rope Using a Retrievable System

11-92. Some situations may require a retrievable rappel, but a threat to the rope is identified (such as sharp rock or falling rock hazard) that cannot be mitigated by simply padding an edge. This problem can be resolved by one of two methods.

11-93. The first involves establishing the retrievable rappel system described above, and then attaching a tube-style device and locking carabiner tight to the anchor. This effectively fixes both strands. All but the last Soldier conducts the rappel with fixed strands. The last Soldier uses the attached device and rappels carefully as normal. This limits the exposure of all but one Soldier to the threat of cut rope. The rope can be pulled from below as previously described.

11-94. The second involves establishing the retrievable rappel system described above, and then tying a big knot close to the anchor with both strands fixing them. All but the last Soldier conducts the rappel with fixed strands. The last Soldier unties the knot and rappels carefully as normal. This limits the exposure of all but one Soldier to the threat of cut rope. The rope can be pulled from below as previously described.

## Rappelling Techniques

11-95. Different situations call for different rappel attachment techniques. They vary in how much friction they produce, the security in which the rappeller is attached to the rope, and whether they allow the rappeller to go hands free. The consequence of falling determines the type of attachment to the rope. Terrain severity determines how much friction is required to control descent. Whether or not other tasks need to be conducted mid-rappel determines the need for a hands-free brake.

11-96. During rappelling, the body generally forms an L-shape with the feet shoulder-width apart, legs straight, and buttocks parallel to the ground. When carrying equipment or additional weight, a modified L-shape is used with the legs slightly lower than the buttocks to compensate for the additional weight. The rappeller’s back is straight. Rappellers never let go of the ropes with their brake hand until the rappel is complete. Rappellers should keep their feet flat against the surface they are rappelling down and their weight fully on the rope throughout the rappel.

### Hasty Rappel

11-97. The hasty rappel technique is only used on low-angle slopes. Its primary advantages are that it is simple to learn and execute and requires no specialized equipment. However, take care not to underestimate the terrain being negotiated. Application of this technique in terrain that is too steep can have deadly consequences. The level of rappeller surefootedness determines the severity of terrain for which this technique is appropriate. Gloves can be worn to prevent rope burns.

11-98. Facing slightly sideways to the anchor, rappellers place the ropes horizontally across their back, under their armpits, and palm up. The hand nearest to the anchor is the guide hand, and the other is the brake hand. Bending the rope around the body increases friction. (See figure 11-10, page 292.)

11-99. While moving, Soldiers should keep their legs as perpendicular to the slope as possible, leaning into the rope. This increases the security of foot placements and reduces the likelihood of a slip and fall.

11-100. To stop, rappellers rotate their body to face uphill and bring their brake hand across the front of their body to their opposite shoulder.

*[Figure 11-10. Hasty rappel — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Munter Hitch Rappel

11-101. This method provides adequate friction and control and is the simplest and easiest method of rappelling to establish on steep terrain when without a standard rappel device. Most often, it is used as a backup for when a more efficient device is lost or forgotten.

11-102. The drawback of using a Munter hitch is that it twists the rope, causing snarl at the bottom that makes future use difficult. An experienced Soldier can separate the strands with the brake hand and counter the twist of the rope as it feeds through the hitch to lessen this effect. It is not recommended to have multiple Soldiers rappel on the same rope using this method. Twisting is lessened if the rappel is on steep terrain and the strands do not touch the ground. This allows the twists to undo themselves. Twisting becomes more of a problem if the terrain is at a low angle, and the rope is mostly lying on the ground.

11-103. To use this technique, attach a locking carabiner into a harness. Then tie a Munter hitch in both strands of the rappel rope and place it in the locking carabiner. (See figure 11-11.)

11-104. Descent is controlled in the same way as with the devices described above.

*[Figure 11-11. Munter hitch rappel — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Figure-Eight Descender

11-105. Using a figure-eight descender provides a secure attachment to the rappel rope and adequate friction for use in steep-to-vertical terrain.

11-106. The advantage of these is that they work well with both larger diameter ropes and static or low-stretch ropes. The disadvantages are that they are less versatile, heavier, and bulkier than tube-style devices, which serve multiple mountaineering purposes.

11-107. To rig the device for descent, pass a double bight through the large opening, and then around the smaller one. Then attach the figure eight to a harness using a locking carabiner through the small opening. (See figure 11-12, page 294.)

11-108. Soldiers assume a typical L-shaped position while rappelling and control their rate of descent with the brake strand by slowly feeding slack from below them toward the device. Two hands should be used on the brake strand, positioned in front of the body below the device.

*[Figure 11-12. Figure-eight descender — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Tube-Style Devices

11-109. Using a tube-style device provides a secure attachment to the rappel rope and adequate friction for use in steep to vertical terrain.

11-110. Most climbing teams use these. The advantages are that they can be used for both belaying and rappelling with one or two ropes. They are compact and lightweight. They do not twist the ropes as much as figure-eight descenders. The disadvantages are that they do not work as smoothly when used with static or low-stretch ropes due to the more tightly woven rope sheath and stiffer feel. They also do not typically work well on ropes larger than 11 mm in diameter.

11-111. To rig the device for descent, pinch the rappel rope into a double bight, and then pass one through each hole in the tube starting on the side without a wire hanger. Clip a locking carabiner through the wire hanger and both bights of rope. Then attach the device to a harness with a locking carabiner so that the brake strands are oriented down or toward the intended brake hand. (See figure 11-13.)

11-112. Descent is controlled in the same way as with figure-eight descenders.

*[Figure 11-13. Rappelling with a tube-style device — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Extending Rappel Devices

11-113. Using an extension moves the rappel device away from the body. This makes it easier to keep two hands on the brake strands. It also reduces the chance of straps or clothing from being caught in the device and stopping rope from feeding through it. This results in the rappeller becoming stuck on the rope, unable to descend, and requires advanced skills to fix. Wearing bulky cold-weather clothing or tactical load-bearing harnesses increase the likelihood of this occurring. Extending the device allows for the use of a hands-free backup.

11-114. To extend a device, create an extension by girth hitching a sling to the harness. Insert the rappel ropes into the device as described above and clip them into the extension with a locking carabiner. A basket hitch can also be used to create a shorter extension. Under most circumstances, it is recommended to use an extension when rappelling in mountainous terrain because it makes controlling descent easier and less fatiguing to the core when heavy loads are carried. Rappels using the Munter hitch technique cannot be extended.

**Hands-Free Backup**

11-115. A hands-free backup is a rigging technique, involving friction hitches or mechanical devices that prevent a load from falling should control of a brake strand be lost. To establish a hands-free backup, tie an autoblock around the rappel ropes below the device, and attach it to the climber’s harness with a locking carabiner. (See figure 11-15, page 300.) Any time an autoblock is used, it must be tested to ensure it grabs before starting the rappel. If it does not, more wraps should be added until it does.

11-116. This allows Soldiers to perform other tasks mid-rappel. The most common example is the first rappeller needing to untangle the rope on the way down. During rappelling, Soldiers must prevent the autoblock from engaging and stopping their downward progress with one hand while the other feeds rope to the device. To engage the autoblock, the Soldier ceases minding the autoblock and it will engage, forcing the rappel rope into the brake position. When Soldiers are ready to continue rappelling, they resume control of the brake strands with both hands and disengage the autoblock. Descent can then continue.

11-117. Using an autoblock can provide a backup in case Soldier drops the brake strand. However, it is not the best back-up technique for large groups of rappellers to use given the additional time it takes to establish, manage, and untie from the rope.

**Rucksack Support**

11-118. An extension can be used to make rappelling with heavy rucksacks easier. (See figure 11-14.) This is done by rigging for rappel with an extension. Then Soldiers can girth hitch a sling to the frame of the pack and attach it to the locking carabiner in their rappel device using any carabiner. This allows the device to support some of the weight of the pack and reduce core fatigue while the Soldier rappels.

*[Figure 11-14. Extended device and hands-free break — illustration not reproduced.]*

### Belaying Rappellers

11-119. The fireman’s belay can be used to prevent rappellers from falling should they lose control of the brake strand. This technique involves a Soldier loosely holding the rappel rope and watching the rappeller from the bottom. If the rappeller falls, the Soldier pulls on the rope hard. This forces the device to brake and stop the rappeller’s fall. When the rappeller regains footing and control of the brake strand, tension is released, allowing them to continue rappelling.

### Rappel Site Operation

11-120. Because of the inherent dangers of rappelling, special care must be taken to ensure safe and successful descents.

11-121. High-consequence rappel sites must be established and managed with the supervision of a qualified advanced military mountaineer or higher.

### Duties and Responsibilities of the Rappel Site Supervisor

11-122. An experienced mountaineer should be overall in charge of any movement on a rope system. This person ensures safety checks are completed and that there is a plan for the flow of personnel onto and off the system and is the one responsible for the entire event.

11-123. Duties of rappel site supervisors are as follows:

- Ensures that the anchors are sound, and the knots are properly tied.
- Ensures that loose rock and debris are cleared from the loading platform.
- Allows only one Soldier per rappel point on the loading platform at a time and ensures that the rappel point is run orderly.
- Ensures that each Soldier is properly prepared for the rappel: helmet with chinstrap fastened and harness or rappel seat is correct (if required). They ensure that the rappeller is hooked up to the rope correctly and is aware of the proper braking position.
- Ensures that the proper signals or commands are used.
- Dispatches each Soldier down the rope.
- Ensures the ropes will pull nicely when the rappel is retrieved; gets the joining knot past the edge, if necessary.
- Ensures safety lines or personal safeties for rappel site personnel are established and Soldiers remain clipped to them when necessary.

### Communication

11-124. Radio communication from the top to the bottom should be used to ensure all ropes touch the ground and someone is providing a fireman’s belay before the rappeller commits to the rappel.

11-125. Loud voice commands for anything other than warning of falling objects should be discouraged, as they are unnecessary and tactically inappropriate. However, if they are used in training, they should be clear, concise, and understood by all participants.

11-126. Soldiers can determine if the rappeller ahead of them is clear of the rope by testing it for tension. If someone is still attached to the rope, then the rope will be tight. If it is slack, the next rappeller can hook up.

### Increasing Speed and Efficiency

11-127. All Soldiers should prepare themselves for rappelling at a staging area a short distance from the rappel site. Ideally, all harness and rigging other than attachment to the rope is conducted there, prior to arrival at the site. This will make rappelling groups more efficient.

11-128. The simplest method of speeding up rappelling is to instruct all rappellers to introduce slack into the system when they reach the bottom. This can be done by pulling slack through their device, or rappelling a couple steps further than necessary, and then stepping back toward the cliff. This will allow the next Soldier to hook up while they are unhooking from the system.

11-129. Rappel devices and locking carabiners may be collected and stacked on the ropes by the site supervisor prior to beginning rappelling. Soldiers then only need to attach their extension to the device before rappelling.

11-130. Retrievable rappels during tactical operations or advanced military mountaineer training can be sped up by establishing the rappel as usual, then tying a BHK knot in the ropes near the anchor. This fixes both strands. Soldiers can now rappel on each strand, doubling the rate that Soldiers can descend. This technique may not be appropriate if sharp rocks are present on the rappel route.

### Multipitch Rappelling

11-131. Descent routes during tactical operations and advanced military mountaineer training often involve a series of rappels to reach the bottom. This requires additional techniques to ensure safety and efficiency of descent. The distance between anchor points is called a pitch.

11-132. When conducting multiple rappels, the standard technique of extending the device should be used and the first Soldier down should use an autoblock for backup. Additionally, a second locking carabiner should be placed in each rappeller’s extension sling. They will use this to attach themselves to the next anchor point prior to detaching from the rappel rope to allow the next rappeller to descend. In this way, they remain safely attached to the rappel rope or an anchor point. Once the first rappeller is safely anchored below, preferably out of the way of possible rock or ice fall, they fireman belay the remaining rappellers one at a time down to this point. Once the last Soldier is secured to the second anchor point, the rope is pulled, and the next pitch is rigged for rappel.

11-133. Soldiers can stack devices rigged for the rappel prior to the first one leaving, or each can hook up independently in turn. Typically, the most experienced members rappel first so that they can establish the next rappel anchor down. Stacking devices allows the leader to inspect all other Soldiers’ hookups prior to leaving, reducing the chance of error.

11-134. The most difficult aspect of multipitch rappelling is when descending into unknown or unfamiliar terrain. The first Soldier down must effectively manage the ropes during the descent and have the necessary equipment for establishing the next anchor point. If adequate anchor points are found before nearing the ends of the rope, the decision must be made whether to use it or continue lower in hopes of finding something further down. Although it is most efficient to gain the maximum distance from each rappel, if a safe anchor point cannot be found lower, it will become necessary to ascend the ropes, taking much more time.

## Section III – Ascending and Descending Transitions

11-135. Many situations exist during which advanced military mountaineers must be able to change direction of travel while hanging from a fixed rope. The direction change can be planned or unplanned and involve single-strand or double-strand systems. Whenever mountaineers’ duties or tasks may require them to make hanging transitions, they should use single-strand systems that allow them to use safer special equipment and techniques. However, they should also be prepared to transition from rappelling to ascending on two-rope systems using equipment routinely carried.

11-136. Transitioning while hanging on a rope should only be done by level 2 or higher mountaineers who have received formal training in ascending and descending transition techniques.
