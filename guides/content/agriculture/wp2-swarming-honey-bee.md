# Swarming (honey bee)

_Encyclopedia article (Wikipedia, CC BY-SA 4.0), converted to plain Markdown and excerpted: references, images, infoboxes, navigation boxes and non-practical sections were removed._

**Swarming** is a honey bee colony's natural means of reproduction. In the process of swarming, a single colony splits into two or more distinct colonies.

Swarming is mainly a spring phenomenon, usually within a two- or three-week period depending on the locale, but occasional swarms can happen throughout the producing season. Secondary *afterswarms*, or *cast swarms*, may occur. Cast swarms are usually smaller and are accompanied by a virgin queen. Sometimes a beehive will swarm in succession until it is almost totally depleted of workers.

One species of honey bee that participates in such swarming behavior is *Apis cerana*. Reproductive swarms of this species may temporarily settle near the original nest before departing for a new nesting site. Scout bees search for suitable locations in which the new colony can establish its nest. Successful scouts then return and communicate information about potential nesting sites to the other bees. *Apis mellifera* participates in a similar swarming process. The nest-site selection process of this species has been studied extensively.

Swarming occurs in both rural and urban areas. In urban environments, swarms may originate from managed hives or from established unmanaged colonies. Swarms can temporarily settle on buildings and other structures, sometimes requiring collection or relocation by beekeepers.

## Preparation

Worker bees create *queen cups* throughout the year. When the hive is getting ready to swarm, the queen lays eggs into the queen cups. New queens are raised, and the hive may swarm as soon as the queen cells are capped and before the new virgin queens emerge from their queen cells. Before swarming, workers reduce the amount of food provided to the queen, causing her weight to decrease and her egg laying to decline. This prepares the queen for flight and temporarily interrupts the brood production of the original colony.

During swarm preparation, scout bees may identify nearby locations where the departing swarm can temporarily cluster. When a honey bee swarm emerges from a hive, it generally does not fly far at first. The bees may gather in a tree or on a branch only a few metres (yards) from the hive. There they cluster around the queen while scout bees search for suitable new nest locations. This intermediate stop is not intended for permanent habitation, and the swarm normally leaves after a suitable nesting site has been selected. The duration of this temporary clustering period can range from several hours to several days. Scouts communicate information about prospective sites through waggle dances, and the collective activity of the scouts contributes to the final selection.

During the intermediate stop, the swarm performs thermoregulation, maintaining a relatively warm cluster core while the outer layers can remain considerably cooler. The arrangement of bees within the cluster helps reduce heat loss and conserve energy. Before departing for the new nesting site, bees warm their flight muscles to approximately 35 °C, enabling coordinated flight. Scout bees produce worker-piping signals that stimulate other members of the cluster to prepare for take-off.

Swarming is a vulnerable time in the life of honey bees. Swarms are provisioned only with the nectar or honey they carry in their stomachs. A swarm may starve if it does not find a new nesting site and additional food resources. This risk can be particularly significant when an early swarm departs during warm spring weather followed by prolonged cold or rain. The original colony usually retains food stores and developing brood after one or more swarms have departed. However, the replacement queen may be lost to predators during her mating flight, or adverse weather may prevent successful mating. If the colony no longer contains eggs or sufficiently young larvae from which another queen can be raised, it may become permanently queenless. A cast swarm usually contains a young virgin queen.

### Absconding

The propensity to swarm differs among honey bee species and populations. Africanized bees are notable for their tendency to swarm or abscond. Unlike reproductive swarming, *absconding* occurs when the entire colony abandons its nesting site rather than dividing into separate colonies. Absconding can be associated with environmental conditions, food shortages, disturbances or unsuitable nesting conditions. Poor physical conditions, such as water entering the hive, excessive temperatures, insufficient shade or water, nearby fires and repeated disturbance, may encourage colonies to abandon their nests.

Absconding behavior has been documented in Africanized honey bees, including responses to disturbances and changes in resource availability. Colonies that abandon their nests may face substantial risks if suitable nesting sites or food sources are unavailable. In colder climates, inadequate winter food stores can also threaten colony survival, although this is not necessarily associated with absconding.

Absconding has been described in terms of different contributing conditions. Resource-related absconding may occur in response to shortages of water, pollen or nectar, whereas disturbance-related absconding may occur following predation, pest infestation or repeated disruption of the nest.

Generally, a weak bee colony is less likely to produce a reproductive swarm until its population has increased. Weak colonies may result from inadequate food supplies, diseases such as foulbrood, or a queen with a reduced rate of egg production. These conditions can affect colony growth and its capacity to reproduce through swarming.

## Nest site selection

A suitable nesting site for western honey bees must provide enough space to accommodate the colony and its developing combs. Studies of nest-site preferences have found that cavities with volumes of approximately 40 L are generally favored over substantially smaller cavities. Other important characteristics include protection from the elements, a relatively small entrance near the bottom of the cavity and a suitable degree of exposure to sunlight. The presence of abandoned honeycombs may also influence the selection of a nesting cavity, although the suitability of the cavity remains important.

Scout bees are typically experienced foragers that investigate potential nesting locations while the swarm rests in a temporary cluster. Only a minority of workers undertake nest-site scouting. An individual scout returning to the cluster promotes a location she has found. She uses the waggle dance to communicate its direction and distance, while the duration and intensity of her dancing contribute to the recruitment of other scouts. Bees recruited through these dances can visit the proposed site and subsequently advertise it if they assess it favorably.

Several sites may be promoted by different scouts at first. After several hours, and sometimes days, a favored location gradually emerges from this decision-making process. Because the swarm has limited food reserves, selecting a suitable site without excessive delay is important. Research indicates that preparations for departure begin when a sufficient number of scout bees are present at a potential nesting site, a process known as *quorum sensing*. Experimental observations suggest that a quorum of approximately 20–30 scouts can trigger the signals associated with preparations for departure. This process does not require every scout to agree on a single location before preparations for departure begin. The recruitment of scouts to favorable sites and the eventual reduction in advertising for alternatives contribute to the colony's collective decision.

When sufficient support has developed for a nesting location, the clustered swarm prepares to depart. Worker-piping signals and warming of flight muscles precede take-off. The swarm may fly a kilometre or more to the chosen location, with scout bees helping to guide the other workers through directional flight behavior. The collective decision-making process enables the swarm to compare potential nesting sites and coordinate its movement towards a selected location.

## Beekeeping

### Swarm control methods

Beekeepers who do not wish to increase their number of active hives may use one or more methods of swarm control. Many approaches involve reducing crowding, modifying the distribution of brood and food stores, or simulating aspects of colony division.

- Clipping one wing of the queen. When one wing of the queen is clipped, a swarm may still emerge, but the queen's limited ability to fly may cause the swarm to gather near the original hive. The cluster can then sometimes be collected. Wing clipping does not prevent the colony from initiating swarming behavior and may result in the loss of the queen. It is therefore more accurately regarded as a means of restricting swarm departure than as a method of preventing swarming itself.
- In the Demaree method, a frame of capped brood is removed with the old queen. This frame is put in a hive box with empty drawn frames and foundation at the same location as the old hive. A honey super is added above this hive box, followed by a queen excluder. The remaining hive box, without the queen, is inspected for queen cells. This upper box, which contains much of the remaining brood and bees, is placed above the queen excluder. Foraging bees return to the lower box, reducing the population of the upper section. Subsequent inspections are used to identify and remove additional queen cells. After a period of separation, the two sections may be recombined. The method is intended to reduce the colony's tendency to swarm while retaining a single managed colony.
- Keeping the brood nest open is another method of swarm control. In preparation for swarming, bees may increasingly occupy brood-rearing space with nectar and honey. The reduction in available cells can limit the queen's egg laying. Expanding the brood area can reduce crowding and allow continued brood production. Different variations include placing empty drawn combs or foundation in the brood nest, or moving brood combs into an upper box to encourage expansion. These approaches are intended to modify the conditions associated with swarm preparation.
- Checkerboarding. In late winter, frames are rearranged above the growing brood nest. Frames containing honey are alternated with empty drawn combs or foundationless frames. The method is based on the hypothesis that altering the arrangement of food reserves may influence a colony's preparation for reproductive swarming. Its effectiveness can vary according to colony conditions and management practices.
- Colony equalization involves transferring food and brood from stronger colonies to weaker colonies. This can reduce congestion in strong colonies while increasing the resources and developing worker population of weaker colonies before major nectar flows.

Alternatively, swarm traps containing Nasonov pheromone lures may be used to attract reproductive swarms. These traps provide potential nesting cavities for scout bees to investigate. Beekeepers managing a colony that has already swarmed may also provide suitable young brood if the original colony has lost its replacement queen. Eggs or young larvae can allow the workers to raise another queen, provided the colony retains sufficient workers and resources.

### Swarm capture

Beekeepers are sometimes called to capture swarms originating from feral honey bee colonies or from managed hives. Depending on local circumstances, swarm collection services may be offered by individual beekeepers or specialist bee removal companies. Honey bee swarms can often be collected alive and relocated to suitable hive equipment. Removal becomes more complicated when the bees have already established a colony within a building or another enclosed structure. Where feasible, relocation provides an alternative to exterminating the colony.
