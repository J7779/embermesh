# Setting Up a Cold-Weather Camp and Organizing the Tent

*From TC 21-3, Soldier's Handbook for Individual Operations and Survival in Cold-Weather Areas (US Army, 30 September 2025), Chapter 5. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Accomplishing the Mission

5-1. The essential requirements for survival and mission accomplishment in cold regions are warmth, food, water, and shelter. Soldiers are provided with tents that can be erected quickly. However, in the confusion of combat, tents can be lost or destroyed, and Soldiers can become separated from their ahkio groups.

5-2. When this occurs, timber and snow, which are usually available, are used to make shelter. Additionally, a detailed explanation of the bivouac routine should be contained in the unit SOP.

### Bivouac Sites

5-3. Bivouac sites, other than those that are more permanent in nature in the rear areas, are tactical or designated to provide living accommodations within a defended position. The tactical bivouac (patrol base) is usually not occupied for more than one night, is established at or after last light, and needs little preparation. It is organized for a rapid and disciplined response to enemy action.

5-4. The defensive position is likely to be occupied for longer periods and is established accordingly. The layout and placement of tents and shelters is coordinated within the defense, and according to the unit SOP.

5-5. The selection of bivouac areas is important and needs careful consideration. The challenge of area selection varies with the tactical situation, weather conditions, and terrain. The bivouac area is strategically located according to the principles of security and defense. There is concealment from air and ground observation, and shelter from the wind.

### Forests

5-6. Forested areas pose few challenges in comparison to areas that are located above the tree line. In selecting areas, ensure that local camouflage materials are available for use with the snow camouflage nets. Forests, especially those where the vegetation is spruce or pine, provide excellent patrol bases. Material for camouflage, firewood, and construction of shelters and fighting positions is abundant.

5-7. Spruce and pine forests are preferable for a variety of reasons; one being that these types of trees generally grow on well-drained soil. Another reason is they retain their needles year-round, offering better concealment and protection from wind and inclement weather than deciduous trees that lose their leaves during the winter months. The thick canopy helps disperse smoke from heater stacks and dissipate any ice fog that may form in the extreme cold.

### Frozen Swamps and Open Country (Tundra)

5-8. Firmly frozen swampy areas (muskeg), especially those that are covered with trees and brush, may also offer a good location to establish a patrol base. Muskeg areas often have very spindly spruce trees that provide virtually no cover, concealment, or protection from the wind.

5-9. Thickly iced streams and rivers located nearby can offer excellent routes for resupply, as well as routes for mounted and dismounted movement. It is generally difficult to construct dug-in fighting positions, forcing above-ground positions in these areas. Additionally, leaders should remember that a sudden thaw could leave everyone wallowing in a pool of mud.

5-10. Open country (tundra) presents difficulties such as poor cover and concealment, exposure to high winds, and drifting snow. (See figure 5-1 on page 59.) If establishing a patrol base in open areas, tents should be pitched (or shelters constructed) in the lee of natural windbreaks such as depressions or the downwind side of eskers, ridges, and hills. Where no natural windbreaks exist, dig tents into the snow as deeply as possible or construct windbreaks with snow or blocks of snow. (See figure 5-2 on page 59.)

### Streams and Areas Above the Tree Line

5-11. Look for streams in order to lower the silhouette of tents and provide a fighting cover. Sometimes small streams are present that have mature trees on the banks. A skilled enemy understands the value of these areas and has them under observation.

5-12. Above the tree line, mountainous terrain provides little shelter from observation or the elements. During high winds, lee slopes may offer shelter. However, they can also increase exposure to avalanche hazards if wind-driven snow is deposited on lee slopes. If the slope is steep enough, the snow eventually slides. In snow-covered mountains or when snowfall is imminent, always examine a slope’s potential for an avalanche prior to establishing a bivouac upon it.

### Valleys and Lakes

5-13. Areas lying in the bottom of deep valleys and large lakes should be avoided due to masses of cold air settling on low ground (also known as a temperature inversion). During windless conditions, temperature inversions can result in a low-lying patrol base producing its own cloud of ice fog due to tent heating. This can potentially reveal the location of the site to enemy observers, especially if the enemy is located at a higher vantage point than the patrol base.

5-14. If low-lying areas are used, look for large mature spruce trees to help mask tent heating signatures. Temperature inversions can usually be overcome by ascending to local high ground. Also, large rivers tend to be funnels for wind. Anchoring tents can be accomplished by using ice screws. Additionally, lakes can be useful for helicopter landing zones.

### Other Terrain and Weather Considerations

5-15. Select terrain that is of little tactical value to the enemy and off the main lines of drift, such as major ridges, creeks, valleys, and couloirs, as they are all likely avenues of approach. Be mindful of other factors:

- If the unit has the proper equipment, water resupply is probably not an issue when melting snow and ice for the main water source, but fuel resupply can become an issue if it is needed for melting snow and ice and/or providing heat.
- Consider dominant terrain features where an attack could develop.
- Look for the prevailing wind direction by observing the drift patterns around trees or rocks (see figures 5-1 and 5-2):
  - The downwind side is longer than the upwind side.
  - Try to select a site that is downwind from suspected enemy positions and avenues of approach.
  - Wind can carry sound and smell to the enemy, depending upon its direction.
  - High winds tend to disperse noise and smell.
  - Extremely cold and still air carries even the smallest sounds for a long distance.
  - While clearing space for the tent, use the bowsaw because it is much quieter than the axe or machete.
  - Be selective about positioning the tent to avoid a massive clearing project.
- Consider escape routes that facilitate rapid withdrawal.
- Consider the availability of camouflage and construction material:
  - Construction materials may have to be gathered and brought to the patrol base from another location.
  - This creates a trail leading straight to the bivouac location, so a good deception plan is enforced.
- Check illumination and weather conditions with the battalion or brigade intelligence staff officer:
  - In good weather or bright moonlight, try to select a shadowed area such as a north-facing slope or the middle of a wooded area.
  - Snow cover amplifies ambient light.
- Check the thermal detection capabilities (also with the intelligence staff officer) of enemy forces.
- Determine the feasibility of leaving ahkio groups and cold bagging or using improvised shelters.
- By leaving heat and shelter behind, the risk to the unit goes up as the temperature goes down.

5-16. When contact with the enemy is likely, the unit SOP identifies specific considerations when selecting the site for a tactical bivouac. Examples include—

- Location of the enemy.
- Enemy patrol habits.
- Air threat.
- Dominating features that identify enemy avenues of approach.
- Wind direction that can carry the sounds of movement (the bivouac site should be downwind of likely enemy avenues of approach, especially when wind speeds are low).
- Availability of natural camouflage.
- Amount of moonlight and weather conditions:
  - In good weather or bright moonlight, the site needs to be in an area of deep shadow.
  - Locating the site in the middle of the woods or on the northern slope of a ridge helps to prevent silhouetting the tents.

### Reconnaissance of the Bivouac Site

5-17. Before occupying a patrol base, a reconnaissance and surveillance (R&S) team pushes forward to ensure the suitability of the tentative patrol base. One thing that consistently gets overlooked is the posture of Soldiers waiting in a security halt for the R&S team to find and establish the site.

5-18. Leaders left with the main body ensure all Soldiers remain warm while maintaining security. The Level VII parka is stowed directly under the flap of the rucksack for this purpose. If the tactical situation allows exercising, Soldiers use buddy teams. In extreme cases it may be wise to move the entire element into the patrol base by force, although this should never be the norm.

5-19. For platoon-sized elements, usually the platoon leader, compass Soldier, and one member of each squad move forward to conduct a reconnaissance of the patrol base. Squad members are positioned at the 10 o’clock, 2 o’clock, and 6 o’clock positions and act as guides for the main body. Leaders that are on the reconnaissance move quickly and return as soon as possible to get the main body moving again. It is up to the unit to decide the method to use for conducting the reconnaissance while maintaining trail discipline.

### Tasks Accomplished by the R&S Team

5-20. All of the factors mentioned earlier in this chapter should be taken into consideration when choosing the exact location of a patrol base or assembly area. Initially, security may be established and maintained until arrival of the main body by positioning the squad members at the 10 o’clock, 2 o’clock, and 6 o’clock positions. (See figure 5-3.)

*[Figure 5-3. Track plan — illustration not reproduced.]*

5-21. If enough Soldiers are present, an observation post can be established at the dogleg track. The reconnaissance party maintains security of the patrol base until relieved by personnel from the main body. Other tasks include:

- Track plans should be established before anyone enters the site:
  - Normally, the main trail is extended well beyond the point where it enters the patrol base and may lead to a dummy patrol base established at a later time.
  - Branching off from the main trail, preferably at an acute angle to the direction of the enemy, is the patrol base track or Main Street.
  - Main Street is the single track that enters the actual patrol base perimeter.
  - Minor tracks are established leading off the Main Street to subunit areas where the tents are located.
  - The inner and outer perimeter tracks are also established.
- Defensive positions are established along the outer edge of the inner perimeter track:
  - The outer perimeter track should parallel the inner perimeter track but be outside of hand grenade range to the positions along the inner track.
  - Observation posts (OPs) can be established along the outer edge of the outer track, with extensive use of early warning devices or mechanical ambushes made there as well.
- The reconnaissance party designates the defensive sectors of responsibility for each subunit of the main body, with the boundary between each subunit sector marked:
  - At a minimum, this is accomplished by the reconnaissance party
  - Soldiers also take into consideration the time that the main body has been stationary.
- The tasks listed below can also be accomplished by the reconnaissance party:
  - The location for each tent to be set up is marked and a single trail is established to that point.
  - subunit integrity is maintained to the greatest extent possible, and subunit tent sites are designated with ease of access to that element’s defensive sector in mind.
  - Provide guides for the main body.

### Speed is Essential

5-22. Once the main body arrives, it is imperative that they are able to rapidly occupy the patrol base or assembly area without compromising the track plan, or noise and light discipline. Speed is essential.

5-23. Soldiers just completing a long, over-snow movement are most likely to be perspiring and tired. If they are left standing in the cold while the chain of command is determining where they belong, the unit is at risk of suffering cold-weather injuries.

## Trail Plans and Occupation

5-24. Trail plans are established before entering the bivouac site. Leading off the incoming trail, usually at an extreme angle to entry, another trail is established. This trail network leading to each subordinate unit area follows the inside of the tree line, preventing enemy observation of trails from the air or ground.

5-25. After linkup between the guides and the main body, the guides (at least one for the commander and for each subunit) explain the site layout and track plan before actually leading their assigned element into the patrol base.

5-26. Immediately upon arrival, the leader confirms decisions made by the reconnaissance or quartering party and issues the following orders:

- Confirms or modifies the track plan.
- Designates temporary location(s) for weapons and equipment under their direct control to prevent loss in the snow
- Confirms or modifies tent or improvised shelter locations.
- Decides the necessity and type of fighting positions (built up or dug in) to be used and the locations.
- Creates the squad or platoon sector sketches that are delivered to the leader within 20 minutes of the main body occupying the patrol base.
- Establishes priorities of work based on weather conditions.

5-27. As the main body moves into the patrol base, care is taken to ensure that track discipline (strict observance of the track plan) is enforced. In addition, noise and light discipline is rigidly enforced. Although it is virtually impossible to occupy a site in total silence (especially when pounding tent pins or cutting wood for positions and shelters), Soldiers are trained so that they do not need lanterns or flashlights to accomplish their tasks.

5-28. Once the main body is in place, the security elements provided by the reconnaissance party should be relieved as quickly as possible. As temperatures decrease, the need for speed in conducting this relief is increasingly important. Soldiers designated to relieve the reconnaissance party security forces are allowed to change out of clothing that may have become perspiration-soaked during movement to the bivouac, and to adjust their clothing to ensure adequate protection while performing relatively sedentary duties in OPs.

5-29. In temperate regions, the priority of work is usually rigidly adhered to but in extremely cold weather, the necessity of getting Soldiers fed or warm may take precedence over other tasks (such as maintenance). This is not a license to be lax toward alert and security requirements, as these always take priority.

5-30. Once the patrol base is established, communicate with higher headquarters and the supporting fire support element. Also, confirm location.

### Withdrawal Plan

5-31. The withdrawal plan is usually an azimuth and distance from the patrol base and is disseminated to all Soldiers. In extreme temperatures, the unit faces tough choices on the equipment they bring with them.

5-32. Trying to repack an ahkio group under pressure is probably not going to happen. Soldiers keep an orderly calm with minimal equipment in the tents. Weapons, FLCs, skis, or snowshoes are stored in a manner that can be reached in a hurry. The unit should rehearse Battle Drill 1: React to Direct Fire Contact While Dismounted—Platoon (07-PLT-D9501). (See appendix B.)

### Alert Plan

5-33. Thirty-three percent of the unit is on alert at the perimeter, usually positioned at the apexes. When tents are heating, there is an additional requirement to have fire guards. These Soldiers should not be counted as part of the alert posture as they are inside the tents performing other duties.

5-34. In very cold conditions, sentries can remain alert only for relatively short periods of time. They cannot remain motionless or look into the wind for long, and their hearing is impaired by the additional headwear required in cold temperatures. Leaders use their judgment on how long Soldiers can remain outside and still be able to perform their duties at peak efficiency without increased risk of cold-weather injury. The time a Soldier can spend outside a heated shelter performing relatively sedentary tasks varies with the temperature, degree of wind chill, visibility, and the cold-weather training and experience level of the Soldier.

### Security Plan

5-35. The apexes are where the security elements normally go, but another option is to have a security patrol walking the outer track. The Soldiers performing security duties are fully dressed and equipped. The squad’s fire guard is not a security element. They have their own duties inside the squad tents.

5-36. The following are some techniques that can be used for maintaining security in cold weather. If using one, ensure that the technique best suits the tactical situation:

- Combined living and fighting positions may be established on the inner track:
  - Improvised shelters or fighting positions can be built and used.
  - Ensure that warning systems are established on the outer edge of the outer track.
- One complete subunit may be used to perform security duties at any given time, allowing the remainder of the unit an extended period of time to rest in heated shelters.
- Double the number of personnel on guard:
  - Allow one Soldier to act as a fire guard inside the shelter or tent while another performs sentry duty.
  - After waking up their relief, the fire guard leaves the tent to relieve the sentry or sentries, who return to the tent for rest and warmth.
- When establishing OPs or emplacing mechanical ambushes and early warning devices, always approach the location from a flank without leaving a trail in the snow that points or leads the enemy directly into the patrol base.
- Keep lanterns inside tents turned as low as possible because—
  - This minimizes loss of night vision for personnel inside the tent.
  - The patrol base signature created from light showing through tent openings is reduced.
- Balance the need to provide a heated shelter against the necessity to reduce the thermal signature of the patrol base by using natural terrain features and snow constructions that can be used to mask the thermal signatures.

### Weapon and Mess Kit Planning

5-37. Special care is taken with weapons and mess kits. Weapons sweat when brought into heated shelters for cleaning. Count on them not to be mission capable for a long period of time. Crew-served weapons should not all be down at the same time.

5-38. Additional maintenance requirements include the SHA and squad stoves. (See TM 10-4520-261-12&P for more information.) JP-8 is an exceptionally dirty fuel, so these items need to be maintained in order to keep them functioning. Maintenance is more than maintaining weapons.

5-39. Mess kits need to be sanitized before each use and individual Soldier equipment needs to be maintained. This includes skis and snowshoes. VB boots and sleeping bags also need to be dried out. Pay attention to the ahkio components and repair covers, tow lines, and traces. Remove dirt and snow, sharpen axes and bowsaws, and perform any other needed equipment care.

### Personal Hygiene Plan

5-40. If necessary, Soldiers should shave in a heated shelter just before the rest cycle. This allows natural facial oils that are stripped off by shaving, time to replenish before once again becoming exposed to the cold. Facial oils provide natural protection against cold-weather injury.

5-41. Soldiers should brush teeth daily. If a toothbrush is unavailable, the chewed end of a twig can help. If a twig is not available, salt on a fingertip can be used to gently scrub the teeth. Additionally, hand washing is enforced, especially as Soldiers make water or prepare food.

5-42. Base layers should be changed as often as is practical but at least twice weekly. Socks should be changed as often as necessary to keep feet dry.

5-43. A central latrine should be established if dispersion within the patrol base is not too great. One latrine usually serves the needs of a platoon-sized unit. The following should be taken into consideration when establishing latrines:

- The preferred type of latrine for field use is a straddle trench: due to environmental restrictions during training or solidly frozen soil, it may not be permissible or possible to construct this latrine.
- Another recommended type of latrine is the cross-tree latrine (see figure 5-4 on page 64), especially when used in conjunction with a ration case lined with trash bags:
  - Once filled, the bags can be sealed, closed into the case, and burned or hauled to the rear for proper disposal.
  - If the ration box method is not practical, the waste alleviation and gelling bag (WAGBAG) can be used.
  - Ensure WAGBAGs are disposed of according to local policy and reordered using NSN 4510-01-485-0760.
- Latrines are sited on the downwind side of the patrol base:
  - Place them close to shelters so that Soldiers are encouraged to enforce sanitation discipline.
  - They should also be downwind, well away from snow gathering areas within the patrol base, and wind-proofed with branches, ponchos, snow blocks, or other available materials.
- Camouflage the latrines:
  - The site should be in-between the inner and outer track to maintain security for Soldiers using the facility.
  - Chemical lights can be used to mark the path when it is dark.
  - Make it directional by placing the lights on the friendly side of a tree.

*[Figure 5-4. Cross-tree latrine — illustration not reproduced.]*

- Soldiers should urinate in a designated spot on the ground, with fresh snow covering this spot daily:
  - The spot should not be covered after each use because the color of the snow at this location gives leaders valuable feedback on whether Soldiers are properly hydrated.
  - If the spot is bright yellow or a darker color, it is time to start melting more snow and forcing hydration.
- Placing an empty ammunition can on the SHA may be used to have warm water available for hand washing:
  - Using the ammunition can in conjunction with soap on a rope is a very effective method of hand washing.
  - Soap on a rope is made by placing an ordinary bar of soap in an old sock and hanging it on a tree adjacent to the latrine area.
  - Hand sanitizer may be considered convenient but is unreliable for removing ground-in dirt.

### Mess Plan

5-44. A mess plan includes water procurement. Water from central sources is often hard to transport due to freezing. Every effort should be made to ensure Soldiers have clean water. The following should be considered for water procurement:

- If water is to be transported to the unit in bulk, fill the 5-gallon cans with hot water:
  - This helps cut down on freezing enroute to the field.
  - Transport the water in a heated cargo compartment.
- Fire guards should continuously make water.
- Mess kits are cleaned and sanitized prior to use:
  - The first pot of boiling water is used as the sanitizer for the pots.
  - It can also serve as the hand wash for the Soldier making the water.
- The procedure for melting snow and ice into useable water is discussed in chapter 4.

5-45. Other considerations should be adhered to when creating a mess plan:

- When using MCWs and MREs:
  - MCWs can be stored frozen.
  - MREs can be frozen but must be consumed hot.
  - Multiple freeze and thaw cycles cause spoilage and damage.
  - Store any bulk food away from latrine sites and petroleum, oils, and lubricants.
  - Food preparation can be done as an assembly line by the Soldier on fire guard prior to other Soldiers getting up or returning from duties.
  - Proper sanitation practice is enforced.
- Plan for waste disposal: poor waste disposal practices—
  - Can provide the enemy with a great deal of information.
  - May constitute violations of environmental regulations and proper field sanitation procedures.
- Follow these guidelines for proper waste disposal:
  - Back haul all trash to the fullest extent possible to deny the enemy a valuable intelligence resource.
  - Burial and burning are the last options.
  - If temporary storage is needed, store away from the ahkio, food and water storage, and snow gathering areas.
  - Guard against animal scavenging.

### Rest Plan

5-46. Sleep is very important. It keeps morale up and aids in sound decision making. Leaders ensure that all Soldiers are getting enough sleep. Normal wear and tear of constant operations in the cold wears on the body. Fatigue can set in very quickly just by trying to stay warm.

5-47. In extreme cases of tactical necessity, the leader may elect to have the unit sleep fully clothed on top of their mats. The leader may also elect to have more Soldiers on the line, increase R&S patrols, or a combination of the two. The alert posture of 33 percent should not be violated.

## Organizing the Tent

5-48. The tent group leader is responsible for allocating personal space within the tent. When doing so, the leader takes into consideration the duty roster and the need for an orderly exit in the event of an emergency.

5-49. The tent group leader, usually the squad leader or senior occupant of the tent, is responsible for ensuring that the tent group is properly set up, maintained, struck, and packed. (See figure 5-5 on page 66.) The leader is also responsible for everything that occurs inside the tent. While bivouacking, the leader ensures—

- Sleeping space is properly allocated.
- Weapons and equipment are stored outside the tent according to the SOP.
- Track, camouflage, noise, and light disciplines are strictly enforced.
- Guard/sentry rotations are established and disseminated.
- Guards/sentries know who their relief is and where that Soldier sleeps.
- Blackout is maintained when personnel enter and exit the tent.
- The tent is de-iced and brushed off, with drift snow removed regularly.
- Fire precautions are observed.
- Squad stoves and lanterns are refueled outside.
- Weapons, equipment, stoves, and lanterns are regularly maintained.
- Soldiers brush snow off clothing and equipment before entering the tent.
- Soldiers dry their clothes at every opportunity.
- Water is made continuously, and proper hydration is enforced.
- Each Soldier receives adequate rations and all rations are consumed.
- Cooking utensils are clean.
- Lanterns are hung by a chain at minimum of 18 inches below the apex of the tent; hanging a lantern any closer to the tent is a fire hazard.
- Fuel usage is monitored.

### Organization of the Tent Interior

5-50. Tents are just large enough to provide adequate sleeping space for Soldiers, along with a small area for cooking, washing, and performing the duties of the fire guard. Orderly and disciplined arrangements are a necessity in such cramped circumstances. The following procedures are established to enhance the comfort, safety, and operational effectiveness of personnel required to live in tents. Many of these procedures are also readily adaptable to life in improvised shelters.

5-51. Only the minimum required individual clothing, equipment, and rations are allowed inside the tent. Generally, Soldiers bring their canteens, daily rations, insulated sleeping mat, sleeping bag, a small sharp knife, and the clothing required when leaving the tent are the items needed. Damp clothing and equipment may be brought inside to be hung up to dry but once dry, these items should be placed in the rucksack outside the tent. Prior to every stand-to, all items are packed into the rucksack so that essential equipment for survival is available if the unit is forced to execute a rapid withdrawal under pressure.

### Storage Safety Inside the Tent

5-52. All Soldiers living in a heated tent should have a knife on hand at all times, especially while sleeping. If a tent fire occurs, it can take as little as 10 seconds for the fire to completely engulf the shelter, and less than a minute to destroy it. If Soldiers are asleep with the zipper of their sleeping bag closed, they may not have enough time to unzip the bag. If they cannot do this due to lack of time or a jammed zipper, they attempt to roll out from under the wall of the tent while still in their bag. This can be difficult, especially for someone disoriented and shocked into wakefulness. With a small, sharp knife immediately at hand, the Soldier can cut their way out of the sleeping bag and through the wall of the tent. This single item of equipment may end up being the difference between life and death.

5-53. As stated above, the tent group leader is responsible for allocating personal space within the tent. When doing so, the leader takes into consideration the duty roster and the need for an orderly exit in the event of an emergency.

5-54. Spare batteries for equipment such as night vision goggles (NVG) or radios should be kept in the tent but away from sources of direct heat such as stoves and lanterns. Battery power output is diminished when batteries become cold or frozen. Small, battery-powered items such as flashlights or electric razors may also be kept in the tent and are ideally stored in the owner’s sleeping bag.

### Weapon and Gear Placement

5-55. Weapons racks should be constructed outside the tent as close as possible to the main entrance. A poncho should be used to keep sights, barrels, and moving parts on weapons from becoming clogged with snow. Soldiers should always remember exactly where on the rack their individual weapon is located. If a situation arises resulting in a mad rush to arms, they can grab the correct weapon. The weapons rack is constructed in a manner identical to the cross-tree latrine but without the wind break that a latrine requires.

5-56. Rucksacks should be lined up on the ground outside the main door of the tent where they do not interfere with Soldiers entering or exiting. Establishing a specific order for Soldiers to line up their rucksack in relation to the others makes it easy for everyone to identify their gear during conditions of limited visibility. Other personal equipment such as helmets and body armor can be draped over or placed inside the rucksack.

5-57. In a cold-dry environment, it is not necessary to cover rucksacks and other gear (except weapons, ammunition, NVG, personal equipment, and communications equipment) with a poncho or other type of waterproof cover; the temperature is too cold for the equipment to become wet. Simply brush off any snow before using the item.

### Critical Considerations When in Camp

5-58. A plastic trash bag filled with snow taken from the snow collection area should be kept inside the tent. One of the duties of the fireguard is to melt snow for drinking water and have hot drinks ready for Soldiers coming in from sentry duty. The 5-gallon water can, which the fireguard can use as a seat, should be kept inside the tent and topped off whenever it is less than full.

5-59. The amount of clothing worn by Soldiers while resting inside the tent is dictated by the tactical situation and whether or not the shelter is heated. For example, if there is an increased chance of enemy contact, it may be best that Soldiers rest on top of their sleeping mat fully dressed. When a scenario such as this occurs, tent group leaders ensure that the temperature of the stove is regulated so that it is warm enough to keep the Soldiers comfortable, but not so warm that they begin to sweat.

5-60. When living in a close environment such as a tent or improvised shelter, the highest possible standards of sanitation and personal hygiene are maintained. Failure to enforce or practice good sanitation and hygiene can expose everyone to sickness and disease. The conditions that Soldiers live in during cold weather creates an exceptionally good opportunity for sickness to run rampant through the unit. Soldiers are more susceptible to becoming a cold-weather casualty if they do not keep everything clean. Remember the C in the mnemonic C-O-L-D. Keep it clean!

### Heating at Night

5-61. The tactical situation, weather, and Soldiers’ level of training and experience is considered when determining whether or not to operate heaters throughout the night. The major disadvantage of heating shelters all night long (other than increased fuel requirements) is the obvious thermal signature of a heated shelter in the middle of a cold environment and the necessity for additional Soldiers losing sleep to perform duty as a fire guard.

5-62. Some advantages of keeping the stove burning are that Soldiers lose less body heat and conserve more energy while sleeping, improving performance the following day. Also, troops can be more ready to react to a threat by sleeping fully clothed on top of their sleeping bag (or just their insulating pad).

5-63. Soldiers sleeping in heated shelters have the opportunity to dry wet clothing in the tent while they sleep. Wet clothes should not be dried in sleeping bags when resting. However, slightly damp clothing can dry in the bag.

5-64. In addition to melting snow and providing hot drinks, fire guard duties include topping off the squads’ water, keeping the heater at a constant temperature, and moving clothing on the dry lines. Fire guards are also tasked with weapons maintenance, monitoring communications, or conducting personal hygiene.

### Breaking Camp

5-65. When a unit departs a patrol base, the commander determines what time the unit begins movement. Using a backwards planning process, the commander also determines the drop-tent time. The time interval commanders allow their Soldiers between drop-tent and departure is kept as short as possible. Dropping tents should be done in the same order in which movement is conducted. The goal is to prevent Soldiers from unnecessarily standing around in the cold. Remember that the alert and security posture requirements reduce the number of Soldiers available to take down the tents or fulfill fighting positions.

5-66. The length of time needed is a direct reflection on the amount (and quality) of individual and collective training that is conducted preparing for cold-weather operations. A well-trained squad should require 45 minutes to pack all their personal and tent group equipment and be ready to move. It is the tent group leader’s responsibility to ensure that their tent is ready to strike at the designated time.

5-67. Before departing a patrol base, all latrines and garbage pits should be covered with at least two feet of earth or packed snow. Finally, leaders ensure that security precautions are not relaxed, and that track, camouflage, noise, or light discipline is not forgotten.

5-68. If a tent is slowly or improperly set up, only the occupants of that tent suffer. However, if a squad is slow in striking their tent, more efficient squads have to stand in the cold and wait for them. Ensuring that breaking camp and striking tents becomes a battle drill for Soldiers is extremely important.

### Wood Cutting Areas

5-69. Wood cutting areas for fighting positions, improvised shelters, or wood burning heaters (if liquid fuel in not available) should be known by all Soldiers in the unit to prevent many areas from being cut down. These are places where activity is not likely and well behind the unit’s defensive area, or in a decoy bivouac site. Dead spruce, pine, or the small limbs and branches under a live canopy of trees make the best firewood. Standing deadwood burns best but hardwood burns longest.

5-70. In an Arctic and subarctic environment, true hardwood is rare. Of the trees available, birch, tamarack, and spruce are the hardest woods. Burn the upper parts of dead trees during daylight because they give off lighter colored smoke. The lower parts of the trees contain resin and tar which burn well but make more and darker smoke. In daylight, this allows Soldiers positions to be easily identified by air and ground observation. Be aware that burning wood for heat produces a smell signature that can be detected at great distances. For fighting positions, use live trees 6 to 8 inches in diameter to provide the best resistance to enemy fire.

5-71. In some areas, animals scavenge garbage. Polar bears are known to boldly come into human habitations to scavenge. Ravens open trash bags and can even peck their way into ration cases in a short period of time.

### Winter Storage

5-72. Storage problems in the winter are increased by snow, low temperatures, thaws, and limited storage space. As space in any shelter is limited, store only those items inside the tent that are affected by the cold or are needed immediately. The remaining equipment should be centralized, marked clearly, and covered.

5-73. Store ammunition and fuel separately. Place wood or metal containers on boughs or poles to keep the containers from freezing to the ground. The unit SOP specifies what equipment is placed inside or outside of the tent, and where.
