# Mountain Route Planning Tools and Time-Distance Estimation

*From TC 3-97.61, Military Mountaineering (US Army, 26 November 2025), Appendix B. Paragraph numbers are the manual's own. Illustrations are not reproduced; their captions are kept so that references to figures still make sense.*

## Section I – Online Resources

B-1. There are many online resources available to assist with route planning in the mountains. It is important to note that these resources are not secure. Therefore, they should never be used for operational planning. They are an excellent resource for conducting mountaineering training off military installations.

B-2. Online resources are an excellent tool to use while gaining experience training in mountainous environments to further develop as a military mountaineer. These resources will help develop solid information-gathering habits. This collection must go beyond standard map reconnaissance. It allows Soldiers to gather important information that would not otherwise be discovered from a map sheet.

B-3. These resources when combined with solid map reconnaissance are vital to aid in safe navigation and information gathering. This will enable Soldiers to create the most accurate picture of the terrain and environment in which they are about to step foot in. They are also essential in mitigating the risk associated in the pursuit of the highly demanding training required to become a proficient military mountaineer.

B-4. See TC 3-25.26 for more information on general route planning strategies.

## Navigational Resources

B-5. Numerous free web-based mapping resources combine detailed maps and excellent route planning tools in a user-friendly format. Many allow users to create printable maps that also show the route, distance, and change in elevation. These programs have slope-shading functions, which help to easily identify steeper sections of terrain. This is particularly useful when identifying avalanche hazards. Soldiers should seek out digital mapping resources to aid in their planning, hazard assessment, and route creation.

## Climbing Route Information

B-6. From a unit training perspective, the two resources listed in paragraphs B-7 and B-8 can aid leaders in the planning process by identifying specific areas that meet realistic training objectives. These resources provide leaders with information that helps prevent potentially dangerous situations or exposing Soldiers to training that is more aggressive than they can conduct safely.

B-7. Mountain Project is the most popular free online climbing guidebook. The website has detailed information about rock-climbing routes all over the world. The mobile, app-based program allows mountaineers to view information on the go. It is a comprehensive collection of information, but it is user submitted. Using a current guidebook for a specific climbing area can still be the most reliable resource.

B-8. Summit Post is an online-based mountaineering resource for information about hiking or climbing larger nontechnical and technical peaks. The nonprofit website has user-submitted trip reports and updated trail and route conditions. It is a great resource for planning expedition-style climbs for high-altitude mountaineering objectives. The website features a vast selection of mountains from around the world.

## Section II – Other Resources

B-9. Guidebooks, local climbing organizations, governmental and private land management organizations, and national alpine associations are all great additional resources for route planning and information gathering.

B-10. There are many other additional resources available; it would be difficult to name them all. It is best to research the specific area in which Soldiers intend to travel and gather as much relevant and current information as possible. It is also important to use any resources available to research seasonal weather patterns, prevailing winds, and areas of potential flooding.

## Guidebooks

B-11. A guidebook is a publication containing detailed climbing routes and approach route information. Current local guidebooks are still the most comprehensive and useful tool for gathering information on a specific climbing route or mountain. They contain detailed instructions for getting to these remote areas including the approach to the climb or trailhead. They contain invaluable route information and are readily available for most established mountaineering training areas.

## Local Climbing Organizations

B-12. When information is not readily available in guidebooks (such as when training overseas), it may be beneficial to contact a local climbing organization in the area. These organizations exist all over the world even in the most remote and unlikely locations. The local community can provide some of the most accurate and current information that may not be available anywhere else.

## U.s. Government Land Management Agencies

B-13. The U.S. government manages millions of acres of public lands that are excellent training venues for military mountaineering training. Generally, most training areas are managed by the Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, or U.S. Forest Service. Other outstanding areas fall under the management of a state government, such as the Adirondack Park in New York. Training on public lands can be a complicated process that varies from organization to organization. Units planning to train on public lands must contact the land management organization early in the planning process to determine requirements and restrictions. Often, some agencies require up to 9 months of lead time.

## Nongovernmental Land Management Organizations

B-14. Some outstanding training areas such as the Shawangunk Mountain range in New York and New Jersey are under the management of private, nongovernmental land management, or conservation organizations. As with federal and state land management agencies, training planners should research regulations and restrictions on group and military use of the training area and contact the managing organization early in the planning process to ensure success in the training event.

## Section III – Time-Distance Estimation

B-15. The time-distance method covered in chapter 7, table 7-2, page 148 is useful but very general. The Munter method described below affords planners more options to enhance their time-to-distance estimate.

## The Munter Method

B-16. The Munter method is an accurate method of calculating route times during planning. It is the most accepted time-distance formula used by mountain professionals and guides. The Munter method combines map distance (kilometers) and elevation change (meters) into a unit of time (hours), then it is divided by an estimated movement rate (kilometers per hour) using various terrain conditions. This method allows the user the ability to break the movement up into different legs and account for different modes of travel. This method is simple to calculate and allows the planner increased flexibility to account for the various paces, changes in elevation, increased loads, and environmental conditions that exist in mountainous terrain.

B-17. When planning, create a route table like the one in the examples below. (See figure B-1 and figure B-3, page 392.) The Munter method form below is meant to be a baseline tool for route planners. It begins with basic data about the weather, situation, hazards, load, and group assessment to set the framework for decision making. These pieces of data paint a picture of the situation and enable the planner to properly account for terrain difficulties, rest breaks, and other additional time requirements. A sample calculation is provided as well as a baseline table to begin determining route legs. As planners become more experienced in the terrain, the table can be customized to their unit abilities.

B-19. For this example, consider the terrain open and unrestrictive along the route. For the following example, assume open unrestricted terrain and a movement goal centered on physical training.

*[Figure B-2. Munter calculation formula and modifier guide — illustration not reproduced.]*

B-20. For each route leg, add one unit for every kilometer of distance. A half kilometer would be 0.5 units. Then find the change in elevation between points and add 1 unit for every 100 meters change (gain or loss) in elevation during the leg. Add the number of units together for the specific leg.

B-21. Next, multiply this number by 60 (minutes).

B-22. Identify if it is an ascent or decent of the terrain. Then select the appropriate modifier to describe the type of movement through the terrain.

B-23. If moving at a patrolling pace, use the bushwacking modifier. Walking should be considered movement on a trail or in open unrestrictive terrain.

B-24. Scree or boulder fields should be considered bushwhacking. Movement in deep snow without flotation from skis or snowshoes should be considered bushwhacking.

B-25. With the modifier identified, the formula can now be calculated. The result is time in minutes to move that leg.

B-26. Complete the route table to estimate the total movement time. Keep in mind this method does not account for any halts in movement, recon, or other operational tasks associated with a patrol. (See figure B-3, page 392.)

*[Figure B-3. Munter route table, example (completed) — illustration not reproduced.]*

B-27. Calculate travel time from point X to point Y:

- Determine units—there is a 500-meters elevation gain (5 units) and a travel distance of 3.5 (3.5 units). Therefore, the total units for this leg are 8.5. Record in appropriate box in chart.
- Determine raw travel time—8.5 units are then multiplied by 60 minutes, yielding 510 minutes.
- Determine adjusted travel time—this movement involves significant ascent, and the method of travel is walking. Therefore, a modifier of 4 is used.

**Note**. This example assumes the movement is in unrestrictive terrain.

- The raw travel time (510 minutes) is then divided by the modifier (4) to yield an actual projected leg time of 127.5 minutes (or 2 hours and 8 minutes). Record in appropriate box in chart.
- Determine approximate point arrival time—add the actual projected leg time (2 hours and 8 minutes) to the start time for the leg. Record in appropriate box in chart.

B-28. Calculate travel time from point Y to point Z:

- Determine units—there are 600 meters of elevation loss (6 units) and a travel distance of 4 kilometers (4 units). Therefore, the total units for this leg are 10. Record in appropriate box in chart.
- Determine raw travel time—10 units are then multiplied by 60 minutes, yielding 600 minutes.
- Determine adjusted travel time—this movement involves a significant descent; the method of travel is walking. Therefore, a modifier of 6 is used.

**Note**. This example assumes the movement is in unrestrictive terrain.

- The raw travel time (600 minutes) is then divided by the modifier (6) to yield an actual projected leg time of 100 minutes (or 1 hour and 40 minutes). Record in appropriate box in chart.
- Determine approximate point arrival time—add the actual projected leg time (1 hour and 40 minutes) to the start time for the leg. Record in appropriate box in chart.

## Northern Warfare Training Center Method

B-29. This technique was developed specifically for military mountaineers:

- 4 kilometers per hour flat terrain. 2 to 3 for elevation.
- 1 hour per 300 meters (1,000 feet) elevation gain.
- 1 hour per 400 to 600 meters (1,600 feet) elevation loss.
- + 10 minutes per kilometer for every 20 pounds over 50 pounds.
